# Comparative Resistome Ecology

Comparative resistome ecology asks how resistance repertoires differ across environments, lineages, species, and genomic compartments, and how those differences arise through inheritance, selection, stress responses, and horizontal gene transfer. The corpus speaks to this question by combining pangenome surveys, within-species comparisons, environmental metadata, metal-fitness experiments, culture-collection validation, and analyses of mobile or integrative genomic structure. Together, these studies support a layered view: resistance is environmentally patterned, but the apparent ecological signal is inseparable from phylogeny, genome architecture, catalog definition, sampling, and measurement context. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_pangenome_atlas, amr_strain_variation, metal_cross_resistance, t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

## Literature Context

Published environmental-resistome studies establish that resistance is structured by habitat, particle association, contamination gradients, and disturbance rather than being distributed uniformly across microbial communities. Guo et al. showed that free-living and particle-attached bacteria can carry distinguishable resistome profiles during a reservoir cyanobacterial bloom, highlighting physical habitat as an ecological dimension of resistance [PMID 29734061](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29734061/). Along the Yangtze River, Jiang et al. reported that the rare resistome, rather than the core resistome, exhibited greater diversity and risk, while Wang et al. traced resistome responses along antibiotic gradients from pharmaceutical wastewater systems into receiving rivers [PMID 38039820](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38039820/); [PMID 39298897](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39298897/). Other studies identify soils, plastispheres, thawing permafrost, saline environments, and contaminated sites as contexts in which disturbance or environmental chemistry can reshape resistance patterns [PMID 34455424](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34455424/); [PMID 33418967](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33418967/); [PMID 38754232](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38754232/); [PMID 39255667](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39255667/). This literature therefore supports an ecological interpretation, but also suggests that the signal depends strongly on the sampled compartment, stressor, and measurement scale.

The present corpus is consistent with that foundation while making its genomic structure more explicit. Its finding that AMR is generally enriched in auxiliary rather than core genomes, and that many AMR genes are rare or strain-variable, extends the rare-versus-core distinction beyond riverine diversity and risk to a broad pangenomic and within-species framework. The observed clinical, soil, aquatic, and plant contrasts likewise reinforce environmental patterning, but the corpus more directly demonstrates why such comparisons must be conditioned on phylogeny and genome architecture. Its evidence for resistance islands, lineage-associated co-inheritance, and strong phylogenetic signal extends habitat-level observations toward mechanisms that can preserve ecological differences within species. Reports of resistance-bearing environmental *Klebsiella quasipneumoniae* strains provide a complementary culture-based reminder that particular lineages can combine environmental origin with clinically relevant resistance genes [PMID 36711372](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36711372/).

The corpus also extends the literature by integrating metal resistance, metal-fitness experiments, environmental MAGs, and mobility-associated neighborhoods. The strong but chemistry-dependent cross-metal correlations and the separation of metal-specific from general-stress responses suggest a broader stress architecture than antibiotic-resistance surveys alone establish. Conversely, the corpus’s weak or null results in some environmental and phenotype comparisons are in tension with the expectation that every contamination or stress gradient should yield a transferable resistome signal. That tension is compatible with literature showing nonlinear resistome responses under salinity and mitigation effects in biofilm-associated soils [PMID 38754232](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38754232/); [PMID 39255667](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39255667/). Finally, methodological work calling for targeted hybrid capture and universal quantitative units underscores the corpus’s warning that catalog scope, detection sensitivity, normalization, and sampling design can determine whether ecological structure is visible [PMID 42294626](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42294626/); [PMID 37310875](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37310875/).

## What the Corpus Shows

**Resistance is usually more accessory than the surrounding genome, but the pattern depends on resistance type.** Across 27,690 pangenome species, 83,008 AMRFinderPlus hits represented 82,908 gene clusters, 1,939 AMR gene families, and 2,079 AMR products. Only 30.3% of AMR genes were core, compared with 46.8% for the pangenome baseline; AMR was therefore 2.2× enriched in the auxiliary genome. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] A paired analysis of 4,252 species found that 63.7% had AMR less core than their species baseline, with mean difference -0.102 and Wilcoxon p=1.1e-130. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] This broad accessory tendency conceals a strong intrinsic–acquired contrast: intrinsic efflux pumps such as *emhABC* were >95% core, whereas acquired examples including *blaTEM*, *tet(C)*, and *ant(2'')-Ia* were each 0% core. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The distinction is therefore genomic and probabilistic, not a rule that assigns every resistance mechanism to one compartment; beta-lactamases, for example, were 54.9% core, while regulatory genes were only 6.5% core. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] See [[concepts/intrinsic-versus-acquired-resistance]].

This compartmentalization also appears within species. Among 37,444 AMR gene–species records, 51.3% of genes were rare, occurring in <=5% of strains, 41.3% were variable, and 7.5% were fixed; the median pairwise AMR Jaccard distance was 0.435. [src: amr_strain_variation] Thus, species labels do not imply uniform resistomes. The corpus identified 1,517 resistance islands across 705 species, with mean size 6.2 genes, median size 4 genes, maximum size 43 genes, and mean phi coefficient 0.827; 1,343 islands (88%) contained multiple resistance mechanisms. [src: amr_strain_variation] These islands are best interpreted as co-inherited modules or lineage-associated associations until physical linkage and selection are tested. [src: amr_strain_variation] See [[concepts/within-species-resistome-heterogeneity]] and [[concepts/resistance-island-coinheritance]].

**Phylogeny is not background noise; it organizes both burden and composition.** In 1,261 species with average nucleotide identity and AMR-distance data, 701 (55.6%) showed significant phylogenetic signal at FDR < 0.05, and 87.8% had positive correlations between nucleotide distance and AMR Jaccard distance. [src: amr_strain_variation] Non-core genes had a median Mantel correlation of 0.222, compared with 0.117 for core genes, consistent with acquired elements becoming stably maintained within lineages. [src: amr_strain_variation] However, core genes have little repertoire variance by definition, so the weaker core signal is partly statistical rather than a direct estimate of lower heritability. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The same issue changes the interpretation of cross-species environmental comparisons. Pangenome openness correlated positively with AMR count in 8/10 tested phyla, yet the pooled correlation was near zero (rho=0.006); the strongest within-phylum associations were Bacillota (rho=0.219) and Bacillota_C (rho=0.374). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Clinical species carried 10.6 AMR clusters per species, compared with 4.6 for Soil/Terrestrial, 3.9 for Aquatic, and 3.0 for Animal species, while clinical AMR was less core at 30.8% than soil AMR at 58.1% or plant AMR at 63.1%. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] These contrasts are compatible with environmental effects, but also with uneven taxonomic composition and the over-representation of clinical pathogens. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The methodological implications are developed in [[concepts/within-species-resistome-heterogeneity]] and [[concepts/phylogenetic-confounding-of-pangenome-associations]].

**Metal resistance reveals a shared stress architecture with chemistry-specific variation.** Across 452 metal experiments involving 37 organisms and 14 metals, 311 of 317 organism–metal-pair gene-level correlations (98.1%) were positive, and 99.1% were statistically significant at p < 0.05. [src: metal_cross_resistance] All 15 pairs tested in at least 5 organisms had greater than 90% sign consistency, with no systematically negative pair. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The strongest associations included Fe–Zn (mean r = 0.61, n = 6 organisms), Co–Ni (r = 0.56, n = 28), and Co–Zn (r = 0.52, n = 18), whereas Al was more independent, with Al–Co at r = 0.30 and Al–Ni at r = 0.34. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

This common direction does not mean all metal responses are specialized. Of 7,609 metal-important gene records, 4,177 (54.9%) were metal-specific, 2,888 (38.0%) were general sick across non-metal conditions, and 544 (7.2%) were metal+stress under a 5% non-metal sick-rate threshold. [src: metal_specificity] The result is a two-layer architecture: metal-specific determinants coexist with genes responding to broad cellular stress. The metal-fitness atlas further found a mean core-fraction delta of +0.148 for essential metals versus +0.081 for toxic metals. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] See [[concepts/metal-cross-resistance]].

**Environmental structure is strongest when analysis targets particular functions, habitats, or genomic neighborhoods.** Among 30,497 high-quality environmental MAGs, 6,652 (21.8%) carried T4SS or conjugative machinery, and T4SS-positive genomes had 10× higher MGE density than other genomes. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] Ninety-two CAZy families showed elevated co-occurrence within <=10 kb of T4SS loci; associations were enriched in marine sediment (OR=5.5, q<10⁻⁹⁸), barley rhizosphere (OR=10.4), and maize rhizosphere (OR=4.1). [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] GT2-neighborhood MAGs carried a mean of 0.045 metal-resistance types versus 0.004 in non-GT2 MAGs, and genomes with GT2 in T4SS-proximal neighborhoods carried 11× more metal-resistance genes. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] These observations connect environmental function, mobility-associated structure, and resistance breadth, while leaving the transfer mechanism unresolved.

At broader geographic scale, 22,356 of 30,497 environmental MAGs had usable coordinates, and 2.8% carried at least one metal-resistance type. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography] Soil prevalence was 5.8% (OR=5.05, q=1.8e-82), marine prevalence was 1.2% (OR=0.20, q=9.2e-80), and rhizosphere prevalence was 1.9% (OR=0.66, q=0.30, NS). [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography] The top hotspot, in the Atacama/Andean region, had 21.8% prevalence, OR=9.83, q=7.6e-12, and n=101 MAGs. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography] These patterns make environmental filtering plausible, but sampling effort and expedition effects remain important alternatives.

**Ecological validation is positive but selective and measurement-dependent.** A BacDive–GTDB bridge matched 42,227 of 97,334 strains (43.4%) to 6,426 GTDB species; only 25,089 matched strains had isolation-source metadata. [src: bacdive_metal_validation] Heavy-metal contamination isolates had median metal score 0.240 versus 0.187 for the environmental baseline, with Cohen’s d=+1.00, Mann–Whitney p=0.006, and n=10. [src: bacdive_metal_validation] The broader gradient was heavy metal (+1.00) > waste/sludge (+0.57) > all contamination (+0.43) > industrial (+0.20). [src: bacdive_metal_validation] Within Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota, contamination-associated deltas were +0.040 and +0.035, respectively, both with p<0.001. [src: bacdive_metal_validation]

Yet only 24 matched strains had metal-utilization records, with 8 positive and 16 negative results; the phenotype comparison was inconclusive (p=0.14, Cohen’s d=-0.57). [src: bacdive_phenotype_metal_tolerance] The score itself divides metal clusters by annotated clusters, so genome size, annotation breadth, and metabolic complexity may remain confounded. [src: bacdive_metal_validation] See [[concepts/cultivation-collection-bias-in-ecological-genomics]], [[concepts/genome-size-confounding-of-functional-scores]], and [[concepts/within-species-conservation-between-species-functional-divergence]].

## Tensions and Caveats

The central tension is between detectable ecological structure and weak generalization. Broad analyses can produce null or near-null associations, while targeted analyses identify strong signals in metals, prophage-linked content, soil functions, or T4SS neighborhoods. The conflict [[conflicts/conflict--ecotype_env_reanalysis--env_embedding_explorer--harvard_forest_warming]] frames this as a broad environmental null versus subsystem-specific effects. The conflict [[conflicts/conflict--env_embedding_explorer--euk_in_prok_correlates--gene_function_ecological_agora]] further emphasizes that geographic or environmental associations can weaken after controlling for genome size, lineage, batch, or out-of-sample performance.

Metal evidence is similarly non-uniform. Strong cross-metal correlations coexist with 38.0% general-sick records, a species-scale BacDive correlation near -0.02 with p > 0.8, and a null groundwater-specific test (rho=+0.042, p=0.242). [src: metal_specificity, metal_cross_resistance, microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The conflict [[conflicts/conflict--metal_cross_resistance--metal_fitness_atlas--metal_specificity]] cautions that fitness correlation, environmental prevalence, isolation metadata, and gene-neighborhood co-occurrence are different outcomes and should not be treated as interchangeable validation.

Finally, genomic proximity and co-occurrence do not establish mechanism. T4SS–CAZy proximity, elevated MGE density, phylogenetic incongruence, and IME occurrence support a chromosomal or integrative transfer model but do not prove that T4SS machinery caused transfer; threshold validation and closed-genome or long-read evidence remain pending. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] Likewise, resistance islands may reflect physical linkage, clonal expansion, co-selection, or shared lineage history rather than functional synergy. [src: amr_strain_variation] Catalog scope is another load-bearing caveat: the AMRFinderPlus catalog includes mercury-, arsenic-, and stress-response genes, so its operational AMR counts are not antibiotic resistance alone. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The conflict [[conflicts/conflict--berdl_data_atlas--cofitness_coinheritance--ecotype_env_reanalysis]] provides the broader warning: a successful computational bridge is not automatically biological validation. In this corpus, 56.6% of BacDive strains remained unmatched, and culture collections under-represent uncultured environmental diversity. [src: bacdive_metal_validation]
