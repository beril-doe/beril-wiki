# Bacterial Resistome Ecology

Bacterial resistome ecology asks how antimicrobial-resistance (AMR) genes are distributed across environments, lineages, genomic compartments, and ecological contexts, and how those distributions arise from selection, inheritance, mobility, and sampling. This corpus speaks to the topic through a large pangenome atlas, within-species variation, fitness experiments, environmental comparisons, prophage analyses, and metal-resistance studies. Together, the projects support strong ecological and genomic structure in the resistome, but they also show that abundance, conservation, co-occurrence, and environmental association are not interchangeable evidence for adaptation or horizontal transfer.

## Literature Context

Published work establishes that antimicrobial-resistance genes are not confined to clinical settings. Crits-Christoph et al. frame the human microbiome as an ecological reservoir in which otherwise beneficial microbial communities can carry and disseminate “bad” genes, emphasizing that resistance must be interpreted in relation to host-associated microbial ecology rather than pathogens alone. [PMID 35332832](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35332832/) Van et al. further show that remote, pristine Antarctic soils contain historical resistance genes, demonstrating that environmental resistomes can predate recent clinical antibiotic use and need not be explained solely by contemporary medical selection. [PMID 29471872](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29471872/) More recent work on non-human primates identifies human activities and horizontal gene transfer as shaping resistome landscapes, while research on deep-sea cold-seep microorganisms highlights the need to assess resistance together with pathogenicity risk in poorly characterized environments. [PMID 41616624](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41616624/) [PMID 40396743](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40396743/)

This corpus is consistent with that literature in showing that resistance is environmentally structured, extends beyond clinical isolates, and is embedded in broader microbial and genomic ecology. The atlas-level finding that clinical species carry more catalog-defined AMR clusters than soil, aquatic, or host-associated species aligns with an influence of human-associated environments, but the corpus strengthens the literature’s ecological framing by quantifying how strongly the result depends on lineage, sampling, and annotation scope. Its finding that only 30.3% of AMR genes are core, compared with 46.8% of the general pangenome, and that 63.7% of sufficiently sampled species have AMR less core than their species baseline, extends reservoir-based accounts by separating conserved intrinsic resistance from variable, potentially mobile or ecologically restricted components. The within-species result that 51.3% of AMR genes are rare and 41.3% variable likewise adds resolution that broad environmental surveys cannot provide.

The corpus also extends—and in some respects qualifies—the literature’s emphasis on horizontal transfer. Resistance islands show strong co-inheritance and frequent mixing of mechanisms, consistent with linked ecological or genomic reservoirs, while prophage-associated AMR is common at the contig scale but close gene proximity is much less frequent. Thus, the corpus does not treat co-occurrence, contig association, or environmental enrichment as equivalent to demonstrated transfer. This is a novel methodological contribution relative to the candidate literature: it makes scale-dependent mobility claims explicit and tests them against proximity thresholds and species-level covariance. The corpus is also in tension with any simple interpretation that environmental differences directly measure local selection. Its near-zero pooled association between pangenome openness and AMR, persistence of environmental effects in only 20 of 141 testable families, and mostly null whole-genome ecotype results indicate that lineage structure and sampling can mimic or obscure adaptation. Finally, its fitness experiments add a condition-dependent mechanism largely absent from these environmental surveys: AMR knockouts were relatively less burdensome without antibiotics, whereas many genes became more important under antibiotic exposure. Together, these results place the corpus as a quantitative bridge between environmental-reservoir literature and experimentally tested, lineage-aware resistome ecology.

## What the Corpus Shows

**AMR is environmentally structured, but the signal is catalog- and lineage-dependent.** The largest atlas analyzed 82,908 AMR gene clusters across 14,723 bacterial species and 293K genomes; 53.2% of species carried at least one catalog-defined hit. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_pangenome_atlas] However, AMRFinderPlus includes stress-response genes alongside classical antibiotic-resistance genes, so these inventories describe a broader resistance landscape rather than a strictly clinical antibiotic-resistance count. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Mercury- and arsenic-resistance families contributed approximately 15,000 and 6,000 annotations respectively, while bla beta-lactamases were the most abundant named family with 6,115 hits. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

Environment-associated differences are substantial at the species level. Clinical species carried 10.6 AMR clusters per species, compared with 4.6 in soil, 3.9 in aquatic samples, and 3.0 in animal-associated samples. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] A separate analysis found median AMR counts of 5 in clinical species versus 2 in soil, aquatic, and host-associated species, with accessory AMR comprising 68% in clinical species versus 43% in soil species. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] These patterns support ecological structuring, but clinical datasets are also more heavily sampled and taxonomically non-equivalent; the comparisons do not establish that clinical exposure caused the observed differences. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_pangenome_atlas]

**The resistome is disproportionately accessory, with a conserved intrinsic component.** The pangenome atlas found that only 30.3% of AMR genes were core, compared with 46.8% for the general pangenome, while the auxiliary genome was 2.2x enriched for AMR: 33.6% versus 15.3%. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] In 4,252 species containing at least 5 AMR clusters, 63.7% had AMR less core than their species baseline. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] “Core” here means present in nearly all sampled genomes of a lineage; “accessory” means variably present or restricted to a subset. This operational distinction is useful for ecology, but it does not by itself identify plasmids, resistance islands, or recent acquisition.

Resistance biology varies sharply within this framework. Beta-lactamases were 54.9% core, intrinsic emhABC efflux pumps were >95% core, regulatory genes were 6.5% core, and blaTEM, tet(C), and ant(2'')-Ia were each 0% core. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Within species, 51.3% of AMR genes were rare, 41.3% variable, and 7.5% fixed; the median variability index was 0.526 and median pairwise Jaccard distance—a distance based on shared gene repertoire—was 0.435. [src: amr_strain_variation] Thus, “the resistome of a species” often conceals major strain-level ecological and evolutionary heterogeneity. The detailed [[concepts/environmental-resistome]] and [[concepts/resistance-island-coinheritance]] pages develop this conservation–acquisition distinction.

**Resistance can be costly in one condition and beneficial in another.** RB-TnSeq, or random-barcode transposon sequencing, measures competitive fitness of insertion mutants relative to a pool rather than absolute growth relative to an unperturbed wild type. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Under non-antibiotic conditions, AMR-gene knockouts had a pooled relative shift of **+0.086 [95% CI: +0.074, +0.098], z = 14.3, p ~ 0**, with median per-organism Cohen’s d **0.18** and heterogeneity **I² = 54.3%**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The interpretation is relative: AMR knockouts averaged **−0.024** fitness, while the comparison knockout background averaged approximately **−0.11**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Therefore, the result supports a burden associated with AMR genes in the tested backgrounds, but not positive absolute growth of AMR knockouts.

Antibiotic exposure changed the picture. Across any-antibiotic experiments, **57.0%** of AMR genes showed a flip toward greater importance, based on **N = 797**, mean flip **+0.045**, and Wilcoxon **p = 0.0001**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Broad-spectrum efflux genes had mean flip **+0.094**, compared with **−0.001** for enzymatic-inactivation genes, with Mann–Whitney **p = 0.007**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This supports condition-specific fitness: the same gene can be relatively burdensome without antibiotics yet valuable during exposure.

The evidence is weaker when antibiotic classes are matched explicitly. A validation of **157 gene-antibiotic pairs** had mean flip **+0.113**, flip rate **54.8%**, and Wilcoxon **p = 0.14**; chloramphenicol resistance showed **6/6 (100%)** expected flips, while beta-lactam resistance showed a **50%** flip rate across **105 pairs**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The resulting [[conflicts/conflict--amr_cofitness_networks--amr_fitness_cost--amr_strain_variation]] is central: broad condition coverage supports a general signal, but class-specific validation does not establish uniform behavior across mechanisms.

**Resistance genes form linked ecological and genomic modules, but linkage is not transfer.** The within-species analysis detected **1,517 resistance islands across 705 species**, with mean size **6.2 genes**, median size **4**, maximum size **43**, and mean phi coefficient **0.827**. [src: amr_strain_variation] The phi coefficient measures pairwise co-occurrence, so these values indicate tight co-inheritance rather than demonstrated physical location or co-selection. [src: amr_strain_variation] **1,343 (88%)** islands contained genes from multiple resistance mechanisms; efflux and enzymatic inactivation occurred in **954** and **698** islands respectively. [src: amr_strain_variation]

Prophage associations illustrate why scale matters. Among **36,041** AMR instances from **1,953 genomes** across **100 species**, **20,073 (55.7%)** occurred on contigs carrying strict prophage markers, but only **3,731 (10.4%)** were within 10 genes and **1,991 (5.5%)** within 5 genes; the median nearest-marker distance was **34 genes**. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] At the 10-gene threshold, nearby AMR was 67.6% accessory versus 65.5% for distal AMR, with odds ratio **1.10** and a 2.1 percentage point difference. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] The odds ratio changed from **0.78** at 3 genes to **1.28** at 50 genes, and the median species-level odds ratio was **0.85**. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] These results support broad covariance between prophage markers and AMR breadth—species-level rho **= 0.572**, partial rho **= 0.464** after controlling for genome count—but not uniform phage-mediated AMR mobilization. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

**Phylogeny and sampling are load-bearing explanations.** Pangenome openness—the fraction and turnover of accessory genes—correlated positively with AMR count in **8/10** tested phyla, yet the pooled correlation was nearly zero, **rho = 0.006**. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Within-phylum associations were stronger in Bacillota, **rho = 0.219**, and Bacillota_C, **rho = 0.374**. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] This is a classic phylogenetic-confounding pattern: lineage-specific baselines can cancel one another when species are pooled. The matched openness analysis found no general relationship between openness and environment effect, **rho = -0.05, p = 0.54**, or phylogeny effect, **rho = 0.03, p = 0.73**. [src: pangenome_openness]

Within species, 55.6% of **1,261** tested species showed significant AMR-repertoire correlation with ANI distance at FDR < 0.05, and 87.8% had positive correlations; the median Mantel r was **0.247**. [src: amr_strain_variation] Non-core AMR had median Mantel r **0.222** versus **0.117** for core AMR, although the difference is partly influenced by the low variance of near-universal core genes. [src: amr_strain_variation] Environmental effects persisted within **5 of 6** major phyla and **20 of 141** testable families, but most family-level comparisons were unresolved. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

## Tensions and Caveats

The corpus supports ecological structure without resolving ecological causation. The promoted conflict [[conflicts/conflict--amr_environmental_resistome--amr_fitness_cost--amr_pangenome_atlas]] captures the central limitation: environment, phylogeny, sampling, annotation, host association, and clinical enrichment are entangled. Only **20 of 141** testable families showed significant within-family environmental effects after FDR correction, while whole-genome ecotype analyses found positive environmental effects in only **12 species (7.0%)**, negative effects in **4 species (2.3%)**, and no effect in **156 species (90.7%)**. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, ecotype_analysis] Broad species-level contrasts should therefore not be read as universal within-species adaptation.

The conflict [[conflicts/conflict--amr_environmental_resistome--amr_pangenome_atlas--amr_strain_variation]] places pooled and lineage-specific results in direct tension. A near-zero global openness–AMR correlation can coexist with positive phylum-level relationships, but neither result is causal. The same caution applies to geographic metal-resistance hotspots: among **22,356** MAGs with coordinates, **2.8%** carried at least one metal-resistance type, with **11 hotspots** and **3 coldspots**, but the report requires sampling-effort and study-origin checks before regional interpretation. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]

Metal-resistance evidence is also not interchangeable with antibiotic-resistance evidence. BacDive reported Cohen’s d **+1.00** for heavy-metal contamination, whereas a species-scale cross-resistance analysis found rho approximately **−0.02, p > 0.8** after matching and collapsing strains to **20 independent species**. [src: bacdive_metal_validation, metal_cross_resistance] The [[conflicts/conflict--bacdive_metal_validation--metal_cross_resistance--metal_fitness_atlas]] page explains that these analyses use different populations, aggregation levels, and phenotypes. Finally, marker-based system claims require caution: prophage calls may include domesticated remnants or bacterial homologs, and local proximity does not establish intact transfer machinery. [src: prophage_ecology, prophage_amr_comobilization]
