# Antimicrobial Resistance Fitness

Antimicrobial-resistance (AMR) fitness concerns how resistance genes alter organismal performance across conditions, especially the trade-off between constitutive burden without antibiotics and protection during exposure. The corpus speaks to this question through genome-wide transposon perturbations, antibiotic-condition comparisons, pangenome classifications, and broader analyses of condition-specific fitness. Its central message is that AMR fitness is neither a fixed gene property nor a simple cost-benefit balance: resistance can impose a measurable relative burden, become beneficial under particular antibiotics, and remain difficult to interpret when laboratory adaptation, gene annotation, and ecological context are considered. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Literature Context

Published work generally treats antimicrobial resistance (AMR) as an evolutionary trade-off rather than a fixed phenotype. Resistance mechanisms can reduce growth or competitive performance in antibiotic-free environments, while improving survival during exposure; the balance depends on the mechanism, genetic background, ecological setting, and antibiotic concentration. These principles are established in broad reviews of resistance evolution and its molecular drivers. [PMID 24847651](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24847651/) [PMID 26603922](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26603922/) Environmental conditions, community interactions, and antibiotic use further influence both the emergence and persistence of resistance. [PMID 37312496](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37312496/) [PMID 39306135](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39306135/) Recent work also emphasizes that resistance costs can be modified by compensatory evolution, plasmid–chromosome interactions, and reversibility after antibiotic withdrawal, rather than being determined solely by the resistance gene itself. [PMID 38870982](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38870982/) [PMID 39738078](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39738078/)

The corpus is consistent with this literature in showing that AMR effects are condition-dependent. Its RB-TnSeq analysis identifies a reproducible relative burden under non-antibiotic conditions, while many genes become more important during antibiotic exposure; the stronger response of broad-spectrum efflux genes than enzymatic-inactivation genes fits the expectation that protection depends on the match between mechanism and environment. This result also extends recent experimental and genomic work arguing that resistance fitness effects are difficult to predict across environments, including the finding that effects of resistance mutations in *Escherichia coli* are often unpredictable when conditions change. [PMID 38709811](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38709811/) [PMID 37312496](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37312496/) By measuring hundreds of genes across many organisms and antibiotic conditions, the corpus moves beyond single-gene or single-pathogen examples and supplies a quantitative cross-organism test of this principle. It also supports genomic perspectives in which resistance, virulence, and bacterial ecology interact rather than forming independent traits. [PMID 39242067](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39242067/)

Several corpus findings are more specific, and potentially novel, than the candidate literature. The absence of a detectable baseline-cost difference between core and accessory AMR genes, and the lack of a significant baseline ordering among resistance mechanisms, indicate that pangenome prevalence and measured knockout burden are decoupled. This refines the usual cost-based expectation that recently acquired or mobile resistance should be more costly, while remaining compatible with compensation and plasmid–chromosome adaptation described in the literature. [PMID 38870982](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38870982/) [PMID 39738078](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39738078/) The corpus’s relative-fitness interpretation is also important: its positive AMR-versus-background shift is not an absolute growth advantage, so it should not be directly equated with genomic estimates of acquisition costs. [PMID 37312496](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37312496/) Thus, the main tension is not a direct contradiction but a difference in estimand: published studies often address resistance acquisition, persistence, or reversion, whereas this corpus primarily measures disruption effects in laboratory-adapted strains.

## What the Corpus Shows

**Resistance usually carries a baseline burden, but the measured effect is relative.** Random-barcode transposon sequencing (RB-TnSeq), a pooled perturbation method that estimates each mutant’s performance relative to the pool average, identified 1,352 AMR genes across 43 organisms and provided per-organism effects for 25 organisms. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Under non-antibiotic conditions, AMR-gene knockouts had systematically higher fitness than non-AMR knockouts, consistent with AMR genes imposing a relative burden in the measured strains. Across all 25 organisms, the random-effects meta-analysis estimated an AMR-versus-background shift of **+0.086 [95% CI: +0.074, +0.098], z = 14.3, p ~ 0**, with every organism showing a positive shift. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The median per-organism Cohen’s d was **0.18**, while heterogeneity was **I² = 54.3%** and Cochran’s **Q = 52.54, p = 0.0007**, indicating that the direction was reproducible but its magnitude varied among organisms. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

This should not be read as evidence that AMR knockouts grow better than an unperturbed wild type. AMR-knockout fitness averaged **−0.024**, compared with approximately **−0.11** for the non-AMR knockout background, and the one-sample Wilcoxon test against zero gave **p = 0.999**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The result therefore supports a relative-cost interpretation: removing AMR genes produced less impairment than removing the genes represented in the comparison background, rather than producing positive absolute growth. The distinction is developed in [[concepts/relative-fitness-and-transposon-interpretation]].

**Antibiotic exposure reverses the direction for many genes.** Baseline cost and antibiotic-dependent importance are partially decoupled. Across any-antibiotic experiments, **57.0%** of AMR genes showed a fitness flip toward greater importance under antibiotic exposure, based on **N = 797**, a mean flip of **+0.045**, and Wilcoxon signed-rank **p = 0.0001**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Here, the flip is defined as non-antibiotic fitness minus antibiotic fitness, so a positive value means that the gene became more important in the antibiotic condition. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The conditional benefit depended more clearly on resistance mechanism than did the baseline burden. Broad-spectrum efflux genes had a mean flip of **+0.094**, whereas enzymatic-inactivation genes had a mean flip of **−0.001**, with Mann–Whitney U **p = 0.007**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This pattern is compatible with efflux systems protecting against several antibiotic environments, while enzymatic inactivation is more dependent on exposure to the antibiotic that the enzyme can neutralize. [src: amr_fitness_cost] A smaller class-matched validation of **157 gene-antibiotic pairs** showed a mean flip of **+0.113**, a flip rate of **54.8%**, and Wilcoxon **p = 0.14**. Chloramphenicol resistance showed **6/6 (100%)** expected flips, whereas beta-lactam resistance showed a **50%** flip rate across **105 pairs across 10 organisms**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The broader analysis is therefore stronger than the matched validation, and the evidence favors condition-specific rather than universal protection. See [[concepts/condition-specific-fitness]].

**Mechanism predicts distribution more reliably than baseline cost.** The data do not support a simple ordering in which one resistance mechanism is intrinsically more costly than another. Baseline effects did not differ significantly among efflux (**N=254**), enzymatic inactivation (**N=304**), metal resistance (**N=144**), and unknown mechanisms (**N=74**): Kruskal–Wallis **H = 0.65, p = 0.89**, and the predicted ordering efflux > enzymatic > metal > unknown gave Jonckheere–Terpstra **z = 0.23, p = 0.41**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] No pairwise comparison survived false-discovery-rate correction. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

Mechanism was nevertheless strongly associated with pangenome conservation status, meaning how broadly a gene is distributed across sampled genomes. The association had **χ² = 69.3, p = 1.4×10⁻¹³**; metal-resistance genes were accessory in **44%** of cases, compared with **13%** of efflux genes and **16%** of enzymatic-inactivation genes. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Thus, mechanism helps explain where resistance genes occur in the pangenome, but not how costly their disruption is under the baseline conditions measured here. This distinction is central to [[concepts/pangenome-conservation-fitness-decoupling]].

The broader metal-fitness corpus gives a related but not identical picture. Across **559 metal experiments in 31 organisms and 16 metals**, broad metal-important genes were **87.4% core** versus **76.9%** for baseline genes, while a specificity analysis classified **54.9%** of metal-important records as metal-specific, **38.0%** as generally sick, and **7.2%** as metal-plus-stress. [src: metal_fitness_atlas, metal_specificity] These results support separating general cellular vulnerability from resistance-specific protection rather than treating every metal-associated phenotype as AMR fitness.

**Pangenome prevalence does not explain the measured AMR cost.** Core or intrinsic AMR genes (**N=638**) and accessory or acquired AMR genes (**N=163**) had virtually identical mean fitness values of **−0.024**, with Cohen’s **d = 0.002** and Mann–Whitney U **p = 0.33**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Related stratifications also found no conservation difference (**p = 0.33**), no Tier 1-versus-Tier 2 difference (**p = 0.26**), and no antibiotic-versus-metal resistance-type difference (**p = 0.87**). [src: amr_fitness_cost] These null results indicate that prevalence across sampled genomes and knockout fitness are different properties.

One possible explanation is that horizontal transfer preferentially retains cost-optimized resistance genes, or that compensatory evolution reduces costs after acquisition; these are interpretations, not processes directly measured in the dataset. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The compensation hypothesis is discussed in [[concepts/antimicrobial-resistance-fitness-cost]]. It is also consistent with the observation that all **25** tested organisms were laboratory-adapted strains, potentially reducing the apparent cost relative to newly acquired resistance in natural populations. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Tensions and Caveats

The first tension is between essentiality as a marker of conservation and the possibility that essentiality is conditional or lineage-specific. ADP1 and cross-organism analyses associate essentiality or strong fitness effects with higher core representation, while other analyses find essential genes among lineage-restricted or poorly annotated families. [src: acinetobacter_adp1_explorer, conservation_fitness_synthesis, truly_dark_genes] Insertion-based assays can also miss essential genes because disruptions do not survive library construction, so observed AMR fitness distributions may underrepresent genes with the strongest costs. Only **4.6%** of AMR genes were absent from the fitness matrices and therefore putatively essential, compared with an approximately **14%** background essential rate from a different organism set. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This conflict is anchored in [[conflicts/conflict--acinetobacter_adp1_explorer--adp1_triple_essentiality--alphafold_msa_annotation]].

A second tension concerns whether genomic capability or model agreement demonstrates condition-specific activity. Some metabolic analyses report substantial agreement between flux-balance predictions and TnSeq measurements, whereas other comparisons show that pathway presence, production, utilization, and mutant dependence are distinct endpoints. [src: acinetobacter_adp1_explorer, annotation_gap_discovery, metabolic_capability_dependency, fw300_metabolic_consistency] For AMR, the implication is direct: detecting a resistance gene does not establish that it is expressed, protective, or costly in a particular environment. The relevant conflict is [[conflicts/conflict--acinetobacter_adp1_explorer--adp1_triple_essentiality--annotation_gap_discovery]].

A third caveat is that metal resistance may combine true metal-specific protection with general stress sensitivity. The metal atlas found strong core enrichment, but explicit controls classified a substantial fraction of responses as generally sick rather than metal-specific. [src: metal_fitness_atlas, metal_specificity] Environmental validations also disagree: some metal-associated genomic or soil signals are strong, while species-scale BacDive and groundwater tests are weak or null. [src: metal_cross_resistance, metal_specificity, bacdive_phenotype_metal_tolerance] This limits claims that metal-associated AMR fitness effects generalize across environments; see [[conflicts/conflict--metal_cross_resistance--metal_fitness_atlas--metal_specificity]].

Finally, RB-TnSeq disruption is not equivalent to measuring the fitness of acquiring or expressing an intact resistance gene. Insertions can have polar effects on downstream genes, and **86%** of AMR genes were in the keyword-based Tier 2 category, which may include general efflux transporters. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The baseline estimate is therefore best treated as a reproducible relative perturbation signal, not a universal selection coefficient.
