# Bacterial Resistance Ecology

Bacterial resistance ecology examines how antimicrobial-resistance (AMR), metal-resistance, phage defense, and associated mobile functions vary across environments, lineages, genomic compartments, and fitness conditions. This corpus speaks to the topic by combining pangenome surveys, environmental metagenomes, strain-level comparisons, perturbation fitness profiles, and phenotype databases. Its central message is that resistance is ecologically structured, but the strength and meaning of that structure depend on scale: broad environmental and lineage associations are common, whereas direct evidence for exposure-driven selection, metal-specific mechanism, or mobile-element transfer is more limited. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, metal_cross_resistance, phage_defense_arsenal]

## Literature Context

Published work increasingly treats antimicrobial resistance (AMR) as an ecological and evolutionary property rather than solely a clinical outcome. Environmental reviews describe resistance as shaped by antibiotics, metals, biocides, pollution, community structure, and movement among human, animal, food, and environmental reservoirs. [PMID 29069382](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29069382/) [PMID 30798805](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30798805/) [PMID 33219299](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33219299/) Population-genomic studies likewise show that resistance and virulence are distributed unevenly among lineages and are frequently associated with accessory genomes and mobile genetic elements, as illustrated especially well for *Klebsiella pneumoniae*. [PMID 32055025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32055025/) Evolutionary-ecology theory further predicts that resistance costs, benefits, and persistence depend on antibiotic exposure, competing genotypes, resource limitation, and interactions within bacterial communities. [PMID 31083687](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31083687/) Experimental community work supports this conditional view: complex communities can reduce selection for resistance relative to simplified settings. [PMID 31689403](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31689403/)

The corpus is consistent with this literature in showing that resistance is structured across environments, lineages, genomic compartments, and fitness conditions. Its finding that AMR is disproportionately accessory and highly variable within species extends the population-genomic perspective beyond a single pathogen, while the environmental comparisons reinforce the expectation that ecological context contributes to resistome composition without by itself proving exposure-driven selection. The corpus also sharpens the fitness argument: RB-TnSeq results show that the apparent burden of AMR depends on the comparison background and that antibiotic exposure reverses the relative importance of many AMR genes. This directly complements evolutionary accounts of context-dependent resistance costs. The observation that broad-spectrum efflux has a more consistent exposure-associated benefit than enzymatic inactivation further specifies how resistance mechanism can mediate ecological generality, although this corpus-level comparison is an extension of the broader published framework rather than a replacement for direct competition experiments. Historical evidence that methicillin resistance predates clinical antibiotic use also supports interpreting resistance as an ecological and evolutionary reservoir phenomenon, not exclusively a response to modern medicine. [PMID 34987223](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34987223/)

The corpus extends the literature most clearly through its quantitative treatment of metal stress, cross-resistance, defense syndromes, and scale-dependent mobilization. Strongly positive cross-metal fitness correlations are compatible with shared cellular stress responses, but the chemistry-dependent correlation magnitudes and the separation of metal-specific from generally stress-sensitive genes add resolution that broad environmental reviews do not provide. Metal-associated selection is biologically plausible in light of established environmental drivers of resistance, but the corpus appropriately treats these associations as hypotheses requiring metal-specific perturbation or expression tests. [PMID 29069382](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29069382/) Its phage-defense results also fit work showing evolutionary coupling between phage resistance and antibiotic phenotypes, while the weak local evidence for prophage-linked AMR cautions against equating co-occurrence with transfer. [PMID 34220747](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34220747/) In tension with simplified narratives of environmental dissemination, the corpus finds broad environment-level structure but limited and uneven within-family or within-species effects. That scale dependence, together with its distinction between resistance-island coinheritance and demonstrated mobility, is the corpus’s principal contribution to the published ecology of resistance.

## What the Corpus Shows

**Resistance is structured by environment, lineage, and genomic compartment.** The broad AMR atlas analyzed 82,908 AMR gene clusters across 14,723 bacterial species and 293K genomes; 53.2% of species carried at least one catalog-defined AMR hit. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The catalog includes stress-response genes alongside classical antibiotic-resistance genes, so these measurements describe a broader resistance landscape rather than a purely clinical antibiotic-resistance inventory. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] AMR is disproportionately accessory: 30.3% of AMR genes were core compared with 46.8% for the pangenome baseline, while the auxiliary genome was 2.2x enriched for AMR. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Within species, 51.3% of AMR gene records were rare, 41.3% variable, and 7.5% fixed, showing that species labels conceal substantial strain-level heterogeneity. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The ecological comparison is directionally consistent with this compartmentalization. Clinical species had 68% accessory AMR compared with 43% in soil, and environment effects remained significant within 5 of 6 major phyla, although effects were incomplete and uneven across families. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] These patterns support an ecological contribution to resistome composition, but not environmental causation: phylogeny, sampling, isolation practices, annotation, and host-associated traits remain alternative explanations. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The distinction between intrinsic resistance—functions conserved in a lineage—and acquired resistance—genes concentrated in accessory or minority compartments—is therefore operational and graded rather than a rule based on gene name. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

**Fitness makes resistance conditional rather than uniformly costly or beneficial.** Random barcode transposon sequencing (RB-TnSeq), which measures competitive fitness of insertion mutants relative to a pool rather than absolute growth, found a pooled AMR-versus-background shift of **+0.086 [95% CI: +0.074, +0.098]** under non-antibiotic conditions. [src: amr_fitness_cost] AMR-gene knockouts averaged **−0.024**, while the non-AMR knockout background averaged approximately **−0.11**; thus the positive contrast means that AMR knockouts were less impaired than the comparison knockout class, not that they grew faster than an unperturbed strain. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

Exposure changes the interpretation. Across antibiotic experiments, **57.0%** of AMR genes showed a flip toward greater importance under antibiotic exposure, with **N = 797**, mean flip **+0.045**, and Wilcoxon **p = 0.0001**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Broad-spectrum efflux genes had mean flip **+0.094**, compared with **−0.001** for enzymatic-inactivation genes, suggesting that efflux can provide benefit across multiple antibiotic conditions whereas enzymatic inactivation is more dependent on a matching drug. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This condition dependence links resistance ecology to [[concepts/condition-specific-fitness]]: a gene may impose a relative burden in one environment and become important in another.

The corpus also separates shared stress from stressor-specific fitness. Across metal and sodium-chloride experiments, 4,304 of 10,821 metal-important gene records, or **39.8%**, were also NaCl-important, leaving **60.2%** classified as metal-specific in that analysis. [src: counter_ion_effects] A separate classification found **54.9%** metal-specific, **38.0%** generally sick across non-metal conditions, and **7.2%** metal-plus-stress records. [src: metal_specificity] These estimates are not interchangeable because their thresholds and condition sets differ, but together they show why a cross-metal signal should not automatically be read as a metal-specific mechanism. See [[concepts/shared-stress-versus-stressor-specific-fitness]].

**Metal resistance has a broad directional layer and a chemistry-dependent layer.** Across **317** organism–metal-pair observations involving **28** organisms and **85** unique metal pairs, **98.1%** of gene-level fitness correlations were positive and **99.1%** were statistically significant at **p < 0.05**. [src: metal_cross_resistance] No tested pair showed systematically negative cross-resistance, supporting a shared directional layer in which many cellular functions respond similarly across metals. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The strongest listed association was Fe–Zn with mean **r = 0.61**, while Al–Co was among the weakest at **r = 0.30**, indicating that correlation magnitude still depends on chemistry. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

The atlas adds a conservation dimension: across **22** organisms and **14** metals, metal-important genes were **87.4%** core versus **76.9%** for baseline genes. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] Essential-metal tolerance genes had a mean core-fraction delta of **+0.148**, compared with **+0.081** for toxic metals, suggesting that conserved cellular requirements contribute strongly to metal-fitness phenotypes. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] Yet a simple gene-presence repertoire score failed to predict metal fitness, and genome-size normalization was necessary because large open pangenomes otherwise dominated the score. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] Composite resistance values should therefore prioritize candidates rather than substitute for metal-specific expression, perturbation, or tolerance assays; see [[concepts/composite-resistance-score-limitations]].

**Mobile elements and defense systems create ecological networks, but association is not transfer.** Resistance islands provide evidence of coordinated inheritance: **1,517** islands were detected across **705** species, with mean size **6.2 genes**, median size **4 genes**, maximum size **43 genes**, and mean phi coefficient **0.827**. [src: amr_strain_variation] Here phi measures pairwise co-occurrence, so the result demonstrates tight co-inheritance but not co-selection, functional synergy, or physical residence on one mobile element. [src: amr_strain_variation] Of these islands, **1,343 (88%)** contained genes from multiple resistance mechanisms. [src: amr_strain_variation]

Prophage associations are scale-dependent. Across **1,953** genomes, **20,073 of 36,041 (55.7%)** AMR instances occurred on contigs carrying strict prophage markers, but only **1,991 (5.5%)** were within **5 genes** of a marker. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] At a **10-gene** threshold, nearby AMR genes were **67.6%** accessory versus **65.5%** for distal genes, with odds ratio **1.10** and bootstrap **95% confidence interval [1.024, 1.185]**. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] The species-level median odds ratio was **0.85**, so broad prophage-density/AMR-breadth covariance does not establish uniform local phage-mediated mobilization. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

Anti-phage defense itself is widespread and modular. Markers for at least one defense system occurred in **27,626 of 27,690** species-level pangenomes, or **99.8%**. [src: phage_defense_arsenal] After permutation testing and false-discovery correction, **27 of 28** defense-system pairs showed positive co-occurrence; the strongest was R-M Type II × Gabija, with odds ratio **24.0**. [src: phage_defense_arsenal] However, prevalence depends on marker definitions: EggNOG matching gave CRISPR-Cas prevalence of **96%**, whereas the Cas1 marker gave approximately **55%**. [src: phage_defense_arsenal] Prophage annotations likewise may include domesticated remnants or bacterial homologs rather than intact prophages. [src: prophage_ecology] These results support defense syndromes while requiring marker-aware interpretation; [[concepts/phage-defense-syndromes-and-arms-race]] is the detailed entry point.

## Tensions and Caveats

The most important ecological tension is between broad environment–AMR associations and weak within-species evidence. Environment explained **2–13%** of AMR-composition variance, yet only **20 of 141** testable families showed significant within-family effects, and whole-genome ecotype analysis found environmental effects absent in **156 species (90.7%)**. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, ecotype_analysis] This is the central issue in [[conflicts/conflict--amr_environmental_resistome--amr_fitness_cost--amr_pangenome_atlas--76f2bfcc]]: environment may structure species and communities without consistently selecting resistance differences within populations.

Pooled comparisons are also vulnerable to phylogenetic confounding. Pangenome openness correlated positively with AMR count in **8/10** tested phyla, but the overall correlation was **rho = 0.006**. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The contrast is documented in [[conflicts/conflict--amr_environmental_resistome--amr_pangenome_atlas--amr_strain_variation--a54e27a7]] and [[concepts/phylogenetic-confounding-of-pangenome-associations]]. Lineage composition, unequal sampling, and annotation scope can produce or conceal ecological associations.

A second load-bearing caveat concerns conservation and essentiality. Metal-important genes were more core than baseline genes, while AMR-specific baseline fitness showed no core/accessory difference, and broader fitness analyses contain neutral or condition-specific core genes. [src: metal_fitness_atlas, amr_fitness_cost, fitness_effects_conservation] Thus conservation is informative but not a universal proxy for essentiality, burden, or ecological importance; see [[conflicts/conflict--acinetobacter_adp1_explorer--adp1_triple_essentiality--alphafold_msa_annotation--cc872022]] and [[conflicts/conflict--amr_fitness_cost--conservation_fitness_synthesis--conservation_vs_fitness--2bd71881]].

Finally, mobile-element and metal signals remain observational. T4SS–CAZy proximity, prophage co-occurrence, resistance-island statistics, and metal-resistance neighborhoods support hypotheses about transfer or co-selection, but none alone identifies the carrier or demonstrates causal mobilization. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt, prophage_amr_comobilization, amr_strain_variation] Geographic hotspot results require similar caution because sampling-effort correction and study-origin checks were not yet reported. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography] These limitations motivate the scale-aware framing in [[concepts/scale-dependent-mobile-element-associations]].
