# Comparative Metabolic Dependencies

Comparative metabolic dependency asks when a genome-encoded pathway represents genuine nutritional independence, a condition-specific requirement, a latent capability, or a function supplied by other organisms. The BERIL corpus speaks to this question by combining pathway-completeness predictions, mutant-fitness measurements, growth phenotypes, metabolic models, pangenomes, metabolomics, and environmental occurrence data. Across these evidence types, the central result is not a single hierarchy of “more” or “less” dependency, but a conditional relationship among encoded capability, physiological demand, organismal network architecture, and community context.

## Literature Context

Published work supports the corpus’s central premise that metabolic dependence is conditional rather than reducible to pathway presence. Microbial communities have long been recognized as contributors to host nutrition, implying that nutritional capacity can be distributed across interacting organisms rather than encoded wholly within one genome ([PMID 3524615](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3524615/)). More recent work formalizes this ecological logic: reciprocal bacterial cross-feeding can limit the emergence of metabolic dependencies, showing that exchange may stabilize community function rather than simply create auxotrophy ([PMID 42117636](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42117636/)). Dynamic metagenome-scale modeling likewise treats community metabolism as time- and context-dependent, rather than as a static sum of annotated pathways ([PMID 37428554](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37428554/)). The corpus is therefore consistent with the literature in distinguishing encoded capability from realized nutritional independence and in treating [[concepts/community-metabolic-interdependence]] as a network property.

The corpus extends this background by quantifying the distinction across many organisms, pathway classes, and experimental conditions. Its finding that complete pathways can be latent, intermediate, or actively fitness-relevant complements examples in which growth depends on a particular environmental metabolite: methanol-essential growth in *Escherichia coli* demonstrates that an apparently broad metabolic repertoire can still yield a specific nutritional requirement ([PMID 29666370](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29666370/)). Similarly, studies of metabolic reprogramming show that changing intracellular resource use can alter bacterial growth or antibiotic susceptibility, reinforcing the corpus’s conclusion that physiological demand depends on condition and network state rather than substrate labels alone ([PMID 40790107](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40790107/); [PMID 39828906](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39828906/)). Work on ancient nitrogenases and light-enhanced respiration further illustrates that energy supply, ATP investment, and electron-transfer architecture constrain what pathways can accomplish under particular conditions ([PMID 38869277](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38869277/); [PMID 33396191](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33396191/)). The corpus’s comparisons of Complex I, alternative dehydrogenases, reducing-equivalent load, and recipient-specific wiring make these principles experimentally testable at comparative scale.

Its strongest novel contribution is the integrated evidence hierarchy linking pathway completeness, mutant fitness, growth, metabolic models, pangenomes, metabolomics, and environmental occurrence. The reported correlations between latent capability, pangenome openness, and metabolic ecotypes extend prior community and modeling perspectives toward genome-scale comparative ecology. At the same time, the corpus is in tension with any interpretation that treats negative associations between community pathway completeness and ambient metabolites as demonstrated exchange: the literature supports community nutritional contribution, but does not by itself establish producer identity, uptake, or flux. The corpus appropriately keeps this as a hypothesis. Likewise, its disagreement between modeled flux and observed respiratory dependency highlights a limitation less visible in static community models: feasible flux is not equivalent to gene essentiality, and occurrence is not in situ catabolic activity.

## What the Corpus Shows

**Pathway completeness is capability evidence, not proof of independence.**  
GapMind, a computational resource that evaluates whether genes plausibly cover the steps of a metabolic pathway, found 17 of 18 amino-acid biosynthesis pathways complete in all 7 successfully mapped organisms. Six organisms had 18/18 pathways, while *Desulfovibrio vulgaris* had 17/18 because serine biosynthesis was incomplete under the computational criterion. [src: essential_metabolome] This supports broad amino-acid prototrophy—the capacity to synthesize amino acids—but the apparent serine auxotrophy remains a hypothesis because divergent enzymes, non-canonical pathways, or annotation gaps could explain the result. [src: essential_metabolome] A matched validation in *Pseudomonas* FW300-N2E3 found 13/13 agreement between complete GapMind predictions and growth in Fitness Browser experiments, supporting pathway completeness as evidence of organism-level capability in that restricted comparison. [src: fw300_metabolic_consistency]

The broader comparisons establish why these results should not be read as universal nutritional independence. Among 1,695 complete pathway–organism pairs from 48 organisms, 267 (15.8%) were latent capabilities, 547 (32.3%) intermediate, and 881 (51.9%) active dependencies. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency] In a separate 161-pair analysis, complete pathways included 57 Active Dependency pairs and 66 Latent Capability pairs, alongside 24 Incomplete but Important and 14 Missing pairs. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] Thus, a complete pathway can be fitness-neutral under standard conditions, while an incomplete pathway can still contain genes with important measured fitness effects. The distinction is developed further in [[concepts/biosynthetic-prototrophy-and-auxotrophy]] and [[concepts/computational-pathway-prediction-validation]].

**Dependency is strongly conditional on pathway class and environment.**  
Amino-acid biosynthesis pathways were less often latent than carbon-source utilization pathways: 48 of 735 amino-acid pairs (6.5%) were latent, compared with 217 of 892 carbon-source pairs (24.3%). [src: metabolic_capability_dependency] This difference indicates that the metabolic category itself helps predict whether encoded capacity is expressed as measured dependency, although it does not identify the physiological mechanism in each organism. Pathway category strongly predicted dependency class, with χ²=163.6, df=4, p=2.5×10⁻³⁴. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency]

The condition space matters just as much. All 66 aggregate Latent Capability pairs in the 161-pair analysis became fitness-important under at least one condition type, especially nitrogen limitation, stress, or carbon limitation. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] The larger analysis found that the latent fraction remained non-trivial across 16 threshold combinations, ranging from 4.7% to 21.1%, although the exact fraction is classification-sensitive. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency] In the *Acinetobacter baylyi* ADP1 deletion matrix, 5 principal components captured 82% of variation across 2,034 genes and 8 carbon sources; PC1 represented general sensitivity, while PC2 separated urea-associated nitrogen metabolism from carbon metabolism. [src: adp1_deletion_phenotypes] These results support a multidimensional view in which broad stress sensitivity and substrate-specific requirements coexist. See [[concepts/condition-space-dimensionality]].

**Physiological demand depends on network architecture, not substrate labels alone.**  
Respiratory-chain comparisons provide a concrete example. Complex I, also called NDH-1, is a proton-pumping NADH dehydrogenase; NDH-2 is a non-pumping alternative NADH dehydrogenase. In transferred fitness data, Complex I orthologs had mean fitness of -1.35 on aromatic conditions versus -0.77 on comparison conditions, with p < 0.0001. [src: aromatic_catabolism_network] However, the largest Complex I defects occurred on acetate (-1.55) and succinate (-1.39), both non-aromatic substrates that generate substantial NADH through the tricarboxylic-acid cycle. [src: aromatic_catabolism_network] The stronger interpretation is therefore that dependence tracks reducing-equivalent load—the demand to reoxidize NADH—rather than aromatic chemistry itself.

ADP1-specific wiring supports this architecture-aware interpretation: Complex I was required on quinate, acetate required Complex I plus cytochrome bo3 and ACIAD3522, lactate specifically required cytochrome bo3, and glucose had no specifically required respiratory component. [src: respiratory_chain_wiring] Flux-balance analysis, a constraint-based method that predicts feasible metabolic fluxes, estimated 1.76× higher Complex I flux on aromatic substrates than on the comparison condition, with fluxes of 0.55 versus 0.31. [src: aromatic_catabolism_network] Yet the model predicted 0% Complex I essentiality, illustrating that modeled flux capacity and observed gene or complex dependency are not equivalent. [src: aromatic_catabolism_network] Ortholog transfer therefore requires recipient-specific network context, as discussed in [[concepts/cross-species-fitness-transferability]] and [[concepts/respiratory-capacity-and-nadh-load]].

**Comparative capacity is concentrated in specialists and variable pangenomes.**  
Carbon-utilization capacities were sparse across the screened genomes. Among 3,109 genomes carrying at least 1 of 8 callable capacities, 675 carried exactly 1, whereas 18 carried at least 3 and the maximum was 4; mean versatility was approximately 1.19 and the median was 1. [src: enigma_carbon_census_1] This pattern supports specialist-dominated utilization, with broader-capacity generalists uncommon. Capacities were concentrated in particular lineages, including Burkholderiales and Pseudomonadota, and some genera showed approximately 92–100% capacity for particular compounds despite sparse distribution across the full collection. [src: enigma_carbon_census_1]

Within species, pathway-completeness profiles produced a median of 4 metabolic ecotypes and a maximum of 8 among 225 species meeting the analysis criteria. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] Ecotype count correlated with pangenome openness at partial Spearman rho=0.322, p=8.0e-07 after controlling for genome count. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] At a broader clade level, latent-capability rate correlated positively with pangenome openness, Spearman ρ = 0.69, p = 0.0004, n = 22 clades. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency] These associations are consistent with genome fluidity creating opportunities for alternative or conditionally useful capacities, but they do not establish whether openness causes latent capability or whether both reflect ecological and sampling context. [[concepts/metabolic-capacity-specialization]] and [[concepts/pangenome-openness-determinants]] provide the comparative detail.

Cultured genomes also provide an incomplete view of available capacity. Quality-filtered deep-clay genomes had mean amino-acid pathway completeness of 15.50/18, lower than the soil baseline of 17.14/18, with p = 0.009 and Cohen’s d = −0.84. [src: clay_confined_subsurface] In contrast, 10 deep-clay Bacillota_B genomes had larger mean genome sizes than 62 soil-baseline genomes, 4,110,038 bp versus 3,046,124 bp, and more orthologous groups, 2,630 versus 2,106. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory] Genome expansion therefore does not necessarily appear as higher completeness in a fixed amino-acid pathway panel. The cultured cohort may also miss uncultured, unusually self-sufficient lineages; this tension is developed in [[concepts/biosynthetic-self-sufficiency-and-cultivation]].

**Community evidence is compatible with sharing, but does not demonstrate exchange.**  
Community-scale metabolomics found negative associations between community pathway completeness and ambient amino-acid intensity for 11 of 13 testable pathways, with a sign-test p = 0.011. [src: nmdc_community_metabolic_ecology] The strongest reported associations were leucine, r=-0.390, q=0.022, n=62, and arginine, r=-0.297, q=0.049, n=80. [src: discoveries] These patterns are compatible with Black Queen-like interdependence, in which some organisms reduce costly biosynthesis while relying on metabolites supplied by neighbors. They are not direct evidence of producers, recipients, uptake, or metabolite exchange. Tyrosine was an anti-Black-Queen outlier with r=+0.419, and isoleucine showed no significant association, r = −0.057, q = 0.823, n = 18. [src: nmdc_community_metabolic_ecology]

Community differentiation was dominated by carbon metabolism: PCA PC1 explained 49.4% of variance and separated Soil from Freshwater at p<0.0001, while amino-acid pathways loaded more strongly on PC2, which explained 16.6%. [src: discoveries] This places amino-acid interdependence within a broader environmental metabolic structure rather than making it the universal driver of community differentiation. The evidence boundary is summarized in [[concepts/community-metabolic-interdependence]] and [[concepts/occurrence-versus-catabolic-activity]].

## Tensions and Caveats

The main methodological tension is that matched comparisons can look nearly perfect while broader analyses reveal substantial latent or intermediate capability. FW300-N2E3 showed 13/13 GapMind–growth agreement, but the larger dataset classified 267 of 1,695 complete pairs as latent and 547 as intermediate. [src: fw300_metabolic_consistency, metabolic_capability_dependency] The promoted conflict [[conflicts/conflict--essential_metabolome--fw300_metabolic_consistency--metabolic_capability_dependency--96fcb005]] frames this as a difference in matched scope, organism coverage, and endpoint rather than a simple contradiction.

Model predictions also disagree with phenotypic and ecological evidence. Baseline FBA accuracy was 42.5% across 574 organism–carbon-source combinations, while the ADP1 respiratory model predicted zero NDH-2 flux despite unresolved evidence about compensation. [src: annotation_gap_discovery, respiratory_chain_wiring] The conflict [[conflicts/conflict--acinetobacter_adp1_explorer--adp1_triple_essentiality--annotation_gap_discovery--8f009ad9]] anchors the limitation: flux, growth, knockout fitness, pathway presence, and ecological activity are distinct endpoints.

The respiratory interpretation remains specifically unsettled. Earlier work treated Complex I as dispensable on glucose and lactate through possible NDH-2 compensation, whereas ADP1-specific analysis reported a glucose growth ratio of 1.44, zero predicted NDH-2 flux, and no significant cross-species compensation pattern, p = 0.52. [src: aromatic_catabolism_network, respiratory_chain_wiring] This is the focus of conflict  aromatic_catabolism_network  respiratory_chain_wiring  10ef6928e and conflict  aromatic_catabolism_network  metabolic_capability_dependency  respiratory_chain_wiring  e62728ec46873dcf.

Finally, occurrence and genomic potential cannot establish in situ catabolism. The environmental atlas detected 83 of 86 implicated genera in 1,719 NMDC metagenomes, but measured neither compound uptake nor degradation. [src: enigma_carbon_census_1] The NMDC metabolomics dataset was dominated by one study, with 125 of 131 samples (95%) from that study, and only approximately 2% of compounds had KEGG identifiers. [src: discoveries] Community associations therefore support hypotheses about provisioning and ecological context, not demonstrated metabolic flux. [[conflicts/conflict--enigma_carbon_census_1--essential_metabolome--metabolic_capability_dependency--8ff1555a]] records this boundary.
