# Subsurface Microbial Ecology

Subsurface microbial ecology in this corpus is the study of how microbial communities and their functional potential vary with depth, groundwater flow, mineral or clay-associated habitats, and isolation history. The corpus speaks to this topic from complementary scales: 16S amplicon sequence variant (ASV) surveys resolve spatial and hydrogeological community structure, while comparative genomics examines accessory functions and respiratory markers in subsurface-associated [[entities/bacillota-b]] lineages. Together, the projects support strong environmental organization of subsurface microbiomes, but they also show that inferred metabolism, genome specialization, and habitat mechanisms must be constrained by marker validation, phylogeny, sampling design, and missing geochemistry. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology, bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory, clay_confined_subsurface]

## Literature Context

Published studies generally show that subsurface and sedimentary microbial communities are spatially organized by depth, physicochemical gradients, and hydrological or depositional setting. In riparian sediments, mill dams have been reported to alter microbiome structure and its distribution with depth, demonstrating that engineered changes to sedimentary environments can reorganize vertical community patterns [PMID 37455732](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37455732/). Vertical bacterial distributions have likewise been associated with soil pH and related edaphic conditions in red-soil profiles [PMID 35109809](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35109809/), while landfill studies have linked microbial structure and diversity to physicochemical parameters [PMID 32671700](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32671700/). Studies of marine sediments and arid desert soils further establish that microbial composition can vary across spatially heterogeneous sedimentary landscapes and environmental gradients [PMID 26869600](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26869600/); [PMID 33438074](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33438074/). Against this background, the corpus’s finding that hydrogeological zone explains more community variance than well identity, and that communities differ strongly among depths within the same well, is consistent with the literature. Its meter-scale distance-decay pattern and persistent well-specific structure over nine days extend that literature by resolving spatial organization at unusually fine spatial and temporal scales within a subsurface observatory.

The literature also supports interpreting environmental stratification as a potential organizer of microbial function, but generally treats the relevant chemistry as an essential constraint. In a petrochemical-contaminated site, biogeochemical stratification has been linked to microbial hydrocarbon-degradation potential [PMID 40617568](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40617568/). Work on petroleum contamination has similarly connected microbial community dynamics with bioremediation conditions [PMID 40241730](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40241730/), while studies of coal-tar-impacted aquifer sediments have examined PAH mineralization together with associated microbial community structure [PMID 17617439](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17617439/). A wetland-bioreactor study provides an additional precedent for relating microbial diversity to perchlorate and nitrate remediation performance [PMID 16128443](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16128443/). The corpus’s proposed O₂-to-nitrate-to-iron-to-sulfate-to-fermentation sequence is therefore consistent with the broader expectation that redox and contaminant gradients structure subsurface metabolism, but it does not yet provide the direct geochemistry or activity measurements used to establish such mechanisms. The inferred SSO redox map consequently extends existing ecological interpretation as a testable hypothesis rather than a demonstrated process model.

The corpus is more novel in combining community-scale hydrogeological zonation with comparative genome-content analysis of subsurface-associated [[entities/bacillota-b]]. Its enriched accessory orthologs and larger deep-clay genomes suggest cohort-level functional differentiation, whereas the literature candidates primarily address community composition, environmental gradients, or measured remediation processes. At the same time, the corrected multi-heme marker analysis places the corpus in tension with a simple claim of enhanced deep-subsurface iron or sulfur respiration: the apparent shallow-enrichment narrative disappears after marker validation, and phylogenetic controls indicate that some signals track lineage rather than habitat. Thus, the corpus reinforces published evidence for environmental structuring, while adding stronger cautions about marker validity, phylogenetic confounding, and the distinction between inferred metabolic potential and measured activity.

## What the Corpus Shows

**Hydrogeological position organizes community composition.** In the SSO sediment dataset, hydrogeological zone explained 27.5% of community variance, whereas well identity explained 19.2% and was not significant; samples from the same well but different depths had median Bray–Curtis dissimilarity of 0.977, compared with 0.835 for samples from the same depth zone in different wells. Bray–Curtis dissimilarity measures compositional difference based on taxon abundances. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology] This pattern indicates that vertical habitat position can structure communities more strongly than horizontal well identity in this dataset, although it does not identify the environmental mechanism responsible. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology] The depth classes were VZ, VSZ, SZ1, and SZ2, and 10 of 12 dominant phyla had significant depth associations. Chloroflexi, Patescibacteria, Myxococcota, and Spirochaetota were enriched toward shallow depths, whereas Firmicutes, WPS-2, Bacteroidota, and Proteobacteria were enriched toward deeper depths. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology] The broader interpretation is developed in [[concepts/subsurface-hydrogeological-zonation]].

The SSO sediment samples also showed meter-scale spatial structure. Across 9 wells arranged in a 3×3 grid spanning approximately 6 m, a Mantel test detected distance-decay of community similarity, with Spearman ρ = 0.323 and p = 0.029 using 9,999 permutations. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology] Several well pairs, including U3–M6, M6–L7, and U3–L7, were more similar than geographic distance alone predicted, supporting a possible northeast-to-southwest corridor of shared communities. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology] The corridor may reflect flow or plume structure, but the dataset does not directly demonstrate that interpretation. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology]

**Spatial structure can persist over short intervals.** Groundwater communities from 5 wells sampled on September 9 and September 18, 2024 showed that well identity explained 49.9% of community variance, with p = 0.001. Sampling date explained only 0.8%, with p = 0.998, while depth within the saturated zone explained 2.5%, with p = 0.430; filter size explained 10.1%, with p = 0.001. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology] Median Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was 0.917 between spatial samples, 0.351 between temporal samples, and 0.750 between filter sizes. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology] A Mantel comparison of the two sampling dates found Spearman ρ = 0.867 and p = 0.001, indicating that well-to-well similarity rankings were largely preserved across the 9-day interval. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology]

This is best described as short-term persistence of spatial organization, not general ecological stability. The study did not resolve seasonal or long-term plume dynamics, and groundwater ASV data were available for only 5 of 9 wells, excluding the proposed hotspot wells M5 and U3. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology] The report registered 221 geochemistry sample tubes in CORAL, but metals, ion chromatography/total organic carbon, isotopes, ammonia, and nitrite measurements had not been loaded into the analyzed dataset. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology] Thus, spatially structured communities are observed, whereas the proposed northeast-to-southwest contamination plume remains a testable hypothesis. [[concepts/spatial-structure-versus-short-term-temporal-stability]]

**Redox organization is plausible but inferred rather than measured.** The SSO analysis assigned taxa to redox-associated processes and used their distributions to infer a possible electron-acceptor sequence. Redox refers to oxidation–reduction chemistry, in which microbes transfer electrons between donors and acceptors. The proposed sequence was O₂ → NO₃⁻ → Fe(III) → SO₄²⁻ → fermentation, with oxidative processes near U3, denitrification near M5, and fermentation near L9. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology]

Specific inferred peaks included denitrification of 7.7% at M5, associated with [[entities/rhodanobacter]], iron oxidation of 2.8% at U3, associated with [[entities/sideroxydans]], nitrification of 2.3% at U3, iron reduction of 2.3% at U1, sulfur oxidation of 1.9% at M4, methanotrophy of 1.9% at M4, and fermentation of 5.3% at L9, associated with [[entities/spirochaeta]] and [[entities/paenisporosarcina]]. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology] M5 was hypothesized to be a plume-mixing zone, while M6 was hypothesized to be an anaerobic dead zone. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology]

The evidential limitation is substantial: class-level trait inference had 78% coverage, but only 65 of 1,038 genera were annotated, genus-level functional annotation covered 21% of total reads, species-level classification was approximately 0%, and 56% of reads remained outside genus-level inference. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology] These percentages describe inferred potential from taxonomy, not measured catabolic activity or geochemical flux. Direct measurements are therefore needed before the proposed redox ladder is treated as a site-specific metabolic map. [[concepts/subsurface-hydrogeological-zonation]]

**Subsurface-associated Bacillota_B show genome-content differentiation.** A comparison of 10 deep-clay and 62 soil-baseline [[entities/bacillota-b]] genomes tested 14,109 Firmicutes-level eggNOG orthologous groups (OGs), which are clusters of evolutionarily related proteins. The analysis used Fisher’s exact tests, Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction, a fold-difference threshold of ≥3, and presence in at least 3 anchor genomes. It found 547 significantly enriched OGs at q<0.05. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory] The deep-clay genomes also had larger genomes and more OGs than the soil-baseline lineages, consistent with an interpretation of expanded accessory capacity and increased self-sufficiency rather than genome streamlining. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory]

Keyword classification identified enriched functions associated with anaerobic respiration, sporulation and revival, mineral attachment or exopolysaccharides, anaerobic regulation, and osmoadaptation. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory] However, manual inspection suggested that approximately 80–100 of the 547 enriched OGs were more plausibly related to anaerobic respiration or electron transfer, because relevant functions had been placed in an “other” category. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory] The genome result therefore supports broad cohort-level differentiation while requiring careful functional reclassification. [[concepts/subsurface-bacillota-specialization]]

The cohort is not a representative census of all subsurface Bacillota_B. The BERDL pangenome contained 334 genomes rather than the v1.1 plan’s estimate of 6,700, and the 10 anchor genomes were clumped among several lineages, including 3 BRH-c8a genomes, 2 BRH-c4a genomes, 2 Desulfosporosinus genomes, Desulforudis, Ch130, and 1 other genome. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory] The available comparison was also dominated by borehole and porewater-associated cultivation and could not test whether rock-attached Bacillota_B have the same gene-content pattern. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory] The clay-focused comparison further found that only SR remained significant after phylogenetic control, while WL and NiFe primarily tracked Bacillota_B lineage. [src: clay_confined_subsurface]

**Functional markers can overturn ecological narratives.** The original clay iron-reduction comparison used K07811, K17324, and K17323, but these corresponded to TMAO reductase, glycerol ABC ATP-binding, and glycerol ABC permease genes rather than canonical iron-reduction markers. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory] The corrected detector combined PFAM protein-family signals PF02085 and PF22678 with a threshold of at least 4 CXXCH heme-binding motifs in gene-cluster protein sequences; a genome was positive if any signal was present. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory]

Under the corrected detector, anchor_deep contained 5/9 positives, or 55.6%; anchor_shallow contained 12/30, or 40.0%; and soil_baseline contained 61/149, or 40.9%. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory] Pairwise Fisher tests found no significant differences: deep versus shallow had odds ratio 1.88 and p=0.46, deep versus baseline had odds ratio 1.80 and p=0.49, and shallow versus baseline had odds ratio 0.96 and p=1.0. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory] The original shallow-enrichment narrative therefore loses support after marker correction. [[concepts/functional-marker-validation]] [[concepts/multi-heme-cytochrome-detection]]

## Tensions and Caveats

The central conflict is whether the Bacillota_B genome-content differences represent broad subsurface specialization or a lineage- and cohort-specific signal. [[conflicts/conflict--bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory--clay_confined_subsurface--enigma_sso_asv_ecology]] The 547 enriched OGs and larger genomes support differentiation among the sampled deep-clay genomes, but the small, lineage-clumped anchor cohort, the 334-genome universe, and phylogenetic-control results limit generalization. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory, clay_confined_subsurface] The absence of elevated completeness across the tested 18 pathways in deep genomes further shows that genome expansion and pathway completeness need not answer the same biological question. [src: clay_confined_subsurface]

A second conflict concerns terminology and possibly biological scope: one project calls the retained 5/9 signal “sulfite reduction,” whereas another calls it dissimilatory “sulfate reduction.” [[conflicts/conflict--bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory--clay_confined_subsurface]] Both report 5/9 positives and the same null scale, but the marker definitions and biochemical step have not been reconciled. [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory, clay_confined_subsurface] The labels should not be treated as interchangeable until the underlying genes, thresholds, and pathway annotations are compared.

Finally, the SSO redox map and plume interpretation remain hypothesis-generating. The direct geochemistry was unavailable, taxonomy-to-trait inference covered only part of the reads, and filter size contributed 10.1% of groundwater variance. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology] Community structure is therefore stronger evidence than the proposed mechanism, and marker presence is weaker evidence than demonstrated activity. Correct interpretation requires combining spatial replication, contemporaneous geochemistry, sequence-level marker validation, and phylogenetically controlled genome comparisons. [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology, bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory]
