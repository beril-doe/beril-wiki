# Functional Dark Matter — Experimentally Prioritized Novel Genetic Systems

## Overview

This report integrates Fitness Browser fitness profiles, essentiality, pangenome links, fitness modules, gene domains, environmental metadata, GapMind pathway analysis, cross-organism concordance, synteny, co-fitness, and Bakta reannotation to prioritize experimentally actionable bacterial genes of unknown function. Across 48 organisms, it identifies 57,011 dark genes among 228,709 genes, ranks candidates through evidence-weighted and conservation-weighted routes, and converts gene-level evidence into organism-level experimental campaigns. [src: functional_dark_matter]

## Key Findings

### Scale and evidence coverage

Of 228,709 genes across 48 Fitness Browser organisms, 57,011 (24.9%) lack functional annotation as hypothetical proteins, DUFs, or uncharacterized genes. Of these, 7,787 show strong fitness effects (|fitness| ≥ 2 in at least one condition) and 9,557 are essential because no viable transposon mutants were recovered; together, 17,344 genes have experimentally measurable phenotypes. Dark genes range from more than 35% to less than 15% of an organism’s genes, a pattern interpreted as annotation-depth variation rather than established differences in functional content. The report notes that its 24.9% dark-gene fraction aligns with published estimates of 25–40% hypothetical genes in typical bacterial genomes (Makarova et al. 2019). [src: functional_dark_matter] Two figures plot these data: the per-organism annotation breakdown (`fig01_annotation_breakdown.png`) and the fitness distributions of dark versus annotated genes (`fig02_fitness_distributions.png`). [src: functional_dark_matter]

Among the 57,011 dark genes, 39,532 (69.3%) have pangenome links, 12,686 are accessory, 511 are both accessory and strongly fitness-active, and 6,142 belong to independent-component-analysis (ICA) fitness modules, which group genes with coordinated fitness profiles. Stress conditions, including metals, oxidative stress, and osmotic stress, dominate strong dark-gene phenotypes, followed by carbon-source and nitrogen-source utilization. [src: functional_dark_matter] The report treats the 511 dark genes that are both accessory and strongly fitness-active as the prime candidates for biogeographic analysis. Dark-gene evidence coverage across pangenome, module, ortholog and essentiality dimensions is plotted in `fig03_dark_gene_coverage.png`, and the condition-class distribution for dark genes with strong phenotypes in `fig04_condition_classes.png`. The census behind these counts integrates four prior observatory projects (fitness_modules, essential_genome, conservation_vs_fitness, module_conservation) with direct Fitness Browser queries into a single table of every dark gene across all 48 organisms, because those projects answered different questions in different formats and no unified catalog existed. The census identifies 57,011 dark genes (24.9% of 228,709 total), of which 39,532 (69.3%) link to the pangenome, 6,142 (10.8%) belong to ICA fitness modules with function predictions, 7,787 (13.7%) show strong fitness effects (|fit| ≥ 2) and 9,557 (16.8%) are essential; the intersection of accessory dark genes with strong fitness is 511 genes. The report concludes that one quarter of bacterial genes remain dark but that the problem is tractable: 17,344 have measurable phenotypes (fitness or essentiality) and 39,532 connect to the pangenome. [src: functional_dark_matter]

### Metabolic gaps and pathway hypotheses

GapMind identified 1,256 organism–pathway pairs across 44 Fitness Browser-linked species in which nearly complete pathways had `steps_missing_low` scores and co-occurred with strongly fitness-active dark genes. The most frequent gaps were carbon-utilization pathways and one amino-acid pathway: fucose utilization in 32 organisms (examples: Marinobacter, *P. stutzeri* RCH2, *D. vulgaris*), rhamnose utilization in 31 (Marinobacter, *P. putida*, Phaeo), sorbitol utilization in 30 (*D. desulfuricans*, *D. vulgaris*, Miyama), myoinositol utilization in 28 (*P. putida*, *P. syringae*, WCS417), gluconate utilization in 26 (Marinobacter, *D. desulfuricans*), and asparagine biosynthesis in 24 organisms across diverse phyla. Marinobacter had 49 gapped pathways, while *Desulfovibrio desulfuricans* ME-23 and *Pseudomonas stutzeri* had 45 each. The report notes that these organisms also have the largest dark-gene complements and the most specific fitness phenotypes. From this it suggests the hypothesis, not an established assignment, that functionally dark genes may encode their missing steps. GapMind gap-filling candidates are plotted in `fig05_gapmind_gaps.png`. The underlying query covered 305M GapMind pathway rows, filtered to the 44 FB-linked species. The report frames a dark gene near a gap as a candidate for the missing enzyme, not a confirmed one. [src: functional_dark_matter]

These GapMind results are organism-level co-occurrences, not direct gene-to-enzyme assignments. Across the 1,256 organism–pathway pairs, supplementary domain matching identified 42,239 gene–pathway candidates across 3,186 unique dark genes, including 5,398 high-confidence EC-prefix matches, 4,687 medium-confidence Pfam-family matches, and 32,154 low-confidence keyword matches. The matching used curated pathway–enzyme mappings: EC prefixes, Pfam families and functional keywords. The resulting domain-compatible candidates narrow the search from all dark genes in a gapped organism to those with enzymatically plausible annotations. Compatibility does not validate enzymatic function. The analysis is plotted in `fig28_domain_matching.png`, which shows matches per pathway and organism, the confidence tiers, and fitness stratified by confidence tier. Confirming individual assignments requires EC matching, structure prediction, enzymology, or other experimental validation. [src: functional_dark_matter]

Cross-organism fitness concordance identified 65 ortholog groups present in at least three organisms whose dark-gene orthologs showed measurable effects under the same condition classes. The top concordant groups span carbon utilization, stress response and motility, and every listed top group had concordance of 1.00. [src: functional_dark_matter]

- OG14628: carbon source; concordance 1.00 across 5 organisms; in *Ralstonia* spp.
- OG12530: carbon source; concordance 1.00 across 4 organisms; DUF2844; in *B. thailandensis* and *Burkholderia* sp. 376. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The report gives three interpretations. It reads the carbon-source concordance as suggesting conserved but unannotated components of carbohydrate metabolism. It reads the motility concordance as suggesting unannotated parts of the chemotaxis machinery. It reads OG05812's Peptidase_M50 domain, from the site-2 protease family, as hinting at a conserved regulatory protease under stress. All three remain functional hypotheses. The cross-organism fitness-concordance heatmap is plotted in `fig06_concordance.png`. The concordance finding places the strongest concordance in carbon source genes (spanning 3–8 organisms) and motility genes. The report's hypothesis assessment, which tests the 65 ortholog groups present in 3+ Fitness Browser organisms and calls H1c supported, names only motility-related dark genes as showing the strongest cross-organism concordance, consistent with conserved but incompletely annotated chemotaxis machinery. The two passages differ in emphasis, and the chemotaxis reading is an interpretation. [src: functional_dark_matter]

Phylogenetic analysis mapped 30,756 dark-gene clusters across 27,690 species. The initial eggNOG ortholog-group breadth classification assigned 30,721 of 30,756 clusters (99.9%) to universal breadth, making it poorly discriminative. As used in NB05's s_pangenome score, it produced identical conservation scores. At this earlier Fitness Browser-only stage, species counts ranged from 1 to 33, with median 1 and mean 2.2. The report stated at that stage that species count (1–33) provides finer resolution than the coarse universal/clade-restricted classification. This 1–33 range is not the later full-pangenome range. A continuous species-count scoring variant (`min(n_species / 20, 1.0) × 0.5`) had Spearman ρ = 0.982 with the original ranking. Its top-50 overlap was 62% and its top-100 overlap was 58%, so the candidate lists change meaningfully; the variant is provided as a supplementary analysis. Breadth patterns range from clade-restricted (single phylum) to widespread (3+ phyla), and the report treats widespread unannotated families as the highest-priority unknowns. The breadth distribution and the species-count variant are plotted in `fig07_phylo_breadth.png` and `fig30_species_count_scoring.png`; the latter shows species-count score distributions, a rank comparison, rank changes and a top-20 table. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The report states that the coarse eggNOG breadth classification (99.9% "universal") and the sparse 48-organism Fitness Browser sampling (37/48 Proteobacteria) fail to meaningfully distinguish conservation patterns among dark genes. The report adds that the NB09 evidence-weighted approach relies on Fitness Browser data, which limits its conservation assessment to the 48 FB organisms. To address this, NB11 queried the full 27,690-species GTDB r214 pangenome. It exploded eggNOG annotations across 93.5M gene clusters and matched them against 11,774 dark-gene root ortholog groups. Ortholog-group identifier propagation then recovered an additional 5,206 dark genes (9.1%), by transferring assignments from genes with pangenome links to genes in the same 48-organism ortholog group that lacked them. Together these steps raised conservation coverage from 32,791 (57.5%) to 37,997 (66.6%) dark genes. Species counts ranged from 1 to 27,482, with median 135 and mean 2,128, replacing the previous 1-to-33 range from the FB-only analysis; phylum counts ranged from 1 to 142. Among 11,774 root ortholog groups, 55.9% were kingdom-level, 11.0% class-level, 10.5% family-level, 6.9% genus-level, 6.5% mobile, 4.8% phylum-level, 3.9% order-level, and 0.5% species-level. Each tier reflects the narrowest rank containing all member species. Mobile elements are detected through phylogenetic patchiness, meaning presence in distant phyla but few species per phylum. At the gene level, across 57,011 dark genes including 19,014 FB-only fallback assignments, the tiers were kingdom 51.4%, species 29.8%, genus 6.2%, class 4.0%, family 3.5%, mobile 1.9%, phylum 1.9% and order 1.3%. The report interprets the over-half share of pan-bacterial dark-gene ortholog groups as showing that functional dark matter is a fundamental limitation in understanding broadly conserved biology, not a minor annotation gap. The full-pangenome species-count distribution, compared with the old Fitness Browser-only distribution, and the phyla distribution are plotted in `fig38_pangenome_species_distribution.png`. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The conservation-by-ignorance classification sorted dark genes into three tiers. 6.0% were strong testable hypotheses: a module prediction with EC number, high cross-organism concordance, a high-confidence GapMind match, or a named domain plus strong fitness. 52.5% were weak leads: a DUF-only domain, a bare module prediction, a medium/low GapMind match, strong fitness without annotation, or a named domain without fitness. 41.5% were true knowledge gaps, with zero functional evidence of any kind. A methods summary instead phrases these 6.0%, 52.5% and 41.5% shares as classifications of dark-gene ortholog groups, placing them alongside the ortholog-group taxonomic-tier percentages. The two denominators, dark genes and ortholog groups, should not be conflated. The highest-ranked knowledge gaps included COG0468 with 27,427 species across 142 phyla, COG0443 with 27,279 species, and COG0491 with 27,393 species. The importance score `conservation × ignorance` ranks all 11,774 dark-gene ortholog groups; the report defines it as "tier-adjusted conservation score + log₂-scaled species fraction × ignorance multiplier" in one passage. A methods passage gives the grouping explicitly as (tier-adjusted conservation + log₂-scaled species fraction) × ignorance multiplier. Kingdom-level true knowledge gaps score highest and species-specific strong hypotheses score lowest. The report's description of this score names three top-ranked groups: COG0468 (27,427 species, 142 phyla, true knowledge gap), COG0443 (27,279 species, true knowledge gap) and COG0172 (27,431 species, weak lead). A conservation-weighted covering set selected 42 organisms covering 95.6% of importance-weighted priority across 28,584 high-priority dark genes. It selected organisms by `sum(importance) × tractability × phylo_bonus`, where phylo_bonus penalizes genus-redundant selections. *Sinorhizobium meliloti* ranks first (1,630 genes, 195 kingdom-level gaps), followed by *P. putida* (1,043 genes, 172 kingdom gaps) and *S. oneidensis* MR-1 (805 genes, 105 kingdom gaps). The report lists *Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron* with 1,382 genes and 267 kingdom gaps, labels this the "highest raw count", and says it was deprioritized by 0.3 tractability. That label is inconsistent with its gene count, which is lower than the 1,630 genes listed for *S. meliloti*, though it would fit the kingdom-gap count; the report does not say which count it means. The first 10 organisms cover 31% of priority. Per-organism plans recommend three experiment types: CRISPRi (CRISPR interference) knockdown for essential genes in tractable organisms, condition-specific TnSeq for fitness-active genes, and broad phenotypic screens for true knowledge gaps. Top conditions come from fitness data or keyword inference. Six figures plot these outputs: the Fitness Browser organism phylum distribution, showing sparse taxonomic coverage (`fig32_organism_taxonomy.png`); conservation tier × hypothesis status stacked bars, absolute and normalized (`fig33_conservation_tiers.png`); a 2D heatmap of taxonomic tier × hypothesis status with counts and percentages (`fig34_classification_heatmap.png`); the top 25 true-knowledge-gap ortholog groups by pangenome species count (`fig35_top_knowledge_gaps.png`); conservation-weighted covering-set optimization, with cumulative coverage and marginal genes (`fig36_covering_set_curve.png`); and per-organism dark-gene coverage by conservation tier for the top 20 organisms (`fig37_experiment_plan_heatmap.png`). [src: functional_dark_matter]

### Environmental and lab–field concordance

Within-species carrier-versus-non-carrier analyses tested 151 accessory dark-gene clusters across 31 species; 10 of 137 clusters with environmental-category tests showed significant enrichment at false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, and 1 of 67 showed significant AlphaEarth embedding divergence. A pre-registered lab–field mapping found 29 of 47 testable clusters concordant (61.7%); pH-associated genes had 100% concordance across 4 tests and nitrogen-source genes had 78% concordance across 9 tests. [src: functional_dark_matter] Six clusters reached FDR < 0.2, including:
- *K. oxytoca* BWI76_RS15525 (carbon |fit| 2.3): 14.3% of carriers versus 2.5% of non-carriers were in the expected soil, freshwater or plant environments (odds ratio (OR) 6.6, FDR 0.069).
- *K. oxytoca* BWI76_RS15535 (carbon |fit| 2.2): the same 14.3% versus 2.5% split (OR 6.6, FDR 0.069).
- *P. putida* N2C3 AO356_12450 (carbon |fit| 2.1): 62.5% versus 0% (OR inf, FDR 0.093).
- *P. putida* N2C3 AO356_11255 (nitrogen |fit| 3.4): 80.0% of carriers versus 8.3% of non-carriers were in the expected soil, freshwater or wastewater environments (OR 44.0, FDR 0.093).
- *P. putida* N2C3 AO356_25185 (anaerobic |fit| 2.7): 55.6% versus 0% in the expected soil, freshwater or animal environments (OR inf, FDR 0.178).
- *P. putida* N2C3 AO356_24150 (nitrogen |fit| 3.0): 55.6% versus 0% in the expected soil, freshwater or wastewater environments (OR inf, FDR 0.178).
Three figures plot this analysis: carrier-species environmental distributions (`fig08_env_distribution.png`), carrier-versus-non-carrier test results (`fig09_carrier_tests.png`) and an AlphaEarth embedding UMAP (`fig10_embedding_umap.png`). The lab–field concordance matrix is plotted in `fig11_concordance_matrix.png`. The report's summary of this analysis adds that NMDC independent validation confirmed all 4 pre-registered predictions and that 76/105 abiotic correlations reached significance. It explicitly cautions that compositional coupling inflates the NMDC signal. It nonetheless argues that directional concordance across independent datasets supports the inference that lab fitness phenotypes reflect real ecological function. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The 10 significant environmental-category clusters were:
- *Pseudomonas putida*: PP_0025 (stress |fit| 4.8; human_clinical; odds ratio 27.5; FDR 7e-6), PP_3434 (nitrogen |fit| 3.1; human_clinical; odds ratio 28.6; FDR 7e-6), PP_0642 (nitrogen |fit| 2.8; human_clinical; odds ratio 11.6; FDR 0.001) and PP_3105 (stress |fit| 3.7; human_assoc; odds ratio inf; FDR 0.004).
- *P. syringae* B728a: Psyr_0167 (in planta |fit| 4.6; plant_assoc; odds ratio 11.9; FDR 0.005) and Psyr_2830 (stress |fit| 3.4; plant_assoc; odds ratio 10.9; FDR 0.031).
- *P. putida* N2C3: AO356_11255 (nitrogen |fit| 3.4; freshwater; odds ratio inf; FDR 0.031; module-predicted D-Ala-D-Ala carboxypeptidase).
- Lower rather than higher carrier association with the category: *B. thetaiotaomicron* 354052 (stress |fit| 2.4; human_assoc; odds ratio 0.17; FDR 0.005), *B. thetaiotaomicron* 350920 (stress |fit| 2.2; human_assoc; odds ratio 0.37; FDR 0.031) and *K. oxytoca* BWI76_RS15640 (carbon |fit| 2.1; human_assoc; odds ratio 0.14; FDR 0.031; module-predicted phage-tail tape-measure protein). [src: functional_dark_matter]

The report draws two patterns from these hits, both interpretations rather than established functions. *P. putida* stress and nitrogen dark genes are enriched in clinical or human-associated isolates, which suggests host-associated niche adaptation. *P. syringae* in-planta and stress dark genes are enriched in plant-associated genomes, consistent with their lab phenotypes. Elsewhere, in the lab–field mapping, the report singles out AO356_11255, the project's top candidate, as the clearest signal: 80% of carrier genomes versus only 8.3% of non-carriers come from soil/freshwater/wastewater environments (OR = 44, FDR = 0.093), matching its lab phenotype of strong nitrogen-utilization fitness. It also describes AO356_11255's carriers in two ways: as found exclusively in freshwater/soil environments, and as enriched in soil, freshwater, and wastewater environments relative to non-carriers. Under either description the carriers match the gene's nitrogen-utilization fitness phenotype. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The one-sided binomial test for 29/47 concordant clusters gave p = 0.072, with Wilson score 95% CI [0.474, 0.742], which includes 0.50, so the result is marginal, while Fisher’s combined probability across 47 individual tests gave p = 0.031; these statistical tests are summarized in `fig31_statistical_tests.png`, which presents a binomial null, a QQ plot, sign tests and a summary table. NMDC, the National Microbiome Data Collaborative dataset, independently mapped 5 of 6 carrier genera to 47 taxon columns across 6,365 metagenomic samples and confirmed all 4 testable pre-registered abiotic predictions: nitrogen-source carriers correlated with total nitrogen (ρ = +0.109, n = 1,231, FDR = 2.3e-4) and ammonium nitrogen (ρ = +0.231, n = 1,230, FDR = 8.0e-16), pH carriers correlated with pH (ρ = +0.157, n = 4,366, FDR = 7.4e-25), and anaerobic carriers correlated negatively with dissolved oxygen (ρ = -0.298, n = 272, FDR = 1.5e-6). The NMDC metagenomic samples are entirely separate from the pangenome-based carrier analysis, so the report treats these nitrogen, pH and oxygen associations as independent confirmations. [src: functional_dark_matter]

Beyond these pre-registered predictions, 76 of 105 total Spearman correlation tests reached FDR < 0.05 (72.4%). The report attributes this high rate largely to confounding: the carrier genera (*Pseudomonas*, *Klebsiella*, *Bacteroides*) are among the most abundant and ubiquitous taxa in NMDC samples, so their abundance correlates broadly with many abiotic variables regardless of condition class. It therefore treats the 4/4 pre-registered prediction rate as the more meaningful metric, because it tests specific directional hypotheses. NMDC abiotic correlation results are plotted as a volcano plot in `fig12_nmdc_correlations.png`. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The condition–environment mapping departed from the plan. The research plan specified 7 condition–environment mappings, including osmotic, temperature and oxidative stress, but the implementation used 6: stress (consolidating metal, osmotic and oxidative stress), carbon source, nitrogen source, pH, motility and anaerobic. The consolidation was needed because the Fitness Browser `expGroup` field uses a broad "stress" category rather than sub-categorizing stress types; motility and anaerobic were added because they emerged as prominent condition classes among dark genes with strong fitness effects. [src: functional_dark_matter]

NMDC trait-feature analysis, using the same genus bridge against 76 functional-group columns across 6,365 samples, confirmed all 7 pre-registered trait-condition predictions at FDR < 10⁻²¹, including nitrogen-source carriers with nitrogen fixation (ρ = 0.60) and nitrate denitrification (ρ = 0.52), and carbon-source carriers with aerobic chemoheterotrophy (ρ = 0.73) and fermentation (ρ = 0.59), and anaerobic carriers with fermentation (ρ = 0.59) and iron respiration (ρ = 0.45). However, 441 of 449 exploratory tests also reached FDR < 0.05, and the report attributes this high rate largely to compositional coupling because abundant genera contribute to both carrier abundance and community trait scores, rather than to independent gene–phenotype validation. Separately, a binomial sign test on the 7/7 correct-direction pre-registered trait predictions gave p = 0.0078, which the report takes as showing that the directional hypotheses are significantly non-random. An NMDC trait–condition correlation heatmap covering the 7 pre-registered and the exploratory tests is plotted in `fig21_trait_correlations.png`. [src: functional_dark_matter]

### Candidate prioritization and experimental design

A six-axis composite score combined fitness importance, cross-organism conservation, inference quality, pangenome distribution, biogeographic signal, and experimental tractability, with each dimension scaled 0–1. It ranked all 17,344 phenotype-bearing dark genes, translating diverse evidence types into a single prioritization. Its top-ranked genes are those where the most evidence converges and the experimental path is clearest. The report describes the six scored evidence axes as producing 100 ranked candidates with specific functional hypotheses and suggested experiments. These 17,344 are the strong-fitness and essential dark genes defined above taken together, not two separate populations. The top 100 candidates span 22 organisms, with Shewanella MR-1 contributing 25, *P. putida* N2C3 18, and Marinobacter 9; 82/100 have high-confidence functional hypotheses supported by at least 3 evidence types, and 85/100 have module-based predictions. Scores range from 0.624 to 0.715, and 97/100 have domain annotations. The report attributes MR-1's 25/100 top candidates to its deep condition coverage (121 conditions) rather than to inherent biology. It also describes MR-1's dominance of the top candidates (25/100) as consistent with MR-1's position as a model organism with extensive condition coverage and a large hypothetical-gene complement. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The highest-ranked fitness-active candidate was *P. putida* N2C3 AO356_11255, with |fit| = 3.4 under nitrogen conditions, a D-alanyl-D-alanine carboxypeptidase prediction, an EamA domain, and a lab–field odds ratio of 44. The next candidates included MR-1 202463 with |fit| = 6.4 under stress and a YGGT domain, and the MR-1 K03306 paralog trio 199738, 203545, and 202450, with nitrogen-associated fitness effects of 5.5, 4.0, and 3.9 respectively. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The report asked which organism × condition experiments would give the highest return for characterizing dark genes, given that RB-TnSeq (randomly barcoded transposon sequencing) libraries already exist for the Fitness Browser organisms. It ranked all organism–condition pairs by the number of top-500 dark-gene candidates they would address and then applied greedy set-cover optimization. This analysis found that 10 organism–condition experiments would address 242 of the top 500 dark genes, a share the report gives as 45.3%. The first three MR-1 experiments (stress, nitrogen source and carbon source) would address 111 top-500 candidates, which the report gives as 20.8%. These reported percentages do not match the stated top-500 denominator, and the report does not explain the difference, so the counts and percentages are reproduced here as reported. The table's intermediate cumulative percentages, such as 10.7% for the first 57 genes, likewise do not match a denominator of 500. The report lists the full 10-experiment sequence as MR-1 stress, MR-1 nitrogen source, MR-1 carbon source, *P. putida* N2C3 stress, *Sinorhizobium meliloti* carbon source, *S. meliloti* stress, *P. stutzeri* RCH2 stress, Marinobacter stress, *P. fluorescens* GW456-L13 carbon source, and *P. putida* carbon source. The report calls MR-1 the single highest-value organism for dark-gene characterization, attributing this to deep condition coverage (121 conditions historically), a large dark-gene complement (587 scored) and high fitness-effect magnitudes (142 genes with |fit| ≥ 4). All coverage figures are projections for experiments not yet run. [src: functional_dark_matter]

Essential dark genes require a separate strategy because standard RB-TnSeq, randomly barcoded transposon sequencing, yields no viable knockout fitness profiles for them. ### Experimental campaign: two prioritization routes

The report distills the analysis into an experimental campaign with two complementary prioritization routes. One is optimized for genes with the strongest existing evidence, the other for maximizing discovery of completely unknown functions, and the report says both converge on a tractable set of organisms. Route A, evidence-weighted prioritization (NB05–NB09), scores each dark gene across 6 dimensions using data from the 48 Fitness Browser organisms: fitness importance 0.25, conservation 0.20, inference 0.20, pangenome 0.15, biogeographic 0.10 and tractability 0.10. It identifies genes where multiple evidence lines converge on a testable hypothesis, and is intended for targeted experiments. Route B, conservation-weighted prioritization (NB11, NB11b), queries the full 27,690-species GTDB r214 pangenome to measure the taxonomic breadth of each dark-gene ortholog group (species range 1–27,482). It classifies each group by taxonomic tier (kingdom through species) and hypothesis status (strong hypothesis, weak lead, true knowledge gap), then ranks by importance = conservation × ignorance. It is intended for discovery experiments on broadly conserved genes where the least is known. [src: functional_dark_matter]

In the report's route comparison, Route A's top organism is MR-1 (deep condition coverage, many converging evidence lines). Route B's is *S. meliloti* (1,630 OGs, 195 kingdom-level gaps). Each route selects 42 organisms (28 genera); Route A covers 95% of composite priority and Route B 95.6% of importance-weighted priority. The routes share 39 organisms but produce different orderings reflecting different experimental strategies. As a result, kingdom-level ortholog groups (55.9% of dark-gene ortholog groups, present across multiple phyla) cannot be experimentally addressed in their non-Proteobacterial hosts using Fitness Browser organisms alone. These phylum counts conflict with the report's taxon-coverage table, which lists 1 Firmicutes organism (the Fitness Browser strain BFirm) and 5 Bacteroidetes organisms; the report does not reconcile them. [src: functional_dark_matter]

To address this gap, the report curated an extended tractable-organism list of 73 organisms: 48 Fitness Browser organisms plus 25 literature-curated organisms with published TnSeq or CRISPRi resources (`data/extended_tractable_organisms.tsv`). The 25 non-Fitness-Browser organisms fill specific phylogenetic gaps. The *C. difficile* strain listed here is R20291, whereas the proposed new RB-TnSeq library above is *C. difficile* 630; both are cited to Dembek et al. 2015. The additions are: [src: functional_dark_matter]
- Bacillota (6): *B. subtilis* 168, *S. aureus* USA300, *S. pneumoniae* TIGR4, *C. difficile* R20291, *E. faecium* and *L. monocytogenes* EGD-e; TnSeq + CRISPRi; Koo et al. 2017, Bae et al. 2004, van Opijnen et al. 2009, Dembek et al. 2015.
- Actinomycetota (2): *M. tuberculosis* H37Rv and *C. glutamicum* ATCC 13032; TnSeq + CRISPRi; DeJesus et al. 2017, Cleto et al. 2016.
- Campylobacterota (2): *C. jejuni* NCTC 11168 and *H. pylori* 26695; TnSeq; Gao et al. 2014, Salama et al. 2004.
- Pseudomonadota (15): *P. aeruginosa* PAO1, *Salmonella* 14028s, *V. cholerae* N16961, *A. baumannii* 17978 and 11 others; TnSeq/CRISPRi; Turner et al. 2015, Langridge et al. 2009, Chao et al. 2013.

An earlier, NB11b-stage passage presents incorporating these organisms into the Route B covering set as prospective. Doing so would enable experimental characterization of kingdom-level dark-gene ortholog groups in their native Gram-positive, Actinobacterial and Campylobacterial genomic contexts. It would require a Spark query mapping each non-Fitness-Browser organism's genome to dark-gene root ortholog groups via the pangenome, which the report calls a natural extension of the NB11b pipeline. The report elsewhere describes a Spark query of genus-level ortholog-group membership for the 25 non-Fitness-Browser organisms and an extended covering set built from it (see above). This passage does not by itself establish the final covering-set status. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The action plan sorts the covering-set genes into three experiment classes, all proposals rather than completed tests. 14,450 hypothesis-bearing genes across all 42 organisms have at least one condition recommendation from fitness data, module co-regulation or gene-neighborhood context. For these the experiment is targeted RB-TnSeq or CRISPRi under the predicted condition. Examples are MR-1:202463 (YGGT domain, stress-responsive) under oxidative, osmotic and heat stress, and the K03306 paralog trio (199738/203545/202450) under nitrogen limitation with single and double mutant comparisons. For 2,038 darkest genes across all 42 organisms, the report finds no converging evidence to suggest a specific condition. These are T1 Void or T2 Twilight genes with detectable fitness phenotypes, and they call for a broad phenotypic screen: RB-TnSeq across a diverse condition panel (the standard Fitness Browser protocol of ~50-100 conditions). For *E. coli* K-12 alone, 109 genes fall in this category. The 8,900 essential genes in the covering set cannot be studied by transposon knockout. The recommended approach is CRISPRi knockdown (Mobile-CRISPRi for non-model organisms, Peters et al. 2019) with growth curves under standard and stress conditions. The top 50 essential candidates have specific CRISPRi experiment designs in NB07. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The report's targeted campaign (Route A) proposes three first experiments, none yet run:
- An MR-1 stress screen: RB-TnSeq under 5 stress conditions (oxidative, osmotic, metal, heat, pH), addressing 161 hypothesis-bearing dark genes, including the #2 overall candidate 202463 (fit=6.4 under stress).
- An MR-1 nitrogen screen: RB-TnSeq under nitrogen limitation and amino acid supplements, addressing the K03306 paralog family (3 genes, fits 3.9–5.5) and 74 total dark genes.
- An *E. coli* K-12 CRISPRi screen: knockdown of the top 20 essential dark genes with growth curves, using the highest-tractability organism (0.9) and mature CRISPRi tools. The top target is Keio:14796 (YbeY domain, score 0.875).
The 161 stress and 74 nitrogen counts differ from the 57 stress and 31 nitrogen top-500 genes given for the MR-1 set-cover screens above. They appear to count different gene sets, and the report does not reconcile them. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The report's discovery campaign (Route B) is meant for labs focused on novel function discovery rather than hypothesis testing. It starts with *S. meliloti* (195 kingdom-level gaps), *P. putida* (172 kingdom gaps) and MR-1 (105 kingdom gaps). It recommends broad phenotypic screens under diverse condition panels, because the target genes are true knowledge gaps without condition predictions. The report says this ordering maximizes the probability of uncovering completely new biology; that benefit is prospective, not demonstrated. [src: functional_dark_matter]

### Hypothesis assessment

The report assesses its central hypothesis H1 as partially supported and H0 as partially rejected. Dark genes with strong fitness effects are not randomly distributed but show non-random patterns across multiple evidence dimensions. Among these patterns, 6,142 dark genes co-regulate with annotated genes in independent-component-analysis (ICA) modules, and 85/100 top candidates have module-based function predictions. [src: functional_dark_matter]

H1b, the conservation signal, was not supported. It predicted that stress-condition dark genes would be more accessory than carbon/nitrogen dark genes. A formal Fisher's exact test on n=7,491 dark genes (NB06) found a significant association in the opposite direction: stress-condition dark genes were 23.0% accessory versus 25.5% for carbon/nitrogen dark genes (OR=1.15, p=0.013), so the predicted direction was rejected. The report reads this as suggesting that the relationship between condition specificity and pangenome conservation is more complex than a simple stress=accessory, metabolism=core dichotomy. The formal test of stress versus carbon/nitrogen accessory rates is plotted in `fig16_h1b_test.png`. [src: functional_dark_matter]

A matched comparison of cross-organism concordance for 65 dark versus 490 annotated ortholog groups (NB06) found no significant difference (Mann-Whitney p=0.17, KS p=1.0). Dark genes with orthologs in 3+ organisms reached concordance levels indistinguishable from annotated genes (dark median=1.0, annotated median=1.0; dark mean=0.976, annotated mean=0.985). The report says this null control, in which dark genes achieve concordance indistinguishable from annotated genes, supports H1. Dark-versus-annotated concordance distributions are plotted in `fig17_concordance_comparison.png`. [src: functional_dark_matter]

For the environmental hypothesis, the report cites 10/137 clusters with significant environmental enrichment and an overall concordance rate of 61.7%, which it says exceeds the 50% chance level. The binomial test, however, is not significant at 0.05 (p = 0.072), although Fisher's combined p = 0.031 is. NMDC independent validation confirmed all 4 testable pre-registered predictions (nitrogen~nitrogen, pH~pH, anaerobic~dissolved oxygen). The strongest within-species signals are in *Pseudomonas* and *P. syringae*, and the NMDC correlations provide community-level corroboration across 6,365 metagenomic samples. [src: functional_dark_matter]

### Literature context

The report places its approach in a line of fitness-based function prediction. Deutschbauer et al. (2011) pioneered genome-wide fitness profiling for function prediction in Shewanella MR-1, using 121 conditions to propose functions for 40 previously hypothetical genes; this project extends that approach to 48 organisms and 7,552 conditions using the Fitness Browser resource (Price et al. 2018). The report also cites Vaccaro et al. (2016), who showed that fitness profiling in *Pseudomonas stutzeri* RCH2 could identify novel metal-resistance genes among hypotheticals, and says its own finding of stress-responsive dark genes in *Pseudomonas* species corroborates that pattern. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The report also positions its work against recent large-scale dark-proteome studies. It cites Pavlopoulos et al. (2023, *Nature*), who identified 106,198 novel protein families from 26,931 metagenomes, doubling the number of known protein families, and Zhang et al. (2025, *Nature Biotechnology*), whose FUGAsseM method predicts high-confidence functions for >443,000 protein families from microbial communities using metatranscriptomic coexpression. By contrast, the report asserts that every gene in its catalog has measured fitness phenotypes from controlled lab experiments and that the top candidates come with specific experimental protocols. That assertion is in tension with the report's own statement that essential dark genes have zero `genefitness` rows and no fitness magnitudes beyond the essentiality call; the report does not reconcile the two. Its first listed contribution is a unified dark-gene catalog (57,011 genes across 48 bacteria) integrating fitness, conservation, module, ortholog and domain data from 4 prior observatory projects, which had previously been fragmented across separate analyses. It also describes its lab–field concordance approach, testing whether lab fitness conditions predict the field environments of gene carriers, as, to the authors' knowledge, novel in its systematic application across multiple organisms and condition classes. [src: functional_dark_matter]

## Caveats and Limitations

Environmental metadata are sparse: AlphaEarth embeddings cover only 28% of genomes (83K/293K), and NCBI isolation-source metadata are inconsistent, limiting the power of biogeographic tests. NMDC validation is genus-level (mapping NMDC taxon columns to pangenome genera via ncbi_taxid) and may miss species-specific dark-gene signals, only 5 of 6 carrier genera were matched, and common genera such as *Pseudomonas*, *Klebsiella*, and *Bacteroides* may generate broad correlations with abiotic variables independent of specific dark-gene functions; the report says the high significance rate (76/105) likely reflects the dominance of such common genera in both datasets. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The 57,011 dark-gene count likely overestimates true functional darkness because annotations may exist in databases or releases not checked, such as UniProt, InterPro or recent NCBI updates. Module predictions, which cover 6,142 dark genes in the integrated census and are reported as 6,691 in a later project-specific accounting, are guilt-by-association inferences rather than direct experimental validation; the "high confidence" label in prioritization reflects evidence convergence, not experimental proof. [src: functional_dark_matter]

Fitness Browser condition coverage is uneven, and MR-1 has 121 historical conditions; organisms with deeper condition coverage can therefore produce more specific phenotypes and receive higher prioritization scores. The GapMind analysis is limited to amino-acid biosynthesis and carbon-utilization pathways, identifies organism-level co-occurrence rather than direct gene-to-step assignments, and does not address signaling, regulation, or structural functions comprehensively. Curated pathway–enzyme domain matching (EC prefix, Pfam family and keyword compatibility) partially addresses the gene-to-step gap, identifying 42,239 domain-compatible gene–pathway candidates including 5,398 high-confidence EC matches, but full gene-to-step validation would require AlphaFold structure prediction or experimental enzymology. [src: functional_dark_matter]

Essential genes are penalized in fitness-centric scoring because they lack viable transposon mutants, no `genefitness` rows, and no differential fitness magnitudes; the separate essential-gene prioritization partly corrects this bias but relies on neighborhood and domain inference. None appear in the NB05 top 100 because dimension 1 (fitness importance) scores them at the essentiality bonus floor and dimension 6 (experimental tractability) penalizes them for being non-knockable. The neighborhood heuristic uses a five-gene window, same-strand orientation, and gaps of no more than 300 bp, whereas tools such as DOOR, STRING, and EFI-GNT use broader taxonomic and multimodal evidence. Cross-species synteny validation across 48 Fitness Browser genomes and co-fitness confirmation add to the positional heuristic, but the analysis still lacks gene-fusion detection, regulatory-element analysis and broad taxonomic sampling. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The NMDC trait correlations are vulnerable to compositional coupling: 441/449 exploratory tests were significant despite only 7/7 pre-registered trait directions being the meaningful directional metric. A full sample-label permutation test remains future work requiring Spark access to raw per-sample matrices. Partial null approximations instead give a sign test of p = 0.0078 for the 7/7 pre-registered trait directions and p = 0.0625 for the 4/4 pre-registered abiotic directions, which the report says confirms non-random directionality; Fisher's combined probability across all 11 pre-registered tests of p ≈ 0; and an inflation factor of ~20× for exploratory tests (441/449 significant versus 22.5 expected), which quantifies the compositional coupling. The report adds that these NMDC correlations demonstrate ecological co-occurrence rather than causal gene–phenotype links. Likewise, the 29/47 lab–field concordance rate has a one-sided binomial p = 0.072, and equivalent annotated-accessory-gene controls were not run through the complete biogeographic pipeline. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The prioritization weights are expert-assigned and exact ranks are sensitive to them. Overall rank correlations remained ρ > 0.93 across six alternative configurations, but only 32/50 (64%) of the original fitness-active top 50 remained under conservation-dominant or drop-tractability settings, while essential-gene top-50 retention was 18/50 (36%) when tractability was dropped and 24/50 (48%) when neighbor context was dropped. Across all 6 fitness-active weight configurations, 18 genes remain in the top 50 and 35 in the top 100 regardless of weight choice; for essential genes, 6 remain always-top-50 and 19 always-top-100. The report calls these always-top candidates the most defensible targets for experimental follow-up. Robust rank indicators, namely rank-range distributions, a min/max rank scatter and always-top-50 candidates by organism, are plotted in `fig29_robust_ranks.png`. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The Fitness Browser’s 77% Pseudomonadota composition (37/48 organisms) limits cross-phylum inference. The extended covering set, which incorporates 25 non-Fitness-Browser organisms from Bacillota, Actinomycetota and Campylobacterota, improves coverage from 4 to 6 phyla, but non-Fitness-Browser ortholog-group coverage is estimated at the genus level and may overestimate individual-organism coverage; these organisms also lack Fitness Browser condition profiles and therefore support broad screens rather than condition-specific experiments. Bacillota organisms (*B. subtilis*, *S. aureus*) were not selected by the covering-set algorithm because their ortholog groups are subsets of coverage provided by Pseudomonadota, despite their value for studying genes in native Gram-positive contexts. [src: functional_dark_matter]

## Future Directions

The report proposes a dual-route experimental campaign, none of it yet run. Route A (NB09, evidence-weighted) identifies 42 organisms covering 95% of composite priority and would start with MR-1 stress/nitrogen screens and *E. coli* CRISPRi for targeted hypothesis testing. Route B (NB11, conservation-weighted) identifies 42 organisms covering 95.6% of importance-weighted priority and would start with *S. meliloti*, *P. putida* and MR-1 broad phenotypic screens for novel function discovery. The two routes share 39 organisms; the report calls them complementary, with Route A producing mechanistic insights for genes with hypotheses and Route B discovering functions for broadly conserved true knowledge gaps. These priority percentages use different denominators from the 98.7% ortholog-group coverage of the extended covering set. [src: functional_dark_matter]

For targeted validation, the report says the top 5 fitness-active candidates (4 in MR-1, 1 in *P. putida* N2C3) are immediately testable via RB-TnSeq under their predicted condition classes. The specific targets are AO356_11255 (D-alanyl-D-alanine carboxypeptidase prediction, EamA domain), to be tested under nitrogen limitation; MR-1 202463 (YGGT domain, T5 Dawn, composite score 0.698), to be tested under multiple stress conditions; and the MR-1 K03306 paralog family 199738/203545/202450, to be tested under nitrogen limitation with single/double mutant comparisons. The functional labels are predictions to be tested. [src: functional_dark_matter]

For CRISPRi validation of essential dark genes, the top 50 essential candidates (NB07) include specific CRISPRi experiment designs. The highest-priority targets are Keio:14796 (YbeY domain, score 0.875, predicted ion transport), MR-1:200382 (RimP_N/DUF150_C, score 0.874, predicted ribosome assembly) and Koxy:BWI76_RS08540 (OmpA/TIGR02802, score 0.865, predicted cell division). The covering set assigns 8,900 essential genes to specific organisms for CRISPRi-based approaches. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The report proposes AlphaFold2 structure prediction for the top 100 candidates and especially the 1,853 T5 Dawn genes (nearly characterized), saying it could confirm functional hypotheses and distinguish between DUF families. It says the 4,273 T1 Void genes, which lack all evidence, would particularly benefit from structure-based function prediction as the only remaining computational inference strategy; such predictions would remain computational, not experimental validation. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The NMDC dataset includes proteomics (346K observations) and metabolomics (3.1M observations) that were not used in this project. The report proposes correlating dark-gene carrier abundance with metabolite or protein profiles as potentially more direct functional evidence than abiotic correlations alone; this is a proposed integration, not a reported result. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The report's future-directions list describes expanding the covering set beyond Fitness Browser organisms as still pending: the current covering sets draw only from 48 Fitness Browser organisms, which are 77% Pseudomonadota, and the extended tractable-organism list (`data/extended_tractable_organisms.tsv`) curates 25 additional organisms with published TnSeq/CRISPRi resources from Bacillota (6), Actinomycetota (2), Campylobacterota (2) and additional Pseudomonadota (15). Incorporating them into Route B's conservation-weighted covering set would enable experimental characterization of kingdom-level OGs (55.9% of dark gene OGs) in their native Gram-positive, Actinobacterial and Campylobacterial contexts, and the passage says this requires a pangenome Spark query mapping each organism's genes to dark-gene root_ogs, an extension of the NB11b pipeline. This conflicts with the report's own output inventory, which lists the genus-level query and an extended covering set (`11c_extended_covering_set.ipynb`, `data/extended_covering_set.tsv`); the report does not reconcile the two. [src: functional_dark_matter]

The report also proposes publishing the darkness-spectrum census (`dark_gene_census_full.tsv`) and the experimental action plan as a community resource for bacterial functional genomics, so other labs could target specific organisms, tiers or condition classes; publication is proposed, not reported as completed. [src: functional_dark_matter]
