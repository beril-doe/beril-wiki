# Conflict: Per-Metal Conservation Deltas and the Claimed Universality of Cross-Metal Fitness Correlation

This page records two disagreements surfaced by [[concepts/shared-stress-versus-stressor-specific-fitness]], and omits none from that input. The first is numerical: the counter-ion analysis and the primary metal fitness atlas report different per-metal core-genome conservation *deltas* — the gap between the fraction of a metal's fitness-important genes that are core (present across the species' genomes, rather than strain-specific accessory content) and the organism's genomic baseline — for the same three metals, after shared-stress genes have been accounted for. It matters because these deltas are the quantitative form of the claim that metal-fitness signal survives correction for general stress response, and because a per-metal ordering that changes between pipelines cannot be used downstream without knowing which pipeline produced it. The second disagreement is interpretive: one project reads positive metal-pair fitness correlations as a universal directional layer of cross-metal response, while another finds that metal-versus-NaCl correlations are strongly heterogeneous. It matters because it decides whether "shared stress response" is a single axis or several. Two caveats govern how much weight either disagreement can bear, and are carried below: the counter-ion analysis itself flags its corrected iron delta as statistically unreliable, and both sources treat manganese as low-powered.

## Evidence Sides

**Side A — counter-ion analysis (post-correction deltas).** After shared-stress treatment, the counter-ion analysis reports manganese at **+0.182**, zinc at **+0.115**, and iron at **+0.182**. [src: counter_ion_effects] The same report marks its corrected iron figure as a sign reversal from the uncorrected value that rests on a very low gene count from a single organism, and states that such corrected deltas (iron and cadmium) are statistically unreliable and should be interpreted with caution; manganese is likewise described as low-powered, from a single organism, with its important genes sitting at the ceiling of fully core. [src: counter_ion_effects]

**Side B — primary metal fitness atlas (deltas).** The primary atlas reports manganese at **+0.198**, zinc at **+0.151**, and iron at **+0.116**. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] The atlas likewise notes that its manganese figure comes from a single organism's small important-gene set in which every important gene is core. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]

The concept page states, citing both projects jointly, that these values should not be reconciled by averaging, because the analyses differ in record sets, organism coverage, and correction procedure, and that matched-data reanalysis is required to determine whether the discrepancy is methodological or biological. [src: counter_ion_effects, metal_fitness_atlas] This is a statement of the concept page, not a documented agreement negotiated between the two projects.

**Side C — metal cross-resistance (universal positive layer).** All tested metal-pair correlations were positive, interpreted as a universal directional layer of cross-metal fitness response. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The word "all" is the report's own universality framing as carried into the tension text; the underlying report states the result as an overwhelming, near-universal majority of organism–metal-pair observations — not literally every observation — with nearly all of them individually significant. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

**Side D — counter-ion analysis (heterogeneous metal–NaCl correlations).** Metal–NaCl correlations vary substantially across metals, including iron at **r=0.086** and zinc at **r=0.715** — Pearson correlations between whole-genome fitness profiles. [src: counter_ion_effects] This hierarchy is derived within a single organism, *Desulfovibrio vulgaris* Hildenborough (DvH), the most extensively metal-profiled organism in that report, against its small set of NaCl experiments; it is not a corpus-wide ranking. [src: counter_ion_effects]

The concept page records that C and D are not a direct contradiction, because the analyses compare different condition pairs; D **refines** C, in that positivity across metals does not establish a uniform response to NaCl or identify which shared stressor generates the signal. [src: metal_cross_resistance, counter_ion_effects]

## Possible Reconciliations

These are hypotheses, not findings.

*Estimand hypothesis (A vs B).* If the counter-ion deltas are computed on the metal-specific gene set remaining after shared-stress genes are removed, and the atlas deltas on the full metal-important set, the two are different estimands and neither is wrong. This hypothesis makes no directional prediction: the counter-ion report's own account is that shared-stress genes are extremely core, so removing them concentrates the analysis on metal-specific genes that are still highly core, producing shifts in both directions with no rule tying the sign of the shift to how shared-stress-heavy a metal is. [src: counter_ion_effects] Consistently, the three metals at issue move in different directions between the two pipelines — zinc and manganese lower in the counter-ion set (+0.151 → +0.115; +0.198 → +0.182), iron higher (+0.116 → +0.182). [src: counter_ion_effects, metal_fitness_atlas]

*Power hypothesis (A vs B).* For iron and manganese the "disagreement" may be an artifact of very small, single-organism gene sets. The counter-ion analysis declares its corrected iron delta statistically unreliable on exactly those grounds, and both sources place manganese's important genes entirely in the core, which means the manganese gap (+0.182 vs +0.198) can only arise from the genomic baseline each pipeline subtracts, not from the metal gene set at all. [src: counter_ion_effects, metal_fitness_atlas] Under this hypothesis only zinc — the metal with broad organism coverage in both analyses — constitutes a substantive numerical conflict.

*Coverage hypothesis (A vs B).* The two analyses differ in organism coverage; per-metal deltas computed over different organism sets can diverge without either being wrong, particularly for metals carried by few organisms. [src: counter_ion_effects, metal_fitness_atlas]

*Scope hypothesis (C vs D).* Metal–metal and metal–NaCl correlations may both be positive on average while differing in spread: a positive floor across metal pairs, measured across many organisms, is compatible with a wide, mechanism-dependent range against a non-metal stressor measured in one organism. Under this hypothesis C describes a floor and D describes variance around it. [src: metal_cross_resistance, counter_ion_effects]

*Mechanism-partition hypothesis (C vs D).* "Shared stress" may be several distinct axes (envelope damage, cofactor displacement, pathway-specific damage) that a single all-positive summary collapses, with pathway-specific metals sitting low against NaCl and broad-acting metals high. In the input evidence this ordering is observed only within DvH — iron at r=0.086, zinc at r=0.715 — so as a general claim about metals it is an untested extrapolation from one organism. [src: counter_ion_effects]

## Resolving Work

For the per-metal delta discrepancy (A vs B):

- Recompute both delta sets on a single frozen record set and a single organism list, holding the correction step as the only varying factor, and ask whether zinc — the only one of the three metals with broad organism coverage in both pipelines — converges to +0.151 or +0.115. [src: metal_fitness_atlas, counter_ion_effects]
- Publish, per metal, the gene counts and organism identities entering each delta, and test with a leave-organism-out sweep whether the between-analysis difference is predicted by the set difference alone.
- Bootstrap confidence intervals on each per-metal delta in both pipelines, reporting power alongside the estimate, to establish whether the manganese gap (+0.182 vs +0.198) and the iron gap (+0.116 vs +0.182) are discrepancies at all rather than noise around gene sets the counter-ion report already labels low-powered or unreliable. [src: counter_ion_effects]
- Decompose the manganese gap into its two components — metal-gene core fraction and genomic baseline — to confirm whether, as both sources' descriptions imply, the entire difference sits in the baseline. [src: counter_ion_effects, metal_fitness_atlas]
- Before any reanalysis of iron, expand iron's organism coverage: with the corrected iron delta resting on a single organism's very small gene set and flagged unreliable by its own authors, adding iron-stress fitness data from additional organisms is the prerequisite for treating +0.182 vs +0.116 as a real disagreement. [src: counter_ion_effects, metal_fitness_atlas]

For the universality-of-positivity claim (C vs D):

- Compute metal–metal and metal–NaCl correlations in the same organisms on the same fitness matrices, and report full distributions rather than signs — asking whether the metal-pair distribution is narrower than a metal–NaCl distribution spanning r=0.086 to r=0.715. [src: counter_ion_effects, metal_cross_resistance]
- Replicate the DvH metal–NaCl hierarchy in additional organisms with sufficient NaCl experiments, asking whether the ranking is reproducible or organism-specific; the mechanism-partition hypothesis is not testable until it is. [src: counter_ion_effects]
- Test the mechanism-partition hypothesis by grouping metals a priori by damage mode and asking whether group membership, rather than metal identity, predicts the metal–NaCl correlation. [src: counter_ion_effects]
- Restate the cross-resistance claim at the granularity its data support — the proportion of organism–metal-pair observations that are positive and significant — and check whether the negative-or-null minority concentrates in particular metals or organisms. [src: metal_cross_resistance]
- Add a second non-metal stressor as a control axis: if positivity is universal across metal pairs but stressor-dependent against non-metals, the "universal directional layer" claim must be restated as a within-metal claim. [src: metal_cross_resistance]
