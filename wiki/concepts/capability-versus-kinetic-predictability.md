# Genomic Capability Is More Predictable Than Continuous Growth Kinetics

The study distinguishes two genotype-to-phenotype tasks: predicting whether a strain can grow under a condition, and predicting continuous kinetic properties such as maximum growth rate, lag, or yield. [[summaries/genotype_to_phenotype_enigma__REPORT]] shows that genome content provides useful but condition-dependent information for the first task, whereas it provides little cross-genus predictive power for the second. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

## Core distinction

Binary growth capability asks whether genomic content supports growth on or under a specified condition, while continuous kinetics asks how rapidly, how long after inoculation, or to what yield the organism grows. The distinction is central to [[concepts/condition-specific-fitness]] and to the definition of the prediction task in [[concepts/gene-essentiality]]. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

The biological interpretation is that gene content can encode the presence of transporters, catabolic enzymes, biosynthetic pathways, and regulators needed to use a substrate, while growth rate also depends on enzyme kinetics, expression levels, regulatory context, and ribosome efficiency. The report therefore treats capability prediction as more directly encoded by comparative genome features than continuous kinetic prediction, framing the split as whether an organism can use a substrate versus how fast it uses it. It names codon usage bias (CUB) — the skew in synonymous codon choice, used here as a proxy for ribosomal efficiency — as the natural next feature for growth-rate prediction, while noting that CUB requires nucleotide sequences that were not accessible from the project's JupyterHub environment, so expression data would be the alternative route. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

The [[summaries/pathway_capability_dependency__REPORT]] evidence **refines** this distinction by separating genomic capability from experimentally observed dependency: across 161 organism–pathway pairs, complete pathways could be either fitness-important or apparently dispensable under aggregate laboratory conditions. All 66 pairs classified as Latent Capability nevertheless became fitness-important under at least one condition type, although the median-based reclassification threshold can produce this result by construction. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] A broader analysis of 1,695 complete pathway–organism pairs similarly classified 267 (15.8%) as latent, 547 (32.3%) as intermediate, and 881 (51.9%) as active; carbon-source pathways were more often latent than amino-acid biosynthesis pathways, at 24.3% versus 6.5%. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency] Thus, genomic capability is not equivalent to either growth in a particular assay or permanent gene dispensability. [src: pathway_capability_dependency]

## Evidence from the ENIGMA growth corpus

The integrated corpus contained 27,632 growth curves from 123 strains, 195 molecules, and 303 plates, representing 7.57M timepoints. Of the 27,632 wells across 303 plates, 15,227 (55.1%) showed no detectable growth — described as a biological signal reflecting substrate incompatibility or stress lethality rather than a measurement failure — and 9,861 (35.7%) were fit-ok, with median modified Gompertz R² = 0.98, median µmax = 0.028 h⁻¹ (reported as a doubling time of ~24.5 h), median lag = 11.4 h, and median asymptotic OD increase A = 0.315. Diauxy is common: 40% of the fit-ok curves show at least two growth phases in their smoothed derivative, which the report reads as suggesting — not demonstrating — that sequential substrate utilization is widespread among Oak Ridge isolates. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

That additional curve structure illustrates why a binary growth label and a kinetic parameter are different phenotype resolutions. Curve-level quality control qualifies the distributions further: the Gompertz fitting pass rate was 35.7%, the 55% no-growth fraction was classified as biological, and approximately 9% of curves failed fitting for technical reasons including monotone violations, short duration, and edge-well effects. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

## Capability prediction is conditional, not universal

The initial seven-strain model achieved AUC 0.633 for binary growth under leave-one-strain-out validation, where AUC means area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve. Its features included 4,305 prevalence-filtered KO features, 23 COG classes, condition class, concentration, phylogeny, and bulk genome features. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

That preliminary design comprised four Pseudomonas_E strains and one each of Cupriavidus, Acidovorax, and [[entities/pedobacter]], so leave-one-strain-out validation over 7 strains did not test cross-genus generalization, did not analyze per-condition prediction quality, and used no condition-specific features; the report describes the resulting model as effectively learning that "big genomes grow on amino acids" — biologically real, but not the condition-specific prediction needed for practical use. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

In the full corpus, [[entities/lightgbm]] gradient-boosted decision-tree modeling used 46,389 genome × condition pairs — 13,632 ENIGMA plus 32,757 Carbon Source Phenotypes pairs — drawn from 727 genomes, with genus-blocked holdout for each of 106 genera having ≥50 pairs, and 4,293 shared [[entities/kegg]] orthologs. Overall binary-growth AUC was 0.620 under that holdout, the most stringent available test of cross-genus generalization because the model cannot use phylogenetic signal from the held-out genus. The same passage records that cross-genus growth-predictive KOs and within-genus metabolite-production-associated KOs are different feature sets (Spearman rho=0.043), answering different biological questions. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

Performance varied by condition class: amino acids reached AUC 0.775 across 7,765 pairs, nucleosides reached 0.780 across 829 pairs, carbon sources reached 0.695 across 8,965 pairs, other conditions reached 0.654 across 24,590 pairs, antibiotics reached 0.619 across 238 pairs, metals reached 0.605 across 232 pairs, and nitrogen reached 0.435 across 152 pairs, which the report calls worse than random. The metals value is qualified in the source as trivially "predicted" given a 98% growth rate in that class. The report's headline statement is that binary growth is predictable on amino acids (AUC 0.775, tryptophan 0.933) and nucleosides (0.780) from KO presence/absence, moderately predictable on carbon sources (0.695), and not predictable on metals, antibiotics, or nitrogen from KO content alone, with 95 of 343 tested conditions achieving AUC > 0.75; it summarizes the full-corpus result across 727 genomes as amino-acid and nucleoside binary growth predictable at AUC 0.78 while growth rate is not. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

Adding [[entities/kegg]]-pathway-based KO × condition interaction features increased mean AUC from 0.620 to 0.653, which the report states as an improvement of +0.032, and improved performance in 80 of 106 held-out genera, most strongly for Microbacterium (+0.088) and Sphingomonas (+0.074). The stated increment and the two quoted AUC endpoints should be read as the source gives them rather than reconciled against one another. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

Per-condition evaluation was restricted to 343 conditions with at least 30 predictions and both growth classes. Of these, 95 achieved AUC > 0.75; the best individual conditions were tryptophan at 0.933, phenylalanine at 0.932, valine at 0.927, mannose at 0.904, and galactose at 0.895, while the worst were turanose at 0.059 and adonitol at 0.010, described as complex sugars with rare catabolic pathways. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

The pathway-capability analysis **supports** the interpretation that capability is condition-dependent rather than universal: its 66 aggregate Latent Capability pairs all showed fitness importance under at least one condition type, with nitrogen limitation, stress, and carbon limitation the most frequent triggers. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] However, this does not by itself validate each reclassification because the condition-specific median importance threshold can cause pathways to cross the threshold by construction. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] The newer analysis **refines** this result by showing that the latent fraction remained qualitatively non-trivial across 16 threshold combinations, ranging from 4.7% to 21.1%, while also identifying a large intermediate class. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency]

Capability prediction was also constrained by annotation and training coverage. GapMind achieved AUC 0.646, 78.8% accuracy, and 24.3% coverage on 118 testable pairs, with 96.5% recall and 79% precision. Matched-condition Carbon Source Phenotypes transfer achieved AUC 0.800 and 76.8% accuracy at 23% coverage, while internal five-fold Carbon Source Phenotypes validation achieved AUC 0.858. Approximately 76% of ENIGMA conditions (metals, antibiotics, nitrogen, stress) had **neither** GapMind pathway coverage **nor** Carbon Source Phenotypes training data, and prediction on those conditions fell to approximately AUC 0.63, no better than generic KO features. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

Those four model rows are not directly comparable, because coverage and evaluation settings differ: the ENIGMA-only gradient-boosted model reached AUC 0.633 at 100% coverage with generic KO features at n=7; GapMind reached AUC 0.646 with 78.8% accuracy at 24.3% coverage by testing pathway completeness for amino-acid and carbon conditions; matched Carbon Source Phenotypes transfer reached AUC 0.800 with 76.8% accuracy at 23% coverage, against 0.633 for ENIGMA-only training on the same matched conditions; and internal five-fold Carbon Source Phenotypes validation reached AUC 0.858, described as the achievable ceiling with sufficient data. The report reads the transfer result as support for cross-corpus training, alongside the full-corpus amino-acid AUC of 0.78. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

These results support [[concepts/computational-pathway-prediction-validation]] and [[concepts/environmental-resistome]] as related methodological concerns: a capability prediction can be limited not only by biology but also by whether the relevant pathway and condition are represented in the annotation and training resources. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma] The pathway-capability analysis **refines** this concern: it used SEED subsystem roles as a proxy for GapMind pathway membership, excluded two pathways without matching SEED descriptions, excluded alanine because fewer than 3 SEED-annotated genes met the coverage threshold, and notes that direct GapMind per-step gene assignments would improve precision. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency] Thus, apparent capability or dependency can reflect annotation resolution as well as genotype. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency, genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

## Continuous kinetics did not transfer across genera

Continuous µmax, lag, and yield-related max_A were not predictable under genus-blocked holdout from KO presence/absence or bulk genomic features in either the 46K-pair model or a dedicated bulk-feature regression, producing negative R² in both analyses. The same failure appeared in the preliminary anchor analysis: with n=7 strains, continuous µmax, lag, and max_A targets also showed negative R² and were reported as not predictable cross-strain. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

Growth rate (µmax), lag time, and yield (max_A) showed negative R² under genus-blocked holdout in both the full KO model at 46K pairs and the dedicated bulk-feature regression over genome size, rRNA/tRNA copies, GC%, coding density, KO count, and operons, and the weak univariate associations present in the data did not overcome this: n_unique_KOs correlated with µmax at r = +0.42 and n_tRNA correlated with µmax at r = +0.30, but both relationships were phylogenetically confounded — large-genome genera such as Pseudomonas grow fast, small-genome genera such as Pedobacter grow slowly — and under cross-genus holdout these correlations provided zero predictive power. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

This result refines [[concepts/cross-species-fitness-transferability]] and [[concepts/lab-field-fitness-concordance]] by showing that even when broad genomic features correlate with a kinetic measurement within the analyzed data, those features need not support prediction in held-out genera. The result is consistent with a distinction between conserved capability and context-dependent performance, rather than evidence that kinetics are unrelated to genotype. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma] The metabolic-capability analysis **supports** the same separation: pathway completeness did not distinguish latent capabilities from active dependencies, with mean conservation values of 0.869 and 0.829, respectively, and Mann–Whitney U p = 0.94. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency] This is pathway-level evidence that encoded capability need not predict measured fitness importance, not a direct test of kinetic transfer. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency]

## Measured kinetics carry information that binary and endpoint readouts miss

A consortium-design project measured the kinetic layer directly instead of predicting it from gene content: growth kinetic parameters (µ_max, lag time, carrying capacity, and area under the curve) were extracted from 676,000 fitted curve points across 32 isolates. [src: cf_formulation_design]

Those kinetic parameters were moderately correlated with endpoint optical density (r ≈ 0.40) but not redundant with it, and for the 29 isolates with all three assay types, adding kinetic features to the metabolic overlap model improved prediction to R² = 0.311. [src: cf_formulation_design] This **refines** the capability-versus-kinetics distinction rather than contradicting it: kinetic parameters of the kind that KO content could not predict under genus-blocked holdout [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma] still carry phenotype information beyond binary or endpoint readouts when they are measured directly. [src: cf_formulation_design]

The same data separate speed from timing in a way a binary growth call cannot: commensals beat [[entities/pseudomonas-aeruginosa]] PA14's maximum growth rate on only 13.8% of substrate comparisons, so PA14 is generally the fastest grower, yet commensals start growing before PA14 on 43.1% of comparisons (lag advantage > 0). The 13.8% figure is narrower than a blanket statement that no individual commensal outgrows PA14. [src: cf_formulation_design]

The predictive value of such features should be read cautiously. The multivariate metabolic-competition model fitted R² = 0.274 on the 142-isolate cohort — reported as the first quantification of metabolic competition's predictive power for PA14 inhibition, establishing resource competition as a real but partial mechanism — but five-fold cross-validation yielded CV R² = 0.145 ± 0.142, below the training R² of 0.274, indicating the multivariate model overfits that cohort. The in-sample value therefore quantifies association, not proof that resource depletion causes inhibition. [src: cf_formulation_design]

## Codon usage bias is a confounded substitute for measured kinetics

The [[entities/pseudomonas-aeruginosa]] genome (6.58 Mb, 6,177 CDS) is 2.5–3× larger than the commensal species in that study (2.1–2.7 Mb, 2,200–3,000 CDS), yet the measured growth curves show PA14 outgrowing most commensals on its preferred amino acid substrates, which the report reads as catabolic enzyme efficiency rather than ribosomal translation rate driving the competitive advantage. Codon-usage-bias analysis of ribosomal proteins showed variation across those species, but the cross-species comparison was confounded by GC content ranging from 31% for [[entities/gemella-sanguinis]] to 73% for [[entities/micrococcus-luteus]]: organisms with extreme GC composition have inflated CUB scores regardless of growth optimization. The report concludes that the CUB analysis cannot reliably distinguish growth-rate differences between these species, and treats the laboratory growth data as the definitive, direct measure of competitive dynamics on amino acid substrates. [src: cf_formulation_design]

The cross-project pitfalls digest generalizes the problem: cross-species CUB comparisons are confounded by GC-content variation (31–73%), CUB is only meaningful within species or across species with similar GC content, and the recommended fix is to use lab growth data as ground truth for cross-species growth-rate comparisons while keeping CUB for within-species contrasts such as comparing PA strain groups. [src: pitfalls]

Taken together, this **weakens** the proposal that codon usage bias will close the kinetic-prediction gap: the feature the ENIGMA analysis proposed but could not compute [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma] is, where it has been computed elsewhere in this corpus, uninterpretable across species of differing GC content. The surviving version of the proposal is a hypothesis restricted to within-species or GC-matched comparisons. [src: cf_formulation_design, pitfalls]

## Mechanistic flux predictions also track growth rate only weakly in one organism

Constraint-based modeling does not rescue the kinetic layer in the one organism where it was tested here. In [[entities/acinetobacter-baylyi-adp1]], per-condition [[entities/flux-balance-analysis]] (FBA — linear-programming prediction of steady-state reaction fluxes through a genome-scale metabolic model) flux predictions showed weak [[entities/spearman-correlation]] coefficients with measured deletion-mutant growth rates across six matched carbon sources, with n counting genes: [[entities/glucose]] -0.021 (n = 387 genes, p = 0.677, ns, no correlation), [[entities/acetate]] -0.153 (n = 352, p = 0.004, weak negative), [[entities/asparagine]] -0.257 (n = 286, p < 0.001, the strongest), [[entities/butanediol]] -0.145 (n = 137, p = 0.092, ns, weak trend), [[entities/glucarate]] +0.246 (n = 127, p = 0.005, positive), and [[entities/lactate]] -0.160 (n = 135, p = 0.065, ns, weak trend). The asparagine coefficient is negative, the direction expected if higher FBA flux through a gene means greater growth impact when that gene is deleted, whereas the positive glucarate coefficient is opposite to prediction and is read as condition-specific model inaccuracy. [src: adp1_triple_essentiality]

The same report separately recommends, for model refinement, using continuous flux values instead of binary essentiality class, with linear or rank regression between flux and growth rate, because continuous flux may capture quantitative growth effects. These results are single-organism deletion-mutant evidence from ADP1: they bound how well this genome-scale model's flux predictions track measured growth in that organism, and do not by themselves establish a general limit on mechanistic kinetic prediction. [src: adp1_triple_essentiality]

## Training scale changes what the model learns

Comparing the seven-strain analysis with the 46K-pair analysis showed a qualitative shift from genome-scale to condition-specific prediction. With seven strains, the model learned a genome-size and condition-class pattern equivalent to "big genomes grow on amino acids"; with 46K pairs, it identified condition-specific catabolic genes — substrate-relevant transporters, catabolic enzymes, and regulators. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

Correlation-grouped SHAP, where SHAP means SHapley Additive exPlanations, was applied to the nested gradient-boosted model fitted on 486 leave-one-strain-out pairs that achieved AUC 0.633 for binary growth with the full feature set; grouping features by |r| > 0.8 connected components, it attributed 25.3% of total importance to a 63-feature genome-scale axis (genome size, gene count, operons, rRNA/tRNA, and co-inherited KOs/COGs) and 45.9% to condition class. Individual KO blocks involving membrane adaptation, tRNA modification, aromatic catabolism, and flagellar motility contributed approximately 2% each but were judged biologically coherent. Comparing attribution between models, importance sat in condition class (45.9%) and genome-scale features (25.3%) with 7 strains, whereas condition-specific catabolic genes emerged at 46K pairs. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

The full-corpus model, trained on 46K pairs with 106 genus-blocked holdouts, identified condition-specific predictors including K03762 (proP, proline/betaine transporter), K10440 (rbsC, ribose transporter), K01857 (pcaB, protocatechuate cycloisomerase for aromatic catabolism), K13633 (ftrA, AraC-family carbon catabolism regulator), and K01214 (treX, isoamylase for complex carbohydrates) — the mechanistically appropriate transporters and catabolic enzymes. In the [[entities/shap]] beeswarm, KO presence pushed predictions toward growth and KO absence toward no-growth, consistent with the expectation that carrying the catabolic gene enables growth on the corresponding substrate. The report concludes that mechanistic gene-specific prediction requires hundreds of genomes per condition rather than a small number of anchor strains. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

The report also treats method choice as sample-size dependent: for cross-genus growth prediction at 46K pairs, multivariate gradient-boosted modeling identified condition-specific features, whereas for within-genus metabolite prediction with n=6 strains, univariate per-metabolite correlation recovered genuine signal that multivariate models missed. Cross-genus growth-predictive KOs and within-genus metabolite-production-associated KOs are described as different feature sets with Spearman rho=0.043 — little rank association, which is not the same as demonstrated non-overlap — answering different biological questions, "can it grow?" versus "what does it produce?", and the report argues gene content explains both when analyzed at the resolution the sample size supports. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

This scale dependence connects the concept to [[concepts/gene-function-acquisition-depth]], [[concepts/fitness-condition-coverage-prioritization-bias]], and [[concepts/condition-space-dimensionality]]: broad genomic proxies dominate when the training design is small, while substrate-relevant features become identifiable when genome and condition coverage expand. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma] The pathway-capability analysis **supports** this emphasis on broader genome sampling: across 2,810 GTDB species, variable pathway count was associated with pangenome openness after controlling for genome count (partial Spearman rho=0.530, p=2.83e-203), while metabolic ecotype count also correlated with openness after that control (partial rho=0.322, p=8.0e-07). [src: pathway_capability_dependency] These associations motivate pathway-level capability models, but do not establish improved kinetic prediction. [src: pathway_capability_dependency]

The same analysis found metabolic clustering in all 10 target species, but environment–cluster associations were significant only for *Salmonella enterica* and *Phenylobacterium* sp.; the absence of explicit phylogenetic correction and the coarse metadata make these ecological interpretations observational. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency] This **refines** the training-scale argument: larger genome collections can reveal metabolic structure, but environmental transfer still depends on metadata quality, taxonomic control, and pathway resolution. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency]

## Implications for experimental design

The asymmetry between capability and kinetics implies that a useful prediction system should report binary capability, kinetic estimates, and uncertainty as separate outputs rather than treating them as interchangeable phenotypes. This separation is a hypothesis motivated by the negative kinetic R² values and the condition-dependent binary AUC results. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

The study audited 42,771 per-pair genus-blocked holdout predictions against ground truth and found 65.1% overall accuracy, 7,844 false positives, and 7,101 false negatives. Among predictions with |p − 0.5| > 0.25, 1,276 high-confidence errors were concentrated in genus × condition-class cells including Methylobacterium on amino acids, Sphingomonas on other (complex) carbon sources, and Microbacterium on nucleosides. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

The report offers three alternative explanations for these confident errors — a missing feature (the relevant gene family absent from the KO ontology or not shared across the genus holdout), a regulatory mismatch (gene present but not expressed under the test condition), or a genuine biological oddity worth investigating — and treats them as hypotheses rather than resolved causes, pairing them with uncertainty-rich regions to build the candidate ranking. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

Active learning ranked 343 conditions using error rate × model uncertainty × field-relevance weight and proposed 50 next experiments. The top conditions included fumaric acid, melibionic acid, fumarate, itaconic acid, 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), hydroxy-glutaric acid γ-lactone, difumarate, L-glutamic acid, nitrate, and pyruvic acid. Prescottella, with 16% growth across tested conditions, and Microbacterium, with 23%, were selected as especially informative genera. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

The metabolic-capability study **supports** prioritizing experiments that distinguish encoded potential from realized dependency: only 48 organisms were fitness-tested among 293,000 genomes with pathway predictions, and laboratory conditions may make environmentally important pathways appear latent. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency]

## Tensions and limits

The results do not establish that genomic capability is intrinsically easy to predict: overall binary-growth AUC was 0.620, nitrogen prediction was AUC 0.435, and approximately 76% of conditions lacked relevant GapMind or Carbon Source Phenotypes coverage. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

The results also do not establish that continuous kinetics are genetically unpredictable in every setting. The negative R² values were obtained under genus-blocked holdout using KO presence/absence and bulk genomic features, and the report proposes adding GC%, codon usage bias, and Morgan molecular fingerprints — but states explicitly that these were planned and **not** computed or used: GC% is only available for 32 of 727 genomes, codon usage bias requires nucleotide sequences not accessible from JupyterHub, and Morgan fingerprints require RDKit. The same report elsewhere lists GC% among the bulk-feature regression inputs; that discrepancy is recorded here rather than reconciled. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]

The pathway-capability result adds a related validation limit rather than resolving these uncertainties: the aggregate Active Dependency/Latent Capability labels were based on a median-based importance threshold, and the analysis recommends independent calibration against known essentials before treating every condition-specific reclassification as biological confirmation. [src: pathway_capability_dependency]

The newer pathway analysis also reports a positive association between latent capability rate and pangenome openness (Spearman ρ = 0.69, p = 0.0004, n = 22 clades), whereas pathway-level conservation did not distinguish latent from active classes. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency] This **refines** the conservation interpretation rather than contradicting the kinetic results: any relationship may operate through clade-level genome dynamics and community context, not direct pathway-level predictability. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency]

A parallel limit appears outside the growth-prediction setting: whether the [[entities/pqqc]] + [[entities/acds]] + [[entities/hcnc]] genomic signature can predict inoculant efficacy remains untested, and the project proposes cross-referencing species carrying that combination with commercially used inoculant strains to assess it. This **supports** the general asymmetry argued here — a genomic marker set can be catalogued long before its quantitative performance consequence is measured. [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology]

## Open Directions

- Use GenBank nucleotide sequences — the files sit on the CGCMS server and were not accessible from JupyterHub — to compute codon usage bias (gRodon S-value or ENC) from CDS sequences and test whether ribosomal efficiency explains the µmax variation KO presence cannot, closing the gap left by the current bulk-feature regressions. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]
- Pair any codon-usage-bias feature with GC-content controls, or restrict it to within-species or GC-matched comparisons, and test directly whether the 31–73% GC spread rather than growth optimization drives the observed CUB variation. [src: cf_formulation_design, pitfalls]
- Regress continuous flux values against measured deletion-mutant growth rate by linear or rank regression, instead of using binary essentiality classes, to test whether a quantitative flux–rate relationship exists where the per-condition correlations were weak and sign-inconsistent. [src: adp1_triple_essentiality]
- Add fitted kinetic parameters (µ_max, lag, carrying capacity, area under the curve) to competition-prediction models on cohorts larger than the 29 isolates with all three assay types, and test whether the R² = 0.311 gain survives cross-validation given the CV R² = 0.145 ± 0.142 seen for the metabolic-overlap model. [src: cf_formulation_design]
- Expand condition canonicalization from normalized names to ChEBI-ID-based matching and test whether increasing the estimated 42 molecular matches to 60–80 improves capability-model training coverage. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]
- Collect hundreds of genomes per condition and compare KO × condition models with pathway-level models to test whether the observed shift from genome-scale proxies to substrate-specific predictors persists at larger sample sizes. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]
- Perform retrospective subsampling that compares the 50 active-learning-ranked additions with random selection, testing whether the proposed design reduces false positives and false negatives more efficiently. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]
- Fit hierarchical or mechanistic kinetic models using growth-curve time series, expression or proteomic measurements, and genomic features to test whether adding regulatory and enzyme-abundance information resolves the negative cross-genus R² values. [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]
- Calibrate the pathway-dependency importance score against independently established essentials, then test whether condition-specific capability labels predict growth and kinetic outcomes without the median-threshold circularity. [src: pathway_capability_dependency]
- Extend pathway-level capability models across the 2,810-species GTDB set and test whether pangenome openness and metabolic ecotype structure improve transfer to held-out genera, while separately evaluating continuous kinetics. [src: pathway_capability_dependency]
- Replace SEED-proxy pathway membership with direct GapMind per-step gene assignments and test whether improved annotation precision changes capability-versus-dependency classification and downstream growth prediction. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency]
- Reanalyze the 22-clade latent-capability/open-pangenome association with explicit organism-to-clade linkage and independent fitness calibration, testing whether the association improves prediction beyond pathway completeness and whether it transfers to kinetic phenotypes. [src: metabolic_capability_dependency]
- Cross-reference species carrying the pqqC + acdS + hcnC combination with commercially used inoculant strains to test whether this genomic signature predicts measured inoculant efficacy. [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology]
