# Genome sampling depth, genome size and phylogeny as confounders of gene-content and pangenome associations

Genome counts per taxon, genome size and phylogenetic structure can inflate or hide associations between gene content or pangenome metrics and ecological traits. In the bacterial CAZyme (carbohydrate-active enzyme) dataset, genome count was a severe confound for pangenome cloud fraction (ρ = 0.913). [src: cazyme_pangenome_ecology] In the metal-ecology projects, the bacterial metal-type effect on niche breadth was not explained away by genome count alone [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology], and it survived genome-size and combined controls in the MicrobeAtlas analysis. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The lanthanide report concludes that H1 is robust to all three phylogenetic-correction frameworks. [src: lanthanide_methylotrophy_atlas] In the PHB (polyhydroxybutyrate) project, by contrast, a niche-breadth association was reduced by 56.3% with sign reversal once genome size was controlled, and the report calls it largely explained by genome size. [src: phb_granule_ecology] The plant-microbiome project adds a third pattern: under within-genus label shuffling only 3 of 15 testable markers survived, while a genome-size and phylum control left 48 of 50 novel orthologous groups (OGs) associated with plants. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] It also finds that 94.2% of tested OGs were associated with plant status, which the report reads as genome-wide taxonomic divergence rather than plant-specific function. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] In the prophage project, genome size was the dominant predictor of prophage burden (rho=0.717), and the report says residual confounding cannot be excluded. [src: prophage_ecology] The reports use covariates, stratification, rarefaction and phylogenetic regression.

## Literature Context

The candidate papers bear on this page only indirectly. None of them tests genome-count, genome-size or phylogeny as confounders of pangenome or gene-content associations. Several do report how taxonomy relates to genome features, and that gives some context for the corpus's controls.

The closest match is a seaweed comparison of 72 bacterial genera across 16 seaweed hosts. It found that taxonomic affiliation strongly influenced GC content, gene number and genome size, and that host association had a lower effect overall [PMID 40454874](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40454874/). A reduced genome size was suggested only in Nereocystis luetkeana-associated microbes [PMID 40454874](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40454874/). The paper also reports a strong taxonomic effect on metabolism, with the clearest host-associated differences in Flavobacteriia [PMID 40454874](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40454874/). Two other papers show how habitat can relate to genome size. In an Amazon study of 69 metagenome-assembled genomes from three forests and three pastures, pasture genomes were larger than forest genomes (4.0 vs 3.1 Mbp) [PMID 40042334](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40042334/). In nectar-dwelling Acinetobacter, genome size was reduced compared with relatives [PMID 39723821](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39723821/). Both findings are correlational and come from small or single-lineage comparisons. The Nocardia pangenome study (263 genomes, 88 species) reports an open pangenome and that 75% of genes are species-specific [PMID 40407329](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40407329/). It does not test sampling depth as a driver of that openness.

The corpus's central claims are consistent with the seaweed result. The plant-microbiome project reads 94.2% of tested orthologous groups being plant-associated as taxonomic divergence rather than plant-specific function. Its within-genus shuffle retained only 3 of 15 markers, and the report treats most marker signals as genus-scale. This matches the seaweed finding that taxonomy outweighs lifestyle [PMID 40454874](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40454874/). The PHB project, with its sign reversal under genome-size control, and the prophage finding that genome size dominates burden both agree with the habitat-genome-size links in the Amazon and nectar papers [PMID 40042334](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40042334/), [PMID 39723821](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39723821/). Those papers also show that genome size can itself be the ecological signal, not just noise. That fits the corpus's mixed picture, in which genome size is a true confounder in some analyses and not in others.

The corpus extends this literature in ways the candidates do not address. These include the genome-count confound of cloud fraction (ρ = 0.913), the log-covariate, rarefaction and combined-model controls on metal niche breadth, and the 48 of 50 orthologous groups that survived a phylum plus genome-size control. The candidates offer no direct support or contradiction for these. They are novel relative to this evidence set, and the open directions on phylogenetic regression and rarefaction remain untested here. None of the candidates is in tension with the corpus.

## Sampling depth and pangenome openness

In the bacterial CAZyme analysis, genome count was a severe confound for cloud fraction (ρ = 0.913), so much apparent openness reflects sampling effort rather than biology. [src: cazyme_pangenome_ecology] After partial correlation controlling for genome size and genome count, the correlation between CAZyme density and cloud fraction dropped to ρ = 0.101. [src: cazyme_pangenome_ecology] The report presents this as a caution for all openness studies using MGnify-scale catalogs. [src: cazyme_pangenome_ecology] It adds that species with few sequenced genomes appear closed regardless of true gene flux. [src: cazyme_pangenome_ecology] See also [[concepts/pangenome-openness-and-its-correlates]].

The pathway_capability_dependency report shows the opposite direction. Genome count is a critical confounder because species with more sequenced genomes may appear to have more variable pathways solely from sampling depth. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] After controlling for genome count, the variable-pathway-count correlation with pangenome openness strengthened to partial Spearman rho=0.530 (p=2.83e-203). [src: pathway_capability_dependency] The report reads this as the raw correlation being partially suppressed by confounding. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] The metabolic ecotype–openness partial correlation stayed significant at rho=0.322 (p=8.0e-07). [src: pathway_capability_dependency] The report cautions that genome-count partial correlations mitigate but do not fully eliminate the sampling bias, since species with more genomes show more pathway variation and more ecotypes. [src: pathway_capability_dependency]

A related effect appears in an unnormalized metal atlas. Species with large open pangenomes (K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa) dominate the scores, reflecting genome size rather than metal biology. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]

## Sampling depth and statistical power

The ecotype reanalysis used all genomes with embeddings, up to 3,505 per species, instead of the original diversity-maximizing downsampling to a maximum of 250. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] It states that larger samples detect weaker correlations. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] It proposes that species with many genomes, often clinical ones, have more power to detect weak correlations. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] It also warns that such species may have inflated correlations and proposes genome count as a covariate. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The gc_ecotype project instead used a stratified subsample of at most 5,000 genomes per species for the large clinical species. [src: gc_ecotype_ecology] In the pathway analysis, small within-genus sample sizes limit power for many groups. [src: pathway_capability_dependency]

## Metal-diversity associations under genome-count and genome-size controls

In the MicrobeAtlas bacterial niche-breadth analysis, genome count tracks the trait. Genome count per genus correlates with inferred metal type diversity (Spearman r = 0.35, p = 2.6×10⁻¹⁹). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Single-genome genera have lower metal type counts than multi-genome genera (Mann-Whitney p = 5.1×10⁻¹⁰). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Despite this, the metal effect was not explained away by genome count alone, and sampling depth made a modest but non-dominant contribution. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

- Linear covariate (R1): with n_species_with_amr in PGLS (phylogenetic generalized least squares, run with gls() + corPagel, not OLS), metal types had β = +0.0204, SE = 0.0057, p = 3.4×10⁻⁴. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] In the simple model, n = 606 genera and BH-FDR (Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate) q = 0.003. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The covariate had β = +0.0093, p = 0.068, which the report reads as a modest, non-dominant sampling effect. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]
- With all three predictors plus n_species, metal types had β = +0.0224, p = 8.3×10⁻⁴, and n_species had p = 0.070. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The multi model had SE = 0.0067 and q = 0.004. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]
- Log covariate (S4): a prior concern was that the linear covariate might be underpowered if the effect is log-linear. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The report added z-scored log1p(n_species_with_amr). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Metal types had β = +0.0195, SE = 0.0057, p = 6.4×10⁻⁴ [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology], with q = 0.004. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Log(n_species) had β = +0.0127, p = 0.016. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]
- The metal effect stayed significant (p = 6.4×10⁻⁴ against 1.5×10⁻⁴ without the covariate). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Log(n_species) became significant where the linear covariate was borderline (p = 0.07). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The report reads this as a log-linear genome-count effect, with well-sampled genera slightly broader-niched, possibly because they include more ecologically diverse isolates. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]
- The metal β dropped only from +0.021 to +0.020, so the report calls it a small confound with independent effects. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Metal types keep a ΔAIC of approximately −10 beyond the log(n_species)-only model. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]
- Rarefaction (R2): sampling one species per genus over 200 iterations gave p < 0.05 in 89.5% of iterations, with median β (+0.015) in the same direction. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Of 606 PGLS genera, 383 had >1 species in the AMR (antimicrobial resistance) database. The other 223 were already singletons and unchanged by rarefaction. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] All 200 of 200 iterations completed, with median β +0.0147, interquartile range +0.0126 – +0.0166, median p 0.0054 and median λ 0.704. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The direction was consistent across all 200 iterations, and 57.5% were Bonferroni-significant at p < 0.0083. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The median β is smaller than the full-data +0.0215. The report calls this expected when multi-species genera are reduced to one representative, and concludes that pangenome sampling depth does not explain the association. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The R2 summary row gives SE ~0.011 and p = 0.005. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]
- Genome size (R5): genus-level mean genome size came from kbase_ke_pangenome.gtdb_metadata through the KBase Data Lakehouse REST (representational state transfer) API, using one representative species per genus. It covered 527/606 genera, and z-scored log(genome_size) was a covariate. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Metal type diversity stayed independent (β = +0.022, p = 3.6×10⁻⁴; ΔAIC = 10.8). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The table gives β = +0.0218, SE = 0.0061, q = 0.003, n = 527 [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology], with λ = 0.629. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Genome size had β = +0.0263, SE = 0.0069, p = 1.5×10⁻⁴. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The genome-size-only baseline had β=+0.029, p=2.7×10⁻⁵, AIC=−630.3, and adding metal types gave AIC=−641.1, ΔAIC = 10.8. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The report concludes genome size is independently positively associated with niche breadth and the metal effect is not a genome-size artefact. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The report takes this to rule out genome size as the primary confounder. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Caveat: 79/606 genera (13%) lacked genome-size data at query time, so n fell from 606 to 527, but direction and significance agreed with the full-data models. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]
- Combined model (R6): the model B_std ~ metal_types_z + log_n_species_z + log_genome_size_z used GTDB r214 species counts and mean genome size in n = 527 genera. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Metal types had β = +0.0218, SE = 0.0061, t = 3.60, p = 3.5×10⁻⁴. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Log(n_species) was not significant (β = +0.0052, p = 0.366) [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology], while log(genome size) had β = +0.0255, p = 2.6×10⁻⁴. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The model had n = 527 and λ = 0.628. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The report concludes metal-type diversity is independently significant (β = +0.022, p = 3.5×10⁻⁴, q = 0.003 BH-FDR). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] It reads the loss of the n_species effect as partial mediation by genome size. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] It concludes the niche-breadth effect is not an artefact of genome complexity or sequencing depth. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

In the comprehensive metal ecology project, genome size is different. It is the only true confounder, reducing the metal-gene β by 46.7% (0.021 → 0.011). [src: comprehensive_metal_ecology] That falls just below the pre-specified 50% threshold, and the association survives (p = 0.006). [src: comprehensive_metal_ecology] The two projects use different models and datasets, so I do not compare their numbers.

## Genome size and niche breadth in PHB species

In the phb_granule_ecology project, AlphaEarth embeddings (64-dimensional vectors capturing environmental context) gave an environmental breadth measure for 2,008 species. [src: phb_granule_ecology] Embedding variance captures within-species environmental variation and serves as a niche-breadth proxy. [src: phb_granule_ecology] PHB-positive species (phaC present) had median embedding variance 0.3295 (n = 531), against 0.2472 (n = 1,477) for PHB-negative species. [src: phb_granule_ecology] The comparison gave Mann-Whitney U = 446,546, p = 1.88 x 10^-6. [src: phb_granule_ecology] The association held when restricted to phyla containing both groups. [src: phb_granule_ecology] But PHB-positive species had larger genomes (median 4.34 Mbp vs 2.44 Mbp, rank-biserial r = -0.592) [src: phb_granule_ecology], and genome size correlated with niche breadth (rho = 0.302, p = 1.5e-43). [src: phb_granule_ecology] PHB+ species have 1.9 Mbp larger genomes on average. [src: phb_granule_ecology] Controlling for genome size changed the Spearman association from rho = 0.106 (p = 1.77e-06) to rho = -0.047 (p = 0.037), a 56.3% reduction with sign reversal. [src: phb_granule_ecology] The report calls the niche-breadth association largely explained by genome size, and genome size rather than PHB the primary correlate. [src: phb_granule_ecology] It concludes H1b is substantially confounded and cannot independently support the feast/famine hypothesis. [src: phb_granule_ecology] It notes that larger genomes encode more metabolic pathways generally, and PHB is one of many pathways enriched in them. [src: phb_granule_ecology]

Environmental enrichment was more robust: PHB stayed enriched in high-variability environments across all four genome-size quartiles (fold enrichment 1.4–4.6x, all p < 1e-11). [src: phb_granule_ecology] The quartile results, as phaC prevalence in high- versus low-variability environments, were:

| Genome-size quartile (Mbp) | High variability | Low variability | Enrichment | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Q1, 0.4–1.8 | 10.7% | 2.5% | 4.4x | 1.18 x 10^-11 [src: phb_granule_ecology] |
| Q2, 1.8–2.5 | 18.5% | 4.0% | 4.6x | 3.25 x 10^-47 [src: phb_granule_ecology] |
| Q3, 2.5–3.7 | 34.3% | 10.9% | 3.1x | 4.17 x 10^-70 [src: phb_granule_ecology] |
| Q4, 3.7–14.0 | 50.9% | 37.4% | 1.4x | 3.21 x 10^-14 [src: phb_granule_ecology] |

The report interprets this as feast/famine selection operating independently of genome size [src: phb_granule_ecology], beyond the general genome-size effect. [src: phb_granule_ecology] It proposes, as a mechanism, that PHB may enable broader niches through general stress protection rather than just carbon buffering [src: phb_granule_ecology], and that PHB may contribute to metabolic flexibility enabling generalism. [src: phb_granule_ecology] It cites Gibbons et al. (2021) for generalists dominating frequently disturbed ecosystems. [src: phb_granule_ecology]

The report frames genome size with literature. It cites Vieira-Silva & Rocha 2010 for a linear scaling of genome size with metabolic enzyme count (r > 0.9) [src: phb_granule_ecology] and Barberán et al. 2014 for genome size predicting habitat breadth in soil bacteria. [src: phb_granule_ecology] It cites Bentkowski et al. 2015 for fluctuating environments selecting larger genomes [src: phb_granule_ecology], and Giovannoni et al. 2005, 2014 for oligotrophic environments selecting streamlined genomes that shed optional pathways including PHB, glycogen and polyphosphate storage. [src: phb_granule_ecology] It proposes a causal chain, environment variability → genome size → metabolic pathway count → PHB presence, which would make PHB presence partly a downstream consequence of genome size. [src: phb_granule_ecology]

Limits: only 28% of genomes (83K/293K) have AlphaEarth embeddings, which may skew the analysis toward better-sampled lineages. [src: phb_granule_ecology] The 2,008 species represent 7.2% of total species diversity. [src: phb_granule_ecology] See also [[entities/alphaearth-environmental-embeddings]].

## Genome size and prophage composition

In the prophage_ecology report, genome size was the strongest of three predictors of bacterial prophage module composition, followed by environment and then host family phylogeny. [src: prophage_ecology] A PERMANOVA (permutational multivariate analysis of variance) on Bray-Curtis distances used a species subsample of n=1,773 and 99 permutations. [src: prophage_ecology] All three predictors were significant at p=0.01, with different F-statistics: genome size quartile 212.99 (4 groups) [src: prophage_ecology], environment 30.04 (10 groups) [src: prophage_ecology], and host family 6.17 (294 groups). [src: prophage_ecology] Genome size correlated with prophage cluster count at rho=0.717. [src: prophage_ecology] See [[entities/permanova]].

The report tried to rule out a genome-size artifact in two ways. Within each genome size quartile, Kruskal-Wallis tests found an environmental effect on prophage module count (all p < 6.5e-78), which it reads as showing the environmental signal is not a genome size artifact. [src: prophage_ecology] Constrained null models with 500 permutations preserved host family × genome size quartile strata across 18,031 species and identified 8 significant module × environment enrichments (FDR < 0.05). [src: prophage_ecology] See also [[concepts/environmental-structuring-of-prophage-gene-modules-beyond-host-phylogeny]].

Limits sit next to these results. Residual genome-size confounding cannot be excluded despite stratification and partial correlations, since larger genomes have more genes of all types. [src: prophage_ecology] The PERMANOVA tested each predictor independently, so the report proposes sequential or partial PERMANOVA to quantify unique environmental variance after controlling phylogeny and genome size together. [src: prophage_ecology] Environment came from NCBI isolation_source metadata for 293,050 genomes, giving 10 categories with ≥30 species. [src: prophage_ecology] That metadata is sparse and inconsistently labeled, and the 10 categories collapse substantial within-category variation. [src: prophage_ecology] The "other_unknown" category holds 9,659 species (35%), which limits statistical power. [src: prophage_ecology] The 200-permutation null model gives adequate but not exhaustive precision for z-score estimation. [src: prophage_ecology] Only 28% of genomes have AlphaEarth environmental embeddings, biasing that subsample toward clinically and environmentally well-sampled lineages. [src: prophage_ecology]

## Genus-level and genome-size control of plant-association markers

In the plant_microbiome_ecotypes report, plant association is heavily confounded with genus, so the choice of control changes how many markers survive. The original NB10 statsmodels logit with genus fixed effects failed to converge for 0/14 markers, because plant association is heavily confounded with genus-level taxonomy. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The research plan specified genus-level fixed effects, but Phase 1 used phylum-level control because of computational cost. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The planned genus-level fixed effects were not executed due to computational constraints. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] A cluster-robust GLM (generalized linear model) with genus clusters, Wald inference and no L1 shrinkage found 8/14 markers significant at q<0.05 BH-FDR. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] By comparison, the NB14 L1-regularized analysis (scikit-learn, saga solver, top-20 genus dummies and log₁₀(genome size)) gave convergent fits, with 9/17 markers having bootstrap 95% CIs excluding zero [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes], and 9/14 in the 14-marker comparison. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The caveat is that L1 bootstrap CIs are biased near zero and n_bootstrap=100 is small. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] 13 of 14 markers agreed between the two models. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The one disagreement was IAA biosynthesis (L1 coef = 0.61 significant; cluster-robust coef = −0.09, p = 0.82). [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The report presents the genus-cluster GLM as the practical analogue of a full PGLMM (phylogenetic generalized linear mixed model). [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes]

Within-genus label shuffling permutes plant labels within each genus, preserves genus-level plant-association rates, and compares observed Fisher odds ratios with the null. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] This strictest species-level null retained 3/15 markers [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes]: nitrogen fixation (perm p < 0.001), ACC deaminase (perm p < 0.001) and T3SS (type III secretion system; perm p = 0.005). [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The other 12 markers failed: phenazine, IAA biosynthesis, CWDE cellulase, CWDE pectinase, effector, DAPG biocontrol, hydrogen cyanide, siderophore, acetoin/butanediol, phosphate solubilization, T4SS and phytotoxin. Their enrichments are consistent with reshuffling labels within genus. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] Two caveats limit the three survivors. The shuffle used n = 200 permutations, which is under-resolved for p ≤ 0.005 and makes T3SS marginal. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] No multiple-testing correction was applied across 15 markers, and Bonferroni α = 0.0033 would demote T3SS while nitrogen fixation and ACC deaminase stay significant. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes]

The combined verdict was 3 species-level robust markers [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes], 5 cassette-level markers (genus-acquired but not species-distinguishing) [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes], and 6 not robust under any test. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The strongest tier is nitrogen fixation, ACC deaminase and T3SS. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The cassette-level tier is phenazine, CWDE cellulase, CWDE pectinase, phosphate solubilization and effector. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The non-robust tier is DAPG biocontrol, T4SS, hydrogen cyanide, IAA biosynthesis, siderophore and acetoin/butanediol. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The report says this breakdown supersedes the earlier "9/17 survive L1" framing. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] It reads most marker-level plant signals as genus-scale rather than species-scale, since marker presence does not reliably separate plant from non-plant species within genera. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] This does not invalidate the H1/H2 compartment and architecture findings. But the 78.7% dual-nature rate and the compartment-specific marker enrichments should be read as genus-level genomic backgrounds, not species-level functional evidence of plant adaptation. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The report regards a full GTDB-tree PGLMM as stronger [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes], and a permulation-style test against the full GTDB tree as the stricter follow-up to its within-genus partition. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] Its 25K × 25K dense covariance matrix is intractable in memory, and sparse approximations or species subsampling are the practical path. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] PGLMMs with the full GTDB tree remain future work. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes]

### Orthologous groups: near-saturation and phylogenetic control

Genome-wide enrichment analysis of 5,671 eggNOG ortholog groups found 5,341 significantly associated with plant versus non-plant species (q<0.05) [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes], and 3,840 of these showed strong enrichment (OR>2). [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The report says this 94.2% rate reflects genome-wide compositional differences rather than plant-specific functional enrichment. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] It adds that the raw Fisher test effectively captures taxonomic divergence, so only the 50 OGs surviving phylogenetic control are candidate plant-interaction genes. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes]

All 50 top OGs survived NB03 phylum-level logistic regression with 28 large phyla as fixed effects [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes], and after this control 50 novel OGs kept significance (all p<0.05, OR>1). [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] Odds ratios attenuated: COG3569, the top hit, had Fisher OR=8.92, phylo-controlled OR=6.01 and q=7.7e-242, occurring in 54% of plant-associated and 12% of non-plant species. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The report reads the attenuation as partial phylogenetic confounding because plant-associated species cluster taxonomically, with a substantial ecological signal persisting. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] It therefore partially rejects the null that phylogeny alone explains the functional differences. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] All these models showed convergence warnings, likely from quasi-separation in wholly plant-associated or non-plant phyla, so p-values and odds ratios are approximate. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] Finer control failed: family-level regression (NB08) found insufficient within-family variation for all 10 OGs tested, indicating family-level taxonomic clustering of plant association. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes]

The NB14 control with phylum plus log₁₀(genome_size) left 48/50 OGs with a positive plant coefficient at BH-FDR q<0.05, with top-10 controlled OR = 4.4–6.6. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] All 50 kept positive coefficients with genome size as a covariate. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The 2 that did not fit were singular-matrix failures, not biological rejections. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The report interprets plant-associated functional enrichment as real but partly confounded by phylogeny, and says it is hard to tell whether genes cause plant association or plant-associated clades retain them. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] It proposes phylogenetic independent contrasts or phylogenetically informed mixed models to resolve this. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] For more on these genes see [[entities/cog3569]].

For novel OGs, a genome-size control replaced an earlier prevalence-based Bernoulli simulation that adversarial review flagged as circular, using the real per-species OG matrix. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The test was unpenalized statsmodels Logit with Wald inference and BH-FDR across 50 OGs. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] Each OG's presence vector was regressed on is_plant, log₁₀(genome_size) and top-10 phylum dummies across 22,384 species (1,134 plant-associated, 21,250 non-plant). [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] Models converged for 48/50 OGs; COG2351 and COG5343 had singular design matrices, possibly from perfect separation by a phylum dummy. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The report calls those failures numerical rather than biological, since raw plant prevalence is 3× higher. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] After control, 48 of 50 OGs (96%) kept positive plant-association coefficients with |coef| > 0.2 and q < 0.05. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] Controlled odds ratios for the top 10 ranged from 4.4 (COG5516) to 6.6 (COG1845), with q-values as small as 10⁻⁹⁶ for COG3569. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The genome-size coefficient was strongly positive (median 3.4 on the log₁₀ scale) yet did not explain the plant effect away. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The report closes C4 on this basis. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes]

### Genome size and the dual-nature rate

Genome size also shapes the dual-nature rate. Among plant-associated species, it was 54% in the bottom genome-size quartile versus 87% in the top three. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] A quartile analysis gave 54% in the bottom quartile against 87% in the top, an observational genome-size gradient. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] About 33% of dual-nature species shift to neutral under genome-size normalization, so the headline 78.7% rate masks a genome-size gradient. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] Normalization shifts them but does not eliminate the dual-nature pattern. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] Genome size (gene cluster count) correlates moderately with total marker count (r=0.44, p<1e-300), so larger genomes accumulate more markers by chance. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] The report concludes that, with this confound and nonspecific markers, the NB02 dual-nature rate of 60.3% substantially overestimates genuine plant-adapted duality. [src: plant_microbiome_ecotypes] See [[concepts/dual-nature-plant-associated-bacteria-and-the-validity-of-marker-based-cohorts]].

## Phylogenetic control

The CAZyme report controlled phylogeny by non-parametric within-phylum stratification rather than PGLS, which would need a resolved tree for 9,347 species. [src: cazyme_pangenome_ecology] The lanthanide atlas used several corrections. A binomial mixed model (GLMM) with random intercepts for phylum and family gave a phylogeny-corrected xoxF:mxaF ratio of ~143 : 1 (95% credible interval [122, 169]). The report says the random effects absorb phylum-level heterogeneity. [src: lanthanide_methylotrophy_atlas] A family-equal-weight bootstrap over 271 MDH-informative GTDB families gave 0.960 [0.941, 0.976], so H1 is not driven by Pseudomonadota's 117 K-genome size advantage. [src: lanthanide_methylotrophy_atlas] The report concludes H1 is robust to all three frameworks. [src: lanthanide_methylotrophy_atlas] It also acknowledges that its per-phylum binomial framework does not formally control non-independence. [src: lanthanide_methylotrophy_atlas]

The PHB report notes that PHB presence correlates with phylogeny as well as genome size. [src: phb_granule_ecology] It says phylogenetic logistic regression would further strengthen the enrichment result [src: phb_granule_ecology], and proposes phylogenetic logistic regression or independent contrasts to separate environmental selection from phylogenetic inertia. [src: phb_granule_ecology]

The pathway_capability_dependency report used genus-level grouping from GTDB species clade names as a conservative phylogenetic control, because full phylum stratification would need the GTDB taxonomy table from the KBase Data Lakehouse. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] It did not compute full phylogenetic independent contrasts. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] It added phylogenetic controls to all correlations and ecotype analyses [src: pathway_capability_dependency], citing ecotype_analysis, where phylogeny dominates gene content in 60.5% of species. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] This raises the possibility that metabolic ecotypes partly reflect intra-species phylogenetic structure. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] Within genera, the pathway–openness signal was positive in 13/18 genera and significant (p<0.05) in 5/18, limited by per-genus sample sizes. [src: pathway_capability_dependency] In genera with ≥20 species the relationship holds in the majority, which the report takes to suggest a general evolutionary dynamic rather than a clade-specific artifact. [src: pathway_capability_dependency]

In gc_ecotype, connected-components control is thin where species have few ANI (average nucleotide identity) clusters. Examples are Streptococcus thermophilus with 5 clusters [src: gc_ecotype_ecology] and Enterococcus faecium with 2 clusters among 1,633 genomes. [src: gc_ecotype_ecology] A phylogenetic-PCoA control mitigates this only for the subset of species that retain significance. [src: gc_ecotype_ecology] The largest partial R² values come from species with many small clusters, such as Aeromonas veronii (87 clusters of 122 genomes), where phylogenetic control uses up most degrees of freedom and leaves a small but well-determined residual variance. [src: gc_ecotype_ecology]

## Proposed controls

The reports propose further controls. One proposes PGLS or phylogenetic mixed models with a GTDB species tree to test whether biome effects on CAZyme composition are independent of phylogeny. [src: cazyme_pangenome_ecology] Another proposes rarefying genera to equal genome coverage (e.g., n = 1 or 2 species) followed by PGLS. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] This would require a species-level AMR table stratified by genome count. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] A formal rarefaction curve over several species-per-genus thresholds would quantify the depth-of-coverage effect more precisely. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The same report proposes genus-level pangenome openness, the proportion of genes in the accessory genome, as a predictor alongside metal type diversity. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] It hypothesizes that a broader accessory genome may underlie both niche breadth and metal type diversity. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

## Open Directions

- Run PGLS or phylogenetic mixed models on a GTDB species tree for the CAZyme biome effects, to replace within-phylum stratification and test independence from phylogeny.
- Redo the CAZyme cloud-fraction analysis with rarefaction to equal genome counts per species, to test whether any biological openness signal remains beyond ρ = 0.101.
- Build the rarefaction curve over several species-per-genus thresholds from a genome-count-stratified AMR table, to quantify the coverage-depth effect.
- Add genus-level pangenome openness alongside metal type diversity, to test whether the accessory genome drives both niche breadth and metal diversity.
- Add genome count as a covariate in the ecotype reanalysis, and compare the downsampled (max 250) and full (up to 3,505) sets.
- Replace connected-components control with an alternative phylogenetic control for species with few clusters but many genomes, such as E. faecium.
- Repeat the PHB niche-breadth and enrichment tests with phylogenetic logistic regression alongside genome size, to see whether the sign reversal and quartile enrichment hold beyond Spearman partial correlation and stratification.
- Fit a tree-based phylogenetic mixed model for the plant-association markers using a reduced or sparse covariance, to test the cassette-level tier against the genus-cluster approximation.
- Rerun the within-genus label shuffle with several thousand permutations and a multiple-testing correction, to settle whether T3SS survives.
- Run a partial PERMANOVA on the prophage module data with phylogeny and genome size entered together, to estimate the unique variance explained by environment.
