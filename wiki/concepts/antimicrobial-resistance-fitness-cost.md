# Fitness costs and conditional benefits of antimicrobial-resistance genes

Antimicrobial-resistance (AMR) genes can impose a measurable baseline burden while becoming beneficial under antibiotic exposure. This distinguishes constitutive cost from condition-specific protection and raises the hypothesis that laboratory adaptation or compensation can conceal the original cost of resistance acquisition. The evidence supports a relative AMR-associated fitness difference, but compensation remains an interpretation rather than a directly measured process. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Baseline fitness cost

Using genome-wide transposon fitness data from the [[entities/kescience-fitnessbrowser]] compendium, the analysis identified 1,352 AMR genes across 43 organisms and tested 25 organisms with sufficient data for per-organism effects. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The perturbation method was RB-TnSeq (random barcode transposon sequencing), a pooled transposon method that estimates mutant fitness relative to the pool average rather than an absolute selection coefficient. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

Under non-antibiotic conditions, AMR-gene knockouts had systematically higher fitness than non-AMR-gene knockouts, indicating a relative metabolic burden associated with AMR genes in the measured strains. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Every organism showed a positive AMR-versus-background shift, and a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects meta-analysis estimated a pooled shift of **+0.086 [95% CI: +0.074, +0.098], z = 14.3, p ~ 0**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The median per-organism Cohen's d was **0.18**, heterogeneity was **I² = 54.3%**, and Cochran's **Q = 52.54, p = 0.0007**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Six organisms were nominally significant at p<0.05, and two were significant after false-discovery-rate correction at q<0.05: acidovorax_3H11 and Koxy. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

This pooled effect is relative, not evidence that AMR-gene knockouts have beneficial absolute growth. Absolute AMR-gene knockout fitness averaged **−0.024**, whereas the non-AMR knockout background averaged approximately **−0.11**; a one-sample Wilcoxon test of AMR-gene knockout fitness against zero gave **p = 0.999**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Thus, the result supports the interpretation that removing AMR genes reduced the burden relative to the comparison background, not that the AMR knockouts had positive absolute fitness. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Conditional benefit under antibiotic exposure

The baseline burden was partially decoupled from antibiotic-dependent importance. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Across any-antibiotic experiments, **57.0%** of AMR genes showed a fitness flip toward greater importance under antibiotic exposure, with **N = 797**, mean flip **+0.045** defined as non-antibiotic fitness minus antibiotic fitness, and Wilcoxon signed-rank **p = 0.0001**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This **supports** [[concepts/condition-specific-fitness]]: the same resistance system can carry a modest relative burden without antibiotics yet become important when the relevant environmental challenge is present. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The antibiotic-dependent shift varied by resistance mechanism. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Broad-spectrum efflux genes had a mean flip of **+0.094**, compared with **−0.001** for enzymatic-inactivation genes, with Mann-Whitney U **p = 0.007**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This supports the hypothesis that broad-spectrum efflux systems can protect against multiple antibiotic conditions, whereas enzymatic inactivation is more dependent on exposure to a matching antibiotic. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

A class-matched validation included **157 gene-antibiotic pairs** across four resistance classes, with a mean flip of **+0.113**, a flip rate of **54.8%**, and Wilcoxon **p = 0.14**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Chloramphenicol resistance genes showed the strongest validation, with **6/6 (100%)** showing the expected flip. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Beta-lactam resistance contributed **105 pairs across 10 organisms** and had a **50%** flip rate. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Mechanism, conservation, and compensation

Baseline fitness cost did not differ significantly among efflux (**N=254**), enzymatic inactivation (**N=304**), metal resistance (**N=144**), and unknown (**N=74**) mechanisms. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The Kruskal-Wallis test gave **H = 0.65, p = 0.89**, and the Jonckheere-Terpstra test for the predicted ordering efflux > enzymatic > metal > unknown gave **z = 0.23, p = 0.41**; no pairwise comparison survived false-discovery-rate correction. [src: amr_fitness_cost] These results contradict the prediction that resistance mechanism alone determines baseline fitness cost in this dataset. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

Mechanism did, however, predict pangenome conservation status. [src: amr_fitness_cost] A chi-squared test found a strong association between mechanism and conservation status (**χ² = 69.3, p = 1.4×10⁻¹³**). [src: amr_fitness_cost] Metal-resistance genes were accessory in **44%** of cases, compared with **13%** of efflux genes and **16%** of enzymatic-inactivation genes. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This **refines** the relationship between mechanism and persistence: mechanism predicted where AMR genes occurred in the pangenome, but not their baseline fitness cost. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The cofitness analysis **supports** this separation between mechanism, conservation, and cost. Across 28 organisms, support-network size was not correlated with AMR fitness cost (Spearman rho = **−0.006**, p = **0.87**, N = **769**); within mechanisms, rho values were **−0.049** for efflux, **+0.038** for enzymatic resistance, and **−0.031** for metal resistance, all with p > 0.4. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] Thus, neither resistance mechanism nor the breadth of its cofitness neighborhood explains the common fitness shift, although the network analysis used fitness profiles rather than a direct measure of metabolic burden. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]

Core or intrinsic AMR genes (**N=638**) and accessory or acquired AMR genes (**N=163**) had virtually identical fitness distributions, with both means at **−0.024**, Cohen's **d = 0.002**, and Mann-Whitney U **p = 0.33**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The broader stratification analysis likewise found no conservation difference (**p = 0.33**), no Tier 1-versus-Tier 2 difference (**p = 0.26**), and no antibiotic-versus-metal resistance-type difference (**p = 0.87**). [src: amr_fitness_cost] This null result supports the possibility that horizontal transfer preferentially retains cost-optimized genes or that compensation occurs rapidly after acquisition, but the evidence is strongest for well-sampled species. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The approximately **+0.086** common cost may therefore reflect residual metabolic overhead after compensatory evolution in lab-adapted RB-TnSeq strains rather than the unmitigated cost of newly acquired resistance. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This is an explanation consistent with the data, not a direct measurement of compensatory mutations, their timing, or their causal effect. [src: amr_fitness_cost] All **25** tested organisms were lab-adapted strains, so laboratory history may have reduced measurable AMR costs relative to natural populations. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The core-versus-accessory null result is also consistent with cost optimization or post-acquisition compensation, but does not distinguish those explanations. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Essentiality and interpretation limits

Only **4.6%** of AMR genes were absent from the fitness matrices and therefore putatively essential, compared with an approximately **14%** background essential rate estimated in a prior analysis using a different organism set. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Tier 1 genes from bakta_amr (**N=110**) and Tier 2 keyword-annotated genes (**N=691**) had indistinguishable fitness distributions (**KS p = 0.17**), although the broader assembly contained **178 Tier 1** and **1,174 Tier 2** genes. [src: amr_fitness_cost] These findings connect the AMR-cost result to [[concepts/gene-essentiality]] while leaving open the possibility that essential AMR genes, if systematically more costly, are underrepresented in the measured distributions. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The cofitness analysis **refines** interpretation of the same fitness measurements: among 801 AMR genes with fitness data, 769 (**96%**) had at least one extra-operon cofitness partner at |r| > 0.3, but cofitness indicates shared fitness phenotypes rather than direct transcriptional control. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] AMR-containing ICA (independent component analysis) modules had a median of **46** genes versus **27** for non-AMR modules (MWU p = **1.7×10⁻⁸**), while network size still did not predict cost. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] Enrichment for flagellar motility and amino acid biosynthesis may therefore represent shared dispensability in shaken, often nutrient-rich laboratory cultures rather than AMR-specific regulation; a mean-fitness-matched permutation is required to distinguish these explanations. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]

Tier 2 contained **86%** of the AMR genes and relied on keyword matching from Bakta annotations, which may include non-AMR genes such as general efflux transporters; the Tier 1 sensitivity analysis gave consistent results. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Transposon insertions can also have polar effects on downstream genes in operons, potentially confounding AMR-gene fitness measurements. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

Core/accessory labels used a **≥95%** prevalence threshold, while most Fitness Browser species had few GTDB genomes: the median was **9**, with a range of **2–399**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] A gene present in all **9** sampled genomes could therefore be labeled core at the available sampling depth but become accessory with broader sampling, making the core-versus-accessory null result especially cautious for species with fewer than **20** genomes. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The product classifier did not handle fosfomycin or tellurite resistance annotations, leaving approximately **25 genes** in the unknown mechanism category; reclassification would move them to enzymatic inactivation and metal resistance, respectively. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Tensions

The main tension is between the strong any-antibiotic signal and the weaker class-matched validation. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The any-antibiotic analysis found a **57.0%** flip rate for **N = 797** genes with **p = 0.0001**, whereas the class-matched analysis found a **54.8%** flip rate for **157** pairs with **p = 0.14**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This does not establish opposing biological effects, because the analyses differ in sample size and matching design; it identifies a need to determine whether broad condition coverage or antibiotic-class specificity best explains the stronger result. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

A second tension concerns the absence of baseline cost differences among mechanisms alongside the strong mechanism-by-conservation association. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Mechanism was not associated with baseline cost (**H = 0.65, p = 0.89**) but was strongly associated with conservation status (**χ² = 69.3, p = 1.4×10⁻¹³**). [src: amr_fitness_cost] The evidence therefore supports different roles for mechanism in fitness and pangenome distribution rather than a single mechanism-to-cost-to-retention pathway. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

A third tension concerns whether AMR-associated cofitness enrichment is mechanistic or reflects shared dispensability. Flagellar motility, chemotaxis, and amino acid biosynthesis were enriched in AMR support neighborhoods, but the cofitness analysis identifies shared dispensability under laboratory conditions as an alternative explanation and reports no network-size relationship with cost. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] This does not contradict the measured AMR fitness burden or antibiotic-dependent benefit; it limits the inference that cofitness-network structure explains that burden. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]

## Open Directions

- Use organism-stratified models on Cup4G11 (**77** AMR genes) and BFirm (**50** AMR genes) to test mechanism effects while controlling for genetic background. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Use matched genomes from the **25** lab-adapted organisms, comparative genomics, and fitness association analysis to ask whether specific compensatory mutations explain the residual **+0.086** shift. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Reconstruct newly acquired and compensated AMR genotypes in isogenic strains and measure growth and competition fitness to distinguish original from post-compensation costs. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Subclassify efflux pumps into narrow-spectrum drug pumps and general RND systems such as AcrAB-TolC, then test whether constitutively expressed systems have lower baseline costs under condition-specific assays. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Replace averages across non-antibiotic experiments with condition-specific analyses of metal, osmotic, and carbon-limitation stresses to test whether baseline cost changes by environment. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Cross-reference the **144** metal-resistance genes with the metal fitness atlas and matched metal-stress measurements to test whether genes costly under standard conditions are protective under metal stress. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Expand GTDB sampling beyond the median of **9** genomes per Fitness Browser species and recompute the **≥95%** core/accessory labels to ask whether the cost null result persists with deeper pangenomes. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Use mean-fitness-matched permutations, antibiotic-versus-standard-growth cofitness matrices, and direct measurements of flagellar-knockout fitness to test whether AMR-neighborhood enrichment reflects shared dispensability or co-regulation. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]
- Extend the analysis from **25** Fitness Browser organisms to all **293K** BERDL genomes by predicting AMR cost from gene-cluster conservation patterns and testing whether the laboratory-derived relationship generalizes across the broader genome collection. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
