# Fitness costs and conditional benefits of antimicrobial-resistance genes

Antimicrobial-resistance (AMR) genes can impose a measurable baseline burden while becoming beneficial under antibiotic exposure. The evidence supports a relative AMR-associated fitness difference, but compensation remains an interpretation rather than a directly measured process. This distinction is essential because RB-TnSeq (random barcode transposon sequencing) measures competitive fitness of insertion mutants relative to the pool average, rather than an absolute selection coefficient in an isogenic strain. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Consequently, a difference between mutant classes is a relative perturbation-fitness shift, not automatically beneficial or harmful absolute growth. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Baseline relative fitness cost

Using genome-wide transposon fitness data from the [[entities/kescience-fitnessbrowser]] compendium, the analysis identified 1,352 AMR genes across 43 organisms and tested 25 organisms with sufficient data for per-organism effects. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Under non-antibiotic conditions, AMR-gene knockouts had systematically higher fitness than non-AMR-gene knockouts, indicating a relative metabolic burden associated with AMR genes in the measured strains. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

Every organism showed a positive AMR-versus-background shift, and a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects meta-analysis estimated a pooled shift of **+0.086 [95% CI: +0.074, +0.098], z = 14.3, p ~ 0**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The median per-organism Cohen's d was **0.18**, heterogeneity was **I² = 54.3%**, and Cochran's **Q = 52.54, p = 0.0007**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Six organisms were nominally significant at p<0.05, and two were significant after false-discovery-rate correction at q<0.05: acidovorax_3H11 and Koxy. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The perturbation scale **refines** the interpretation of this result. AMR-gene knockouts had average fitness **−0.024**, whereas the non-AMR knockout background averaged approximately **−0.11**, producing the relative difference of **+0.086**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The positive difference means that AMR knockouts were less impaired than the comparison knockout background; it does not mean that they grew faster than an unperturbed reference. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Absolute AMR-gene knockout fitness averaged **−0.024**, and a one-sample Wilcoxon test against zero gave **p = 0.999**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Thus, removing AMR genes reduced burden relative to the comparison background, not that AMR knockouts had positive absolute fitness. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The report describes this relative shift as a baseline AMR burden, but the inference is indirect because it compares knockout classes rather than directly measuring the cost of acquiring or expressing an intact resistance gene. [src: amr_fitness_cost] A relative fitness effect is meaningful only with an explicitly defined comparison background: here, AMR and non-AMR knockouts were compared under non-antibiotic conditions, not against an unperturbed wild type. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This makes [[concepts/fitness-matched-null-models]] and condition matching central to deciding whether an apparent cost reflects resistance biology or differences in experiment composition. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Conditional benefit under antibiotic exposure

The baseline burden was partially decoupled from antibiotic-dependent importance. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Across any-antibiotic experiments, **57.0%** of AMR genes showed a fitness flip toward greater importance under antibiotic exposure, with **N = 797**, mean flip **+0.045** defined as non-antibiotic fitness minus antibiotic fitness, and Wilcoxon signed-rank **p = 0.0001**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] A positive flip therefore indicates greater importance of the gene during antibiotic exposure, rather than a positive absolute growth rate. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This **supports** [[concepts/condition-specific-fitness]]: the same resistance system can carry a modest relative burden without antibiotics yet become important when the relevant environmental challenge is present. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The antibiotic-dependent shift varied by resistance mechanism. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Broad-spectrum efflux genes had a mean flip of **+0.094**, compared with **−0.001** for enzymatic-inactivation genes, with Mann-Whitney U **p = 0.007**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This supports the hypothesis that broad-spectrum efflux systems can protect against multiple antibiotic conditions, whereas enzymatic inactivation is more dependent on exposure to a matching antibiotic. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

A class-matched validation included **157 gene-antibiotic pairs** across four resistance classes, with a mean flip of **+0.113**, a flip rate of **54.8%**, and Wilcoxon **p = 0.14**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Chloramphenicol resistance genes showed the strongest validation, with **6/6 (100%)** showing the expected flip. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Beta-lactam resistance contributed **105 pairs across 10 organisms** and had a **50%** flip rate. [src: amr_fitness_cost] These results support separating [[concepts/condition-specific-fitness]] from a pooled baseline contrast: mechanisms had similar modest relative costs without antibiotics, while broad-spectrum efflux systems became important across many antibiotic conditions and narrow-spectrum enzymes generally required matching antibiotics. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The strain-variation atlas **refines** this condition-dependent interpretation by showing that AMR repertoires are also structured within species: among 37,444 AMR gene-species records, 51.3% were rare, 41.3% variable, and 7.5% fixed, with median variability index **0.526** and median pairwise Jaccard distance **0.435**. [src: amr_strain_variation] These prevalence patterns do not measure fitness costs, but they support the hypothesis that conditional benefits and lineage or ecological context can produce substantial within-species AMR heterogeneity rather than a uniform species-level phenotype. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The broader conservation analysis **supports** treating conditional fitness as compatible with conservation rather than assuming that condition-specific genes are mainly accessory: across approximately 194,000 genes from 43 bacteria, genes tagged with strong condition-specific effects were 77.3% core versus 70.3% without such phenotypes, and the analysis identified 4,450 neutral-overall but condition-critical genes, 3.0% of core genes versus 1.7% of auxiliary and 1.6% of singleton genes. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] This is not an AMR-specific estimate, but it supports testing whether antibiotic-conditioned AMR effects are more detectable for conserved genes instead of interpreting conservation as evidence of constitutive, condition-independent importance. [src: fitness_effects_conservation]

## Mechanism, conservation, and compensation

Baseline fitness cost did not differ significantly among efflux (**N=254**), enzymatic inactivation (**N=304**), metal resistance (**N=144**), and unknown (**N=74**) mechanisms. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The Kruskal-Wallis test gave **H = 0.65, p = 0.89**, and the Jonckheere-Terpstra test for the predicted ordering efflux > enzymatic > metal > unknown gave **z = 0.23, p = 0.41**; no pairwise comparison survived false-discovery-rate correction. [src: amr_fitness_cost] These results contradict the prediction that resistance mechanism alone determines baseline fitness cost in this dataset. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

Mechanism did, however, predict pangenome conservation status. [src: amr_fitness_cost] A chi-squared test found a strong association between mechanism and conservation status (**χ² = 69.3, p = 1.4×10⁻¹³**). [src: amr_fitness_cost] Metal-resistance genes were accessory in **44%** of cases, compared with **13%** of efflux genes and **16%** of enzymatic-inactivation genes. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This **refines** the relationship between mechanism and persistence: mechanism predicted where AMR genes occurred in the pangenome, but not their baseline fitness cost. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The strain-variation analysis **supports** this separation between prevalence and measured cost. Atlas Core AMR genes were fixed in **77.3%** of cases, whereas **78.7%** of Singleton genes were rare; Auxiliary genes were **57.3%** variable and **42.7%** rare. [src: amr_strain_variation] Thus, conservation class strongly organizes within-species prevalence, while the Fitness Browser comparison found no baseline fitness difference between core or intrinsic and accessory or acquired AMR genes. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The two results are compatible because prevalence and knockout fitness are different observables, but neither establishes that conserved genes are intrinsically less costly.

The all-gene conservation analysis **refines** this AMR-specific null result rather than overturning it: essential genes were 82% core versus 66% for always-neutral genes, and core genes were more likely than auxiliary genes to show both negative and positive fitness tails, including 24.4% ever beneficial versus 19.9% for auxiliary genes. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] Therefore, conservation is weakly associated with broader and stronger measured fitness effects across genes, whereas the AMR subset showed no detectable core/accessory difference in its baseline knockout contrast. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] [src: amr_fitness_cost] The distinction may reflect subset-specific power, resistance-mechanism composition, or the fact that the AMR result compares perturbation classes rather than intact-gene costs; it should not be resolved by importing the all-gene gradient into the AMR subset.

Core or intrinsic AMR genes (**N=638**) and accessory or acquired AMR genes (**N=163**) had virtually identical fitness distributions, with both means at **−0.024**, Cohen's **d = 0.002**, and Mann-Whitney U **p = 0.33**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The broader stratification analysis likewise found no conservation difference (**p = 0.33**), no Tier 1-versus-Tier 2 difference (**p = 0.26**), and no antibiotic-versus-metal resistance-type difference (**p = 0.87**). [src: amr_fitness_cost] This null result supports the possibility that horizontal transfer preferentially retains cost-optimized genes or that compensation occurs rapidly after acquisition, but the evidence is strongest for well-sampled species. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The approximately **+0.086** common cost may therefore reflect residual metabolic overhead after compensatory evolution in lab-adapted RB-TnSeq strains rather than the unmitigated cost of newly acquired resistance. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This is an explanation consistent with the data, not a direct measurement of compensatory mutations, their timing, or their causal effect. [src: amr_fitness_cost] All **25** tested organisms were lab-adapted strains, so laboratory history may have reduced measurable AMR costs relative to natural populations. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The core-versus-accessory null result is also consistent with cost optimization or post-acquisition compensation, but does not distinguish those explanations. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Network breadth and fitness cost

The cofitness analysis **supports** a separation between mechanism, conservation, network breadth, and cost. Across 28 organisms, 769 of the 801 AMR genes with fitness data (**96%**) had at least one extra-operon cofitness partner at |r| > 0.3. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] Support-network size was not correlated with AMR fitness cost: Spearman's rho, a rank-based correlation coefficient, was **−0.006**, p = **0.87**, N = **769**. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] Within mechanisms, rho values were **−0.049** for efflux, **+0.038** for enzymatic resistance, and **−0.031** for metal resistance, all with p > 0.4. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] The uniform resistance cost of **+0.086** was therefore not explained by co-regulatory-neighborhood size, although the cost analysis did not measure network breadth directly. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] This **refines** [[concepts/cofitness-network-architecture]]: the number of genes sharing a fitness-profile pattern and the magnitude of the resistance-associated fitness phenotype were distinct in this dataset, rather than network breadth necessarily imposing a larger cost. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]

The AMR strain atlas adds a different kind of network evidence and **refines** this conclusion: it detected 1,517 resistance islands across 705 species, with mean size **6.2** genes, median size **4**, maximum size **43**, and mean phi coefficient **0.827**; phi measures pairwise co-occurrence. [src: amr_strain_variation] Of these islands, **1,343/1,517 (88%)** contained genes from multiple resistance mechanisms. [src: amr_strain_variation] These tightly co-inherited genomic modules show that physical or occurrence linkage can be extensive even when cofitness-network breadth is unrelated to measured cost. [src: amr_strain_variation] Co-occurrence does not establish co-selection, functional synergy, or a larger fitness burden. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The null association does not show that network context is biologically irrelevant, because insufficient variance in fitness cost could limit detection of a relationship. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] At |r| > 0.3, the mean support network contained **233 genes**, while means at |r| > 0.4 and |r| > 0.5 were **110** and **71** genes, respectively. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] These broad networks may include many weak associations, and confirmation at |r| > 0.4 was identified as necessary. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]

Cofitness measures shared fitness phenotypes rather than direct transcriptional control, so a large network can reflect shared condition-dependent dispensability without implying a proportionally greater cost. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] AMR-containing ICA (independent component analysis) modules had a median of **46** genes versus **27** for non-AMR modules (MWU p = **1.7×10⁻⁸**). [src: amr_cofitness_networks] Enrichment for flagellar motility and amino acid biosynthesis may therefore represent shared dispensability in shaken, often nutrient-rich laboratory cultures rather than AMR-specific regulation; a mean-fitness-matched permutation is required to distinguish these explanations. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] The absence of a network-size–cost relationship is a direct null result within this dataset, not a general demonstration that network architecture can never influence fitness cost. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]

## Essentiality and measurement limits

Only **4.6%** of AMR genes were absent from the fitness matrices and therefore putatively essential, compared with an approximately **14%** background essential rate estimated in a prior analysis using a different organism set. [src: amr_fitness_cost] These omitted genes cannot contribute ordinary knockout fitness values and could alter the observed contrast if they are disproportionately costly. [src: amr_fitness_cost] These findings connect the AMR-cost result to [[concepts/gene-essentiality]] while leaving open the possibility that essential AMR genes, if systematically more costly, are underrepresented in the measured distributions. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

Tier 1 genes from bakta_amr (**N=110**) and Tier 2 keyword-annotated genes (**N=691**) had indistinguishable fitness distributions (**KS p = 0.17**), although the broader assembly contained **178 Tier 1** and **1,174 Tier 2** genes. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Tier 2 contained **86%** of the AMR genes and relied on keyword matching from Bakta annotations, which may include non-AMR genes such as general efflux transporters; the Tier 1 sensitivity analysis gave consistent results. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This connects relative-fitness interpretation to [[concepts/environmental-resistome|annotation-dependent resistome inference]]. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

Transposon insertions can have polar effects on downstream genes in operons, potentially confounding attribution of a fitness phenotype to the targeted AMR gene itself. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The cofitness analysis used fitness profiles rather than a direct measure of metabolic burden. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] Missing fitness values were treated as zero in z-score space through `np.nan_to_num`, which approximates but does not equal pairwise-complete Pearson correlation, although the report judged the dense Fitness Browser matrices unlikely to substantially alter the conclusions. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]

Core/accessory labels used a **≥95%** prevalence threshold, while most Fitness Browser species had few GTDB genomes: the median was **9**, with a range of **2–399**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] A gene present in all **9** sampled genomes could therefore be labeled core at the available sampling depth but become accessory with broader sampling, making the core-versus-accessory null result especially cautious for species with fewer than **20** genomes. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The product classifier did not handle fosfomycin or tellurite resistance annotations, leaving approximately **25 genes** in the unknown mechanism category; reclassification would move them to enzymatic inactivation and metal resistance, respectively. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The AMR atlas provides a complementary sampling warning: its collection included **180,025 genomes**, but the GTDB/NCBI set was biased toward clinical and human-associated isolates, and **52.7%** of genomes had no classifiable isolation source. [src: amr_strain_variation] Host-associated species carried more AMR genes per genome than terrestrial or aquatic species under both approximate environment classifiers, with human-clinical isolates highest; this is ecological association, not evidence of a larger per-gene fitness cost. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The **28** cofitness organisms were lab-adapted, phylogenetically biased, included many Pseudomonas organisms, and had limited ecological diversity, restricting ecological generalization. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] The independent AMR cost estimate likewise covered **25** lab-adapted strains and cautions that compensation may have reduced measurable costs relative to wild populations. [src: amr_fitness_cost] These limitations connect the result to [[concepts/condition-space-dimensionality]] and to perturbation-modality-dependent phenotypic architecture. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Tensions

The main tension is between the strong any-antibiotic signal and the weaker class-matched validation. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The any-antibiotic analysis found a **57.0%** flip rate for **N = 797** genes with **p = 0.0001**, whereas the class-matched analysis found a **54.8%** flip rate for **157** pairs with **p = 0.14**. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This does not establish opposing biological effects, because the analyses differ in sample size and matching design; it identifies a need to determine whether broad condition coverage or antibiotic-class specificity best explains the stronger result. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The class-matched analysis was less powered than the any-antibiotic analysis, so the positive pooled signal should not be treated as equally strong for every drug-resistance class. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

A second tension concerns the absence of baseline cost differences among mechanisms alongside the strong mechanism-by-conservation association. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Mechanism was not associated with baseline cost (**H = 0.65, p = 0.89**) but was strongly associated with conservation status (**χ² = 69.3, p = 1.4×10⁻¹³**). [src: amr_fitness_cost] The evidence therefore supports different roles for mechanism in fitness and pangenome distribution rather than a single mechanism-to-cost-to-retention pathway. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

A third tension concerns the AMR-specific conservation null versus the all-gene conservation gradient. The all-gene analysis found higher core representation among essential and broadly fitness-active genes, plus stronger positive and negative fitness tails among core genes. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] In contrast, core or intrinsic and accessory or acquired AMR genes had indistinguishable baseline fitness distributions (**d = 0.002, p = 0.33**). [src: fitness_effects_conservation] [src: amr_fitness_cost] These results address different scopes and observables, but resolving whether AMR genes are an exception requires a larger, condition-matched AMR subset with comparable coverage of core, accessory, and singleton genes.

A fourth tension concerns whether AMR-associated cofitness enrichment is mechanistic or reflects shared dispensability. Flagellar motility, chemotaxis, and amino acid biosynthesis were enriched in AMR support neighborhoods, but the cofitness analysis identifies shared dispensability under laboratory conditions as an alternative explanation and reports no network-size relationship with cost. [src: amr_cofitness_networks] This does not contradict the measured AMR fitness burden or antibiotic-dependent benefit; it limits the inference that cofitness-network structure explains that burden. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]

The resistance-island result introduces a related measurement tension rather than resolving the third one: mean phi **0.827** and **88%** multi-mechanism islands indicate strong co-inheritance, but the atlas explicitly cautions that linkage on mobile genetic elements does not prove co-selection or functional synergy. [src: amr_strain_variation] Thus, genomic co-inheritance can coexist with a null cofitness-size–cost relationship; the two analyses should not be treated as interchangeable measures of network architecture. [src: amr_strain_variation] [src: amr_cofitness_networks]

## Open Directions

- Use isogenic strains with direct growth-rate or competition assays to test whether the **+0.086** knockout-class contrast corresponds to an absolute cost of intact AMR genes rather than only a relative perturbation shift. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Use organism-stratified models on Cup4G11 (**77** AMR genes) and BFirm (**50** AMR genes) to test mechanism effects while controlling for genetic background. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Use matched genomes from the **25** lab-adapted organisms, comparative genomics, and fitness association analysis to ask whether specific compensatory mutations explain the residual **+0.086** shift. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Reconstruct newly acquired and compensated AMR genotypes in isogenic strains and measure growth and competition fitness to distinguish original from post-compensation costs. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Model insertion position and operon context, using gene-neighborhood information to test how much of the measured AMR phenotype could arise from polar effects on downstream genes. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Incorporate the approximately **4.6%** putatively essential AMR genes through targeted essentiality assays or alternative perturbations to test whether their exclusion makes **+0.086** a lower bound. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Reanalyze the **801** non-antibiotic per-gene fitness records with matched metal, osmotic, and carbon-limitation conditions to ask whether the baseline contrast changes across stress environments. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Subclassify efflux pumps into narrow-spectrum drug pumps and general RND systems such as AcrAB-TolC, then test whether constitutively expressed systems have lower baseline costs and whether breadth predicts antibiotic-dependent importance using condition-matched assays. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Replace averages across non-antibiotic experiments with condition-specific analyses of metal, osmotic, and carbon-limitation stresses to test whether baseline cost changes by environment. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Cross-reference the **144** metal-resistance genes with the metal fitness atlas and matched metal-stress measurements to test whether genes costly under standard conditions are protective under metal stress. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Expand GTDB sampling beyond the median of **9** genomes per Fitness Browser species and recompute the **≥95%** core/accessory labels to ask whether the cost null result persists with deeper pangenomes. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Use the 1,517 resistance-island records to map islands to plasmids, chromosomes, integrons, and insertion sequences, then test whether physical linkage predicts antibiotic-conditioned fitness or persistence beyond cofitness and prevalence effects. [src: amr_strain_variation]
- Use mean-fitness-matched permutations, including random non-AMR genes in the **−0.05 to +0.05** range, to test whether AMR-neighborhood structure exceeds what is expected from fitness level alone. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]
- Recompute cofitness separately for antibiotic-treatment and standard-growth conditions, and repeat network-size–cost analyses at |r| > 0.4 and |r| > 0.5. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]
- Quantify variance in AMR fitness costs and use power analysis or a hierarchical model to ask whether the null result reflects insufficient variation across genes, mechanisms, or organisms. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]
- Compare cofitness network breadth with direct regulatory evidence and Pfam-domain enrichment to test whether broad networks reflect shared cofitness, co-regulation, or common functional architecture. [src: amr_cofitness_networks]
- Within organisms with dense AMR and cofitness data, test network breadth, mechanism, and core/accessory status jointly under matched conditions; directly measure flagellar-knockout fitness to distinguish shared dispensability from co-regulation. [src: amr_fitness_cost] [src: amr_cofitness_networks]
- Test whether the AMR ecotypes identified in **190/974** species predict condition-specific fitness, virulence profiles, or metabolic variation after controlling for clinical and sampling bias. [src: amr_strain_variation]
- Extend the analysis from **25** Fitness Browser organisms to all **293K** KBase Data Lakehouse genomes by predicting AMR cost from gene-cluster conservation patterns and testing whether the laboratory-derived relationship generalizes across the broader genome collection. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Reanalyze AMR genes against the all-gene conservation framework, stratifying by core, auxiliary, and singleton status, fitness breadth, and antibiotic condition, to test whether the AMR-specific null differs from the broader core-gene fitness gradient. [src: fitness_effects_conservation]

A reported positive or negative transposon fitness contrast should state its perturbation, reference population, condition set, and scale before being called a biological cost or benefit. [src: amr_fitness_cost] For this dataset, the most defensible wording is that AMR knockouts showed a positive relative shift against the non-AMR knockout background under non-antibiotic conditions, while AMR genes often became more important under antibiotic exposure. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The all-gene conservation analysis **refines** this interpretation by showing that conservation can accompany both broad and conditional fitness effects, without establishing that the AMR-specific baseline cost differs by conservation class. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] Within-species atlas data further indicate that the ecological and lineage distribution of AMR repertoires is heterogeneous, but do not convert prevalence or co-inheritance into a direct fitness estimate.
