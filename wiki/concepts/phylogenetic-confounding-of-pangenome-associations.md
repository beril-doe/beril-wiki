# Phylogenetic Confounding of Pangenome Associations

Phylogenetic confounding occurs when differences among taxonomic groups dominate an aggregate association, potentially hiding relationships that exist within lineages or creating apparent relationships because two traits share the same taxonomic distribution. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The [[summaries/amr_pangenome_atlas__REPORT]] provides a direct example using antimicrobial-resistance (AMR) burden and pangenome openness across bacterial species. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Pangenome openness positively correlated with AMR count in 8/10 tested phyla, but the overall correlation across the dataset was near zero (rho=0.006), indicating that phylogeny dominated the aggregate signal. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The strongest within-phylum associations were observed in Bacillota (rho=0.219, p=1.0e-16) and Bacillota_C (rho=0.374, p=3.1e-5). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

This pattern **supports** the interpretation that a near-zero global association does not demonstrate the absence of a relationship between openness and AMR burden. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Instead, associations may be positive within several phyla while differing in magnitude, direction, or baseline across phyla, causing them to cancel when species are pooled. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The result is an association rather than a causal inference, because the analysis describes correlations across pangenome species and does not by itself establish that openness causes AMR accumulation. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The [[summaries/pangenome_openness__REPORT]] **refines** this interpretation by testing whether openness itself predicts the relative influence of environment or phylogeny on gene-content variation. In the matched species set, openness was not significantly related to the environment effect (Spearman rho = -0.05, p-value = 0.54) or the phylogeny effect (Spearman rho = 0.03, p-value = 0.73). [src: pangenome_openness] Thus, the AMR example supports lineage-aware analysis, but the broader result does not support treating open or closed pangenome status as a general predictor of whether environmental or phylogenetic structure dominates. [src: pangenome_openness] This does not remove phylogenetic confounding from individual associations; it limits the claim that a single openness metric predicts that confounding across species. [src: pangenome_openness]

Within-species AMR evidence **supports** the need for this lineage-aware interpretation at a finer scale. Across 1,305 species and 180,025 genomes, 55.6% of 1,261 species tested showed significant correlation between ANI (average nucleotide identity) distance and AMR Jaccard distance at FDR < 0.05, and 87.8% had positive correlations; the median Mantel r for all AMR was 0.247. [src: amr_strain_variation] Non-core AMR genes had a higher median Mantel r than core genes (0.222 versus 0.117; paired t-test t = -8.35, p = 7.0e-16, n = 489), consistent with acquired resistance elements becoming lineage-associated, although near-universal core genes have little Jaccard-distance variance and can therefore produce attenuated distance-based correlations. [src: amr_strain_variation] This **refines** the cross-species argument: shared ancestry can structure AMR repertoires among strains within the same named species, but the contrast between non-core and core signal is partly a property of prevalence and metric variance rather than proof of a distinct biological process. [src: amr_strain_variation]

Phylogeny can also generate an aggregate association when both variables are concentrated in the same clades, even if the variables are not mechanistically related within those clades. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] In this dataset, Gammaproteobacteria contained 45% of all AMR clusters (37,752/83,008), while the taxonomic census recorded 4,992 Pseudomonadota species with AMR, 42,904 total AMR hits, 8.6 mean AMR hits per species, and 67.0% of species carrying AMR. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] These taxonomic concentrations make lineage composition a necessary consideration when interpreting pan-bacterial AMR summaries. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The [[summaries/prophage_amr_comobilization__REPORT]] **supports** this lineage-aware interpretation while showing that it is not the only relevant structure. Across 4,770 species, prophage marker density was positively associated with AMR repertoire breadth (Spearman rho=0.572, p<10^-300, n=4,770 species); after controlling for genome count, the partial Spearman correlation remained rho=0.464 with p=1.0×10^-253. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] The association was significant across all five reported major phyla—Pseudomonadota (rho=0.54), Bacillota_A (rho=0.55), Bacillota (rho=0.40), Bacteroidota (rho=0.59), and Actinomycetota (rho=0.29)—which supports a relationship extending beyond a single clade, while not proving phage-mediated AMR transfer. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] The report also found only a modest and threshold-dependent enrichment of accessory AMR genes near prophage markers, with an odds ratio of 1.10 at 10 genes and a median species-level odds ratio of 0.85, so species-level covariance should not be treated as evidence that local prophage proximity uniformly drives AMR acquisition. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

The same issue applies to comparisons of environmental AMR patterns. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Human/Clinical species carried 10.6 AMR clusters per species (n=2,248), compared with 4.6 for Soil/Terrestrial (n=2,469), 3.9 for Aquatic (n=1,827), and 3.0 for Animal (n=959), with a significant Kruskal-Wallis result (H=440, p=7.0e-93). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Clinical AMR was less core (30.8%) than soil AMR (58.1%) or plant AMR (63.1%), but these contrasts may combine environmental effects with differences in taxonomic composition, sampling, and genome representation. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The report identifies database sampling bias because genome collections over-represent clinical pathogens, potentially inflating AMR counts for human-associated species. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The [[summaries/amr_environmental_resistome__REPORT]] **supports and refines** this caution with a larger analysis of 14,723 bacterial species and 293K genomes: environment was associated with AMR burden and mechanism composition, but the effects persisted only incompletely across phylogenetic strata. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Five of six tested major phyla showed significant within-phylum environment effects, with Bacteroidota having the largest effect (eta-squared = 0.130); at the family level, 20 of 141 testable families showed significant effects after FDR correction, while most families lacked sufficient environmental breadth. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Thus, the new result **supports** a genuine ecological component beyond taxonomic composition, while **refining** the claim by showing that phylogenetic control remains limited by uneven sampling and incomplete within-lineage coverage. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The larger analysis also found that clinical species had a median of 5 AMR gene clusters versus 2 for soil, aquatic, and host-associated species (Kruskal–Wallis H = 781.9, p = 9.4×10⁻¹⁶⁷, eta-squared = 0.056), but it remained observational and subject to sampling and annotation bias. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The within-species study **further supports and qualifies** these environmental comparisons. Host-associated species carried more AMR genes per genome than terrestrial or aquatic species under both a BacDive keyword approximation and an NCBI keyword classifier, with Kruskal-Wallis p < 0.05; however, only 2 species had sufficient within-species environmental diversity for strict environment–AMR-ecotype testing, while 52.7% of genomes had no classifiable isolation_source. [src: amr_strain_variation] The direction of the host-associated contrast was consistent across classifiers, but the report cautions that clinical and human-associated overrepresentation, approximate environment labels, and sparse metadata prevent a general environment-to-ecotype inference. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The concept connects directly to [[concepts/pangenome-integration]], because interpreting pangenome openness requires integrating taxonomy, gene-family distributions, and environmental metadata rather than treating species as exchangeable observations. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] It also qualifies interpretations of [[concepts/environmental-resistome]]: environmental and clinical AMR differences should be tested with lineage-aware models before being attributed to environment alone. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The newer within-phylum, within-family, and within-species results **support** this qualification but do not eliminate it. [src: amr_environmental_resistome; amr_strain_variation] Associations between AMR burden and [[concepts/condition-specific-fitness]] should likewise be evaluated across comparable lineages and organisms, because cross-organism fitness measurements can inherit taxonomic structure. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

## Tensions

The aggregate result and the within-phylum results point in different directions: the overall openness–AMR correlation was near zero (rho=0.006), whereas openness positively correlated with AMR count in 8/10 tested phyla, including Bacillota and Bacillota_C. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] This is a tension between a pooled analysis that suggests little association and stratified analyses that show positive associations in multiple lineages; the report attributes the discrepancy to phylogenetic dominance of the aggregate signal rather than resolving it as a causal effect. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The prophage analysis introduces a related but non-identical tension: prophage marker density remained associated with AMR repertoire breadth across all five reported major phyla and after controlling for genome count, whereas the openness analysis found that openness did not predict the magnitude of environment or phylogeny effects. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization; pangenome_openness] These results concern different predictors and response variables, so they do not establish whether prophage density is independent of shared ancestry or whether the association reflects open-pangenome lineages acquiring multiple mobile elements. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

The openness-effects analysis introduces a related scope tension rather than directly contradicting the AMR result: openness did not predict either environment or phylogeny effect sizes in the matched species set, despite the AMR analysis showing lineage-dependent openness associations. [src: pangenome_openness; amr_pangenome_atlas] The two results concern different response variables, so they do not establish whether the within-phylum AMR patterns persist after explicit phylogenetic correction. [src: pangenome_openness; amr_pangenome_atlas]

The environmental-resistome analysis **qualifies rather than contradicts** the earlier environmental comparison: significant effects within five of six phyla and 20 of 141 families support an ecological association, but the majority of families were not testable or significant because of limited environmental breadth. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Its species-level classifications, sampling imbalance, and AMRFinderPlus-focused annotation also leave unresolved how much of the clinical contrast reflects lineage, representation, or unrecognized environmental resistance. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The within-species analysis adds a similar tension: visible environmental structuring in case-study AMR ecotypes was not matched by a general statistical demonstration because metadata and within-species environmental diversity were insufficient. [src: amr_strain_variation]

## Open Directions

- Use the existing species-level openness, AMR-count, and taxonomic data with phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS), a regression method that models covariance among related species, to test whether the openness–AMR association remains after accounting for shared ancestry. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]
- Combine the pangenome species table with a species phylogeny and fit within-phylum hierarchical models to ask whether the positive associations in 8/10 phyla share a common slope or instead arise from lineage-specific processes. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]
- Reanalyze the six-category environmental comparison using taxonomic matching or lineage-stratified permutation tests to determine whether the clinical, soil, plant, aquatic, and animal contrasts persist after controlling for uneven taxonomic sampling. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]
- Extend this matching to the 14,723-species environmental-resistome dataset and test whether the five significant within-phylum effects and 20 significant within-family effects remain under balanced sampling and explicit phylogenetic models. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Add environmental metadata and AlphaEarth embeddings for the currently under-covered genomes, then use phylogeny-aware partial association analyses to test whether environmental diversity predicts AMR count independently of lineage. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]
- Estimate AMR gene gain and loss rates on a dated or calibrated species phylogeny, and test whether pangenome openness predicts transition rates rather than only present-day AMR counts. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]
- Stratify the openness-versus-environment and openness-versus-phylogeny analyses by gene function, auxiliary fraction, Heap's law alpha, and pangenome fluidity to test whether the null result reflects an overly coarse openness metric. [src: pangenome_openness]
- Recompute the prophage-density–AMR-breadth association using dedicated prophage predictors, base-pair distances, all available genomes, and explicit phylogenetic models to distinguish shared ancestry, broad mobile-element propensity, and phage-mediated transfer. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]
- Within the 1,305-species AMR dataset, subsample species with more than 500 genomes and fit phylogeny-aware models to test whether within-species AMR Mantel signals and non-core/core contrasts persist without computational-cap and prevalence-variance artifacts. [src: amr_strain_variation]
- Curate collection dates and isolation sources, then repeat AMR ecotype–environment tests with balanced within-species sampling to distinguish ecological structure from lineage and metadata coverage. [src: amr_strain_variation]
