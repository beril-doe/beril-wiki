# Fitness cost of antimicrobial resistance genes

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes carry a small laboratory fitness cost, based mainly on one report, amr_fitness_cost, which analyzed 25 organisms in the Fitness Browser (FB). [src: amr_fitness_cost] A second, smaller line of evidence from the amr_pangenome_atlas cross-reference points the same way: AMR genes in the Fitness Browser look neutral or slightly less costly than other genes. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The meta-analysis finds a small, consistent shift in AMR knockout fitness, but no difference by mechanism or by core versus accessory status. [src: amr_fitness_cost, discoveries] The evidence comes from lab-adapted, mostly environmental strains measured under non-antibiotic conditions, so costs under antibiotic stress and costs of recently acquired mobile resistance in pathogens are untested here. [src: amr_fitness_cost, amr_pangenome_atlas] The prophage-proximal cost hypothesis could not be tested at all. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] See also [[summaries/amr_fitness_cost__REPORT]].

## Literature Context

The candidate papers broadly agree that resistance usually carries a biological cost in the absence of antibiotics, and that compensatory evolution can offset it. A review of resistance evolution states that resistance usually has a biological cost, but that compensatory mutations accumulate rapidly and abolish it, which explains why many resistances may never disappear [PMID 12190884](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12190884/). A later review reports that resistance generally reduces fitness, whether it arises by target alteration or by other mechanisms. It also notes that rare cost-free mechanisms and genetic linkage can stabilize resistance [PMID 16890008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16890008/). Experimental studies show compensation in specific systems. Examples are levofloxacin-resistant Mycobacterium aurum, where five of six mutants with substantial growth defects regained fitness after serial passage [PMID 32482677](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32482677/), and rifampicin-resistant M. tuberculosis, where NusG mutations raised fitness [PMID 38509362](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38509362/). In Streptococcus pneumoniae, pbp2b costs were fully compensated by pbp2x and pbp1a mutations [PMID 21379570](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21379570/). In Staphylococcus aureus, a plasmid imposed a significant cost, and the cost was relieved by deleting a 12.8-kb fragment that carried several resistance genes [PMID 33622733](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33622733/). A review of Pseudomonas aeruginosa likewise says that acquiring mobile genetic elements produces a fitness cost that compensatory mutations can ease over time [PMID 31492517](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31492517/).

Two caveats in the candidate set bear on how far lab costs generalize. One paper argues that laboratory experiments overestimate compensation in nature, because they miss the costs of compensatory mutations and the competition from sensitive strains that drives resistance down after treatment ends [PMID 25535278](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25535278/). A theoretical model suggests that compensation can occur even under stress, through exchange of high-cost resistance mutations for low-cost ones. This is a modelling result, not a measurement [PMID 40215274](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40215274/). One S. aureus study reports that fitness advantages were maintained or enhanced in penicillin-induced resistant isolates [PMID 40022365](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40022365/). The candidate abstracts report no pan-bacterial transposon-fitness comparison of resistance genes.

The corpus's main finding is a small, consistent cost. AMR knockouts across 25 Fitness Browser organisms show a pooled shift of +0.086, a relative difference from non-AMR knockouts, not an absolute selection coefficient. This is **consistent** with the literature that resistance carries a modest cost, and with compensation limiting it. The corpus's null results are less directly addressed by these papers. It finds no difference by mechanism (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.89) or by core versus accessory status (Cohen's d = 0.002). The candidates do not test these comparisons, so this part is best called an **extension**, not a confirmation. The corpus's reading that equal core and accessory costs reflect rapid compensation matches the compensation findings above [PMID 12190884](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12190884/). It remains a hypothesis. The plasmid study shows that costs can differ by element [PMID 33622733](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33622733/). The corpus did not test this for recently acquired mobile genes.

The corpus uses lab-adapted, mostly environmental strains measured without antibiotics. Costs under antibiotic stress, and the cost of recently acquired mobile resistance in pathogens, are untested. The pathogen studies above, and the warning about overestimated compensation [PMID 25535278](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25535278/), show that these are the settings where the corpus's estimate is least secure. The prophage-proximal cost hypothesis could not be tested, so the literature neither supports nor contradicts it.

## The size and universality of the cost

AMR knockouts show systematically higher fitness than non-AMR knockouts under non-antibiotic conditions, which the report reads as a metabolic burden. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The report describes this as the first pan-bacterial meta-analysis of AMR fitness costs using genome-wide transposon fitness data, built on 27M fitness measurements across 25 diverse bacteria. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Data assembly found 28 organisms with both AMR genes and fitness matrices. Of these, 25 qualified for per-organism tests (at least 5 AMR genes). [src: amr_fitness_cost] A DerSimonian-Laird random-effects meta-analysis (see [[entities/dersimonian-laird-random-effects-meta-analysis]]) across the 25 gave a pooled shift of +0.086 [95% confidence interval (CI): +0.074, +0.098], z = 14.3, p ~ 0. [src: amr_fitness_cost] All 25 of 25 organisms had a positive shift. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The central digest reports the same figure for 801 AMR genes. [src: discoveries] Heterogeneity statistics were I² = 54.3% and Cochran's Q = 52.54, p = 0.0007. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Per organism, 6 were nominally significant at p<0.05, and 2 (acidovorax_3H11 and Koxy) were significant at false discovery rate (FDR) q<0.05. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The median per-organism Cohen's d was 0.18, which the report calls consistent with the literature prediction of +0.05 to +0.20 for costs after compensatory evolution. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

### What +0.086 means

The +0.086 is a relative difference, not an absolute selection coefficient. It is the gap between AMR knockout fitness (−0.024) and non-AMR knockout fitness (~−0.11). [src: amr_fitness_cost] Random barcode transposon sequencing (RB-TnSeq) measures fitness relative to the pool average. [src: amr_fitness_cost] AMR knockouts therefore still grow slightly slower than the pool average. [src: amr_fitness_cost] A one-sample Wilcoxon test of AMR knockout fitness > 0 gave p = 0.999, so there is no support for AMR knockouts growing faster than the pool wildtype. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The report's per-organism Mann-Whitney design asks whether AMR knockouts are less costly than typical knockouts. [src: amr_fitness_cost] On that reading, AMR knockouts are 0.086 fitness units less detrimental than the average gene knockout. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The report concludes that AMR genes are small, consistent burdens across all 25 species, all mechanisms, and both core and accessory pools. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

### The Fitness Browser–pangenome cross-reference

A separate analysis used a DIAMOND-based Fitness Browser–pangenome link table (177,863 links at 100% sequence identity). It identified 178 AMR genes across 37 Fitness Browser organisms, yielding 29,386 fitness measurements. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Here AMR genes showed slightly less fitness cost than the non-AMR baseline (median fitness -0.007 versus -0.012, Mann-Whitney p=3.7e-6). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Beta-lactamases were nearly neutral (median -0.001), and singleton AMR genes were costliest (median -0.019). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] This is a different gene set and test from the meta-analysis, so the numbers are not directly comparable. The report reads the result as AMR genes in these environmental organisms being well-integrated intrinsic resistance genes, not recently acquired mobile elements. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] It also states that intrinsic resistance genes impose negligible fitness costs. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] It treats core AMR genes as an exception to its earlier finding that core genes are more burdensome overall, since they appear less costly than non-AMR core genes. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

## Mechanism, intrinsic versus acquired, and conservation

Cost does not depend on mechanism. Efflux (N=254), enzymatic inactivation (N=304), metal resistance (N=144) and unknown (N=74) groups did not differ (Kruskal-Wallis H = 0.65, p = 0.89). [src: amr_fitness_cost, discoveries] The predicted ordering efflux > enzymatic > metal > unknown was unsupported (Jonckheere-Terpstra z = 0.23, p = 0.41). [src: amr_fitness_cost] No pairwise comparison survived FDR correction. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Antibiotic-resistance and metal-resistance genes did not differ (Mann-Whitney U p = 0.87), and neither did T1 and T2 genes (p = 0.26). [src: amr_fitness_cost, discoveries] Mechanism classes are discussed in [[entities/efflux-pumps]], [[entities/enzymatic-inactivation-of-antibiotics]] and [[entities/metal-resistance-genes]].

Core (intrinsic, N=638) and accessory (acquired, N=163) AMR genes have virtually identical fitness distributions: mean −0.024 vs −0.024, Cohen's d = 0.002, Mann-Whitney U (MWU) p = 0.33. [src: amr_fitness_cost, discoveries]

Mechanism does strongly predict conservation (χ² = 69.3, p = 1.4×10⁻¹³). [src: amr_fitness_cost] Metal-resistance genes are 44% accessory, against 13% for efflux and 16% for enzymatic inactivation. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The report calls this decoupling of location from cost previously unobserved. [src: amr_fitness_cost] It suggests that conservation reflects how and when genes were acquired, not how much they cost. [src: amr_fitness_cost, amr_cofitness_networks] Efflux systems are often chromosomally encoded as part of general stress response and kept as core genes. Metal-resistance operons are frequently on mobile elements and stay accessory, with the same modest cost once established. [src: amr_fitness_cost] See [[concepts/intrinsic-versus-acquired-resistance-gene-conservation]].

### Core-designation caveat

Most FB species have few genomes in GTDB (median 9, range 2–399), so the ≥95% prevalence threshold for "core" is imprecise. [src: amr_fitness_cost] With 2–9 genomes, a gene present in all 9 could be labeled core yet be accessory at greater depth. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The null should be read cautiously in species with <20 genomes. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The report argues that this imprecision dilutes a real difference rather than creating a false null. It therefore treats the null as likely genuine, at least in well-sampled species. [src: amr_fitness_cost] These include K. michiganensis (399 genomes), B. thetaiotaomicron (287) and S. meliloti (241). [src: amr_fitness_cost] Separately, the Tier 2 classifier omits fosfomycin and tellurite resistance annotations, leaving ~25 genes in "unknown". [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Prophage-proximal AMR genes

Whether prophage-proximal AMR genes have distinct fitness costs (hypothesis H3 of prophage_amr_comobilization) could not be tested, because Fitness Browser organisms overlap poorly with the GTDB species analyzed. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] The Fitness Browser covers only 48 model organisms with RB-TnSeq data. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] The attempted comparison used the genefitness, gene and organism tables of kescience_fitnessbrowser. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] The report proposes revisiting H3 as coverage expands. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

## Interpretation and literature context

These explanations are hypotheses, not tested results.

- Uniform costs may mean only AMR genes whose cost was minimized by compensatory evolution persist. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- The uniform cost may be a "floor": the irreducible overhead of maintaining any extra gene. [src: amr_fitness_cost, discoveries]
- The +0.086 may be the irreducible cost after compensatory evolution: the minimum overhead of expressing a functional protein that gives no benefit without antibiotics. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Horizontal transfer may capture genes already cost-optimized in donors, or recipients may compensate rapidly. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Equal core and accessory costs are consistent with rapid compensatory evolution, even for recent acquisitions. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Retention forces (transfer frequency, environment-specific advantage) may differ from the forces governing metabolic cost. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- The modest transposon-based estimate may reflect many FB AMR genes being acquired determinants such as beta-lactamases and acetyltransferases, rather than costly target modifications. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Efflux genes may show the same modest cost as other mechanisms because compensation is a "general outcome". [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The report places a small cost in context. Real but small costs reconcile resistance decline under stewardship with persistence long after withdrawal. [src: amr_fitness_cost] A cell losing an AMR gene gains a small advantage that matters over evolutionary time but is invisible in short assays. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Andersson & Hughes (2010) predicted compensation would reduce or remove costs, making reversal unlikely. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Levin, Perrot & Walker (2000) demonstrated that compensatory mutations arise faster than reversion to susceptibility, predicting resistance maintenance at reduced cost. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Olivares Pacheco & Alvarez (2017) showed that efflux pump costs in *P. aeruginosa* are metabolic (proton motive force drain) and rapidly compensated through metabolic rewiring. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Melnyk et al. (2015) meta-analyzed ~600 measurements, finding costs in ~70% of cases and mean relative fitness 0.91–0.95 (5–9% cost). [src: amr_fitness_cost] Vanacker, Lenuzza & Rasigade (2023) found plasmid-borne resistance genes less costly than chromosomal mutations in E. coli. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Vogwill & MacLean (2015) found little systematic variation by mechanism after controlling for confounders, and emphasized environment-dependence. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This **supports** the report's mechanism-independence null. Durão, Balbontín & Gordo (2018) reviewed epistasis as a driver of cost variation across genetic backgrounds. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The report reads its consistent signal across 25 diverse organisms as suggesting the cost is robust across genetic backgrounds despite epistasis. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The report notes the isogenic-strain literature and its pooled-knockout difference are different measurement scales. [src: amr_fitness_cost] It still says both converge on small single-digit percent costs. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The comparison is reassuring but not a direct equivalence. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Limits

All 25 organisms are lab-adapted strains. Compensatory evolution may have reduced measured costs, so natural-population costs may be underestimated. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Conversely, Roux et al. (2015) showed that resistance genes, particularly efflux pumps, can be beneficial in vivo by exporting host antimicrobials, and the report cautions that its in vitro cost measurement may overestimate the true ecological cost. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

Fitness Browser coverage is also narrow. Only 37/48 organisms had AMR genes, and these are predominantly environmental strains, so the analysis does not capture the cost of recently acquired mobile resistance in pathogens. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The conservative 100% DIAMOND identity threshold avoids paralog confusion but may miss closely related alleles, such as those differing by a single synonymous substitution, and so undercount AMR fitness effects. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Costs under antibiotic stress remain untested, and the amr_pangenome_atlas report proposes using the full FB-pangenome link table to test them rather than only standard lab media. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] See [[concepts/linking-fitness-assay-genes-to-pangenome-clusters]].

## Open Directions

- Measure the same AMR knockouts under sub-inhibitory antibiotics, to test the cost-versus-benefit flip that this page does not cover. See [[concepts/antibiotic-dependent-fitness-flip-of-resistance-genes]].
- Repeat the core-versus-accessory comparison only in species with hundreds of genomes (such as K. michiganensis, B. thetaiotaomicron, S. meliloti). This would remove the sampling-depth caveat.
- Add fosfomycin and tellurite rules to the Tier 2 classifier and re-run the mechanism tests. This would show whether the ~25 "unknown" genes change the null.
- Compare cost in recently acquired plasmid or prophage-borne genes with chromosomal ones, to test the compensation and donor-optimization hypotheses and to revisit H3 as fitness coverage grows.
- Calibrate RB-TnSeq differences against isogenic competition assays for a few resistance genes. This would close the measurement-scale gap.
- Relax the 100% identity link threshold, for example to near-identical alleles, and check whether AMR fitness effects change.
