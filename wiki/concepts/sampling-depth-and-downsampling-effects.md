# Sampling Depth and Downsampling Effects

Genome sampling depth, clinical overrepresentation, and downsampling can substantially alter estimated relationships between environmental context and bacterial gene-content variation. Clinical sampling bias here means overrepresentation of human-associated genomes in a comparative collection, potentially making genomic patterns appear ecological when they instead reflect epidemiological structure, sampling composition, or unequal statistical power. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The [[summaries/ecotype_env_reanalysis__REPORT]] directly tested whether this bias explained the weak environment–gene-content relationship and found that the bias was real, but did not support the hypothesis that removing or accounting for it would reveal substantially stronger correlations. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

The [[summaries/env_embedding_explorer__REPORT]] **refines** this sampling-bias context: among 83,287 genomes with AlphaEarth embeddings, 38% were human-associated, including 16,390 Human clinical genomes (20%), 13,466 Human gut genomes (16%), and 1,669 Human other genomes (2%), whereas Soil, Marine, and Freshwater each contributed 6,073 (7%), 5,850 (7%), and 5,840 (7%) genomes, respectively. [src: env_embedding_explorer] Thus, the clinical skew is directly visible in the embedding subset, but its magnitude should not be generalized to the full pangenome because AlphaEarth covered 28.4% of 293,059 genomes. [src: env_embedding_explorer]

## Core Finding

The reanalysis used all genomes with AlphaEarth embeddings, including up to 3,505 genomes per species, whereas the original analysis used diversity-maximizing downsampling with a maximum of 250 genomes per species. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Across all 183 species, the reanalysis produced a median partial correlation of 0.081, compared with 0.003 in the original analysis, a difference characterized in the report as 27x. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] A partial correlation measures the association between environmental embedding similarity and gene-content similarity after accounting for the analysis covariates used by the project. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

The report attributes the discrepancy partly to different genome sets and the resulting changes in distance distributions, while cautioning that absolute correlation values should not be compared across the two methodologies. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] This **supports** the methodological principle that genome inclusion and sampling depth can change pangenome correlation estimates even when the biological question is unchanged, but it does not establish which method gives the more accurate absolute estimate because both the sampling strategy and genome sets changed. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

The independent [[summaries/ecotype_analysis__REPORT]] **supports** the weak-signal interpretation: across 172 species, the median partial correlation was 0.0025 for environment and 0.0143 for phylogeny; significant positive environmental effects occurred in 12 species (7.0%), significant negative effects in 4 species (2.3%), and no significant environmental effect occurred in 156 species (90.7%). [src: ecotype_analysis] Phylogeny dominated in 60.5% of species, while environment dominated in 39.5%. [src: ecotype_analysis] This **refines** the sampling result rather than resolving the absolute-scale discrepancy, because the analysis used a distinct species and genome subset. [src: ecotype_analysis]

Thus, sampling choices affect correlation distributions, but the available analyses caution against interpreting a larger environmental correlation as evidence of a stronger genome-wide ecological effect without matching the genome universe and procedure. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

## Clinical Sampling Composition and Within-Method Comparisons

Among 224 species selected for the reanalysis using at least 20 genomes with AlphaEarth embeddings and at least 30% coverage, 106 species (47%) were majority human-associated, 47 (21%) were majority environmental, and 71 (32%) were Mixed/Other. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Environmental categories included Soil, Marine, Freshwater, Extreme, and Plant; classification used a harmonized env_category mapping followed by majority voting over genome-level isolation_source assignments. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] This composition demonstrates strong clinical sampling bias in the AlphaEarth subset because human-associated species were the largest classification group. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

The majority-vote rule can classify a species with 51% gut genomes as Human-associated, so the analysis also used continuous fractions of environmental or human-associated genomes to reduce dependence on a categorical threshold. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

Within the reanalysis methodology, Environmental species had a median partial correlation of 0.051, mean 0.073, standard deviation 0.299, and range [-0.50, 0.78]. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Human-associated species had a median of 0.084, mean 0.110, standard deviation 0.226, and range [-0.30, 0.73]. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Mixed/Other species had a median of 0.109, mean 0.148, standard deviation 0.261, and range [-0.38, 0.69]. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Environmental species therefore did not show stronger environment–gene-content correlations than Human-associated species; the Human-associated group had a slightly higher median and mean. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

The one-sided Mann-Whitney U test for Environmental > Human-associated gave U=1536 and p=0.83, so the null hypothesis was not rejected. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The continuous Spearman analysis found no relationship between the fraction of environmental genomes per species and partial-correlation strength, with rho=-0.085 and p=0.25; the corresponding analysis for the fraction of human-associated genomes gave rho=0.030 and p=0.69. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] These results **contradict** the explanation that clinical sampling composition alone caused the weak ecological signal. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

The original analysis likewise found no significant difference between environmental and host-associated bacteria, with p=0.66. [src: ecotype_analysis] This **supports** the reanalysis despite the different workflows, while the systematic genome-level classification and p=0.83 result **refine** the earlier coarse comparison rather than making the analyses numerically interchangeable. [src: ecotype_analysis, ecotype_env_reanalysis]

These within-method comparisons **refine** the interpretation of the 27x discrepancy: sampling and extraction choices changed the absolute correlation scale, but the environmental-versus-human-associated comparison remained internally consistent under the reanalysis protocol. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The pangenome-openness null result **supports** treating openness as an insufficient explanation for these group-level patterns, although it does not test the specific within-method comparisons. [src: pangenome_openness]

The new explorer **supports** the existence of a stronger geographic embedding gradient in environmental samples without showing that this gradient produces stronger gene-content correlations: mean cosine distance rose from 0.27 within <100 km to 0.90 above 10,000 km for environmental categories, compared with 0.37 to 0.75 for human-associated categories. [src: env_embedding_explorer] This **refines** the within-method null comparison: stronger spatial structure in an embedding is not equivalent to a stronger environment–gene-content relationship, particularly when the human-associated and environmental groups differ in composition and metadata quality. [src: env_embedding_explorer]

## Missingness, Coverage, and Unequal Power

Of the 30 species with NaN partial correlations, the NaN rate was 10/47 = 21% for Environmental species, 13/66 = 20% for Mixed/Other species, and 7/100 = 7% for Human-associated species. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Environmental species were therefore disproportionately removed by NaN filtering rather than Human-associated species. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The report argues that this filtering would, if anything, bias the comparison toward finding a stronger environmental signal, which was not observed. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] This **supports** explicit missingness audits and connects the result to [[concepts/nonrandom-missingness-in-comparative-genomics]]. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

The unequal number of genomes per species remains a possible source of unequal statistical power because species with more genomes may be better able to detect weak correlations; the report presents this as a possible explanation rather than a demonstrated cause. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Species with more genomes were often clinical species, and the Mixed/Other group had the highest median partial correlation, 0.109, possibly because it includes diverse sampling campaigns, but this explanation was not demonstrated. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

The original analysis adds a separate coverage limitation: AlphaEarth embeddings covered only 28.4% of genomes, while geographic metadata were often missing or imprecise. [src: ecotype_analysis] This **supports** treating the weak environmental result as conditional on incomplete and noisy environmental representation rather than as proof that ecological adaptation is absent. [src: ecotype_analysis]

The explorer **refines** this coverage limitation with a larger coverage audit: AlphaEarth embeddings were available for 83,287 of 293,059 genomes, 79,449 had valid values across all 64 dimensions, and 3,838 contained at least one NaN. [src: env_embedding_explorer] Among the embedding records, isolation_source was available for 76,295 (91.6%), host for 53,091 (63.7%), env_broad_scale for 34,800 (41.8%), env_local_scale for 31,541 (37.9%), and env_medium for 31,483 (37.8%). [src: env_embedding_explorer] These unequal fields create additional opportunities for selection and unequal power beyond species-level genome counts; they therefore **support** joint modeling of genome count, embedding validity, and metadata completeness rather than treating embedding coverage as a neutral sample. [src: env_embedding_explorer]

## Embedding Signal Is Not Necessarily Ecological Signal

Environmental embeddings had a 3.4x geographic-signal ratio, whereas Human-associated embeddings had a 2.0x geographic-signal ratio. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Despite this stronger geographic signal, environmental embeddings did not correspond to stronger gene-content correlations. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] This **supports** [[concepts/environmental-embedding-ecological-validity]] by showing that embedding similarity should not automatically be interpreted as ecological similarity. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

The explorer **supports** the geographic-distance component of this interpretation: across 50,000 sampled genome pairs, mean embedding cosine distance increased from 0.41 for pairs <100 km to 0.82 for pairs 10K–20K km, with a plateau at intercontinental scales. [src: env_embedding_explorer] This **refines** the meaning of the 3.4x and 2.0x ratios: the embedding captures spatially autocorrelated context, but the distance relationship is strongest below 2,000 km and plateaus above 5,000 km, so raw geographic distance is not a simple ecological proxy. [src: env_embedding_explorer]

The report proposes, as a hypothesis rather than an established mechanism, that Human-associated species can show geographic structure because regional epidemiological patterns may cause different lineages of species such as Klebsiella or Enterococcus to dominate different regions. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Under that hypothesis, AlphaEarth embeddings may capture regional epidemiological patterns rather than ecological differences. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The result **refines** [[concepts/collection-site-versus-microenvironment-mismatch]]: geographic or collection-level context may encode real structure while remaining an imperfect proxy for the microenvironmental pressures that shape gene content. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The original analysis makes this limitation explicit for host-associated organisms, whose coordinates may describe collection sites rather than actual microenvironments. [src: ecotype_analysis]

The explorer further **supports** this collection-context caution: among 83,286 records with coordinates, 60.2% were classified as Good, 36.6% as Suspicious cluster, and 3.3% as Low precision; the heuristic flagged both likely institutional locations and legitimate field sites such as Rifle and Saanich Inlet. [src: env_embedding_explorer] Consequently, coordinate quality can alter apparent sampling depth and spatial similarity, and should not be treated as a resolved nuisance variable. [src: env_embedding_explorer]

## Interpretation for Pangenome Integration

The result **supports** [[concepts/pangenome-integration]] by showing that integrating environmental embeddings with gene-cluster data requires explicit control of genome-set construction and sampling depth. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] It also connects to [[concepts/ecotype-environment-gene-content]], because the environmental-versus-human-associated null result persisted after replacing the original sampling strategy with a full-genome extraction approach. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

The explorer **refines** this integration principle by showing that keyword harmonization mapped 5,774 unique isolation_source values to 12 broad categories but captured 71% of genomes with a label; 17% remained Other and 12.5% were Unknown. [src: env_embedding_explorer] The structured env_broad_scale field was cleaner but covered only 41.8% of the embedding subset. [src: env_embedding_explorer] Thus, changing genome inclusion is not the only source of correlation-scale uncertainty: changing environmental classification and retaining or excluding ambiguous records can also change the effective genome universe. [src: env_embedding_explorer]

The reanalysis does not show that downsampling is intrinsically invalid, nor that using all available genomes is intrinsically preferable. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Instead, the two approaches generated materially different correlation distributions whose absolute values should not be treated as interchangeable. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The ecotype_analysis **refines** this conclusion by suggesting that environmental adaptation may affect specific gene subsets rather than whole-genome gene content, and that AlphaEarth embeddings may not capture all ecologically relevant variation. [src: ecotype_analysis]

The [[summaries/core_gene_tradeoffs__REPORT]] **supports** this subset-level safeguard: core-gene burden was function-specific, with condition-dependent trade-off genes and costly-but-conserved genes identified from laboratory fitness measurements rather than as a uniform genome-wide property. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs] This **refines** the interpretation that weak whole-genome correlation does not exclude ecological effects on particular gene subsets, while also cautioning that the Fitness Browser conditions are biased toward experimentally convenient rather than necessarily ecologically relevant conditions. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs]

The analyses also **refine** [[concepts/condition-space-dimensionality]] and [[concepts/environmental-resistome]] as interpretive safeguards: changing genome inclusion and metadata classification changed absolute correlations while preserving the within-method group comparison. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Partial correlations assume linear relationships between distance matrices and may miss nonlinear ecological effects, so weak whole-genome correlation does not exclude environment-dependent effects on particular gene subsets. [src: ecotype_analysis]

The pangenome-openness analysis **supports** this integration principle but **refines** its scope: openness showed no significant relationship with either environmental or phylogenetic effect size, so open-versus-closed status should not be assumed to identify which driver dominates gene-content variation. [src: pangenome_openness] Because openness is a single summary metric and the analysis was limited to species with both openness statistics and ecotype results, this does not establish independence between pangenome structure and ecological dynamics. [src: pangenome_openness]

## Tensions

The principal tension is between the confirmed clinical sampling bias and the absence of a stronger environmental correlation after group comparison. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The AlphaEarth subset is clinically skewed, but the evidence does not support the claim that this skew alone explains the weak environment–gene-content relationship. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The explorer independently confirms the skew but also shows that environmental samples have stronger geographic embedding structure, so spatial signal and gene-content signal remain discordant rather than reconciled. [src: env_embedding_explorer]

A second tension concerns missingness: Environmental species had a 21% NaN rate compared with 7% for Human-associated species, yet removing more Environmental species did not produce the expected stronger environmental signal. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] This indicates that missingness, sampling depth, and ecological classification must be analyzed jointly rather than treated as interchangeable explanations. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The explorer adds 3,838 records with at least one embedding NaN and uneven metadata coverage, **refining** rather than resolving this tension. [src: env_embedding_explorer]

A third tension is between the large difference in overall correlation values and the stability of the within-method environmental-versus-human-associated comparison. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The reanalysis reported a median partial correlation of 0.081 across 183 species, whereas the original analysis reported 0.003, but the absolute values are not comparable because the analyses used different genome sets, sampling strategies, and distance distributions. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The ecotype_analysis provides a separate median environmental value of 0.0025 across 172 species and a phylogenetic value of 0.0143, but does not resolve the tension because its coverage and analysis population also differ. [src: ecotype_analysis]

The function-specific fitness evidence adds a related interpretive tension rather than resolving the sampling discrepancy: core genes were more burdensome in several functional categories but less burdensome for Cell Wall genes, and the Fitness Browser condition types were biased toward experimentally convenient conditions. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs] This **refines** the claim that sampling controls alone can explain weak whole-genome ecological associations: even with matched genome sampling, condition coverage and functional aggregation could affect which biological signal is visible. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs]

The pangenome-openness null correlations do not contradict the sampling-depth result: they test whether an openness summary predicts effect sizes rather than whether changing genome sampling changes correlation estimates. [src: pangenome_openness] They leave unresolved whether openness fails because pangenome structure is genuinely decoupled from eco-phylogenetic dynamics, because the metric is too coarse, or because the matched species sample and upstream effect estimates limit power. [src: pangenome_openness] Resolving whether the correlation-scale difference is driven mainly by downsampling, genome-set composition, extraction behavior, embedding coverage, condition coverage, or another methodological factor requires a controlled reanalysis. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis, ecotype_analysis, core_gene_tradeoffs]

## Open Directions

- Use the same species and genome universe to compare diversity-maximizing downsampling with full-genome extraction, using matched distance calculations, then test whether sampling strategy or genome-set composition explains the change from 0.003 to 0.081 and the reported 27x difference. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]
- Add genome count as a covariate to the species-level correlation model, then test whether unequal sampling depth inflates correlations in species with more genomes. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]
- Recompute correlations after matched-depth downsampling across species, then test whether the environmental-versus-human-associated comparison remains null when statistical power is balanced. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]
- Analyze transport, secondary-metabolism, and other functional gene subsets with identical downsampling and extraction settings, then test whether whole-genome Jaccard distances mask sampling-depth or ecological effects. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]
- Replace majority-vote categories with structured ENVO terms from env_broad_scale and repeat group and continuous-fraction analyses, testing whether finer environmental ontology changes the null result. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]
- Reassess species with NaN correlations using alternative missing-data and minimum-sample thresholds, and model NaN outcomes jointly with environment category, genome count, and phylogeny to test whether the higher Environmental NaN rate changes the group comparison. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]
- Compare AlphaEarth geographic signal with independent epidemiological and ecological metadata for species such as Klebsiella or Enterococcus, testing whether embedding structure reflects regional epidemiology rather than ecological differentiation. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]
- Using the same matched genomes, compare AlphaEarth distances with direct environmental metadata and test whether COG categories such as V-Defense and L-Mobile reveal ecological effects hidden at the whole-genome level. [src: ecotype_analysis]
- Reanalyze the 172 species with sufficient environmental and phylogenetic data using alternative embedding distances and direct environmental metadata, then test COG subsets and ecotype-cluster gene content to determine whether ecological effects are localized rather than genome-wide. [src: ecotype_analysis]
- Recalculate openness using auxiliary fraction, Heap's law alpha, and pangenome fluidity on the matched genome universe, then test whether functional subsets or lifestyle interactions reveal relationships hidden by the single openness metric. [src: pangenome_openness]
- Pair matched genome-set analyses with matched condition or functional-subset analyses, then test whether the observed correlation scale changes are attributable to sampling depth, condition coverage, or aggregation across heterogeneous gene functions. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs, ecotype_env_reanalysis]
- Repeat the environmental-only ecotype analysis after excluding ambiguous coordinates and records classified as Other or Unknown, then test whether the environment–gene-content relationship changes when spatial and harmonization uncertainty are reduced. [src: env_embedding_explorer]
- Compare keyword harmonization with env_broad_scale on the 41.8% of records covered by the structured field, using matched genomes and identical downsampling to test whether classification uncertainty contributes to the correlation-scale discrepancy. [src: env_embedding_explorer]
