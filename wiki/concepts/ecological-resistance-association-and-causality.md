# From Ecological Association to Causal Resistance Selection

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is unevenly distributed across ecological settings, but ecological association alone does not establish that environmental exposure caused resistance selection. This concept frames the evidence linking environment, AMR diversity, and resistance mechanisms while identifying the data and analyses required to distinguish causal selection from phylogeny, sampling, annotation, and isolation effects. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The central evidence comes from [[summaries/amr_environmental_resistome__REPORT]], which analyzed 82,908 AMR gene clusters across 14,723 bacterial species and 293K genomes in a pangenome collection. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Clinical-source species had a median of 5 AMR gene clusters, compared with 2 for soil, aquatic, and host-associated species, with a Kruskal–Wallis H statistic of 781.9, p = 9.4×10⁻¹⁶⁷, and η² = 0.056. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Human-gut species also had a median of 5 AMR gene clusters, and 13 of 15 pairwise environment comparisons were significant after false-discovery-rate (FDR) correction. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

## Evidence for Environment-Structured Resistance

The findings **support** [[concepts/environmental-resistome]] by showing that both AMR abundance and the composition of resistance mechanisms vary with ecological classification. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Clinical species had 68% accessory AMR, compared with 43% in soil species, with a Kruskal–Wallis H statistic of 506.0, p = 4×10⁻¹⁰⁷, and η² = 0.036. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The reported mean core and accessory AMR fractions were 57.1% and 42.9% for soil, 54.4% and 45.6% for aquatic species, 49.7% and 50.3% for host-associated species, 40.4% and 59.6% for other environmental species, 32.4% and 67.6% for clinical species, and 19.7% and 80.3% for human-gut species. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

Mechanism composition also differed across environments after Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Metal resistance represented 45.0% of AMR in aquatic species and 44.0% in soil species, but 6.1% in human-gut species, with η² = 0.107. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Target modification represented 43.6% in human-gut species and 27.5% in clinical species, but 6.2% in aquatic species, with η² = 0.100. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The corresponding Kruskal–Wallis statistics were H = 1498.4, p ~ 0, η² = 0.107 for metal resistance; H = 1394.9, p = 1.7×10⁻²⁹⁹, η² = 0.100 for target modification; H = 768.0, p = 9.8×10⁻¹⁶⁴, η² = 0.055 for efflux; and H = 266.0, p = 2.0×10⁻⁵⁵, η² = 0.019 for enzymatic inactivation. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The environmental pattern is consistent with, but does not prove, distinct selection regimes. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The report interprets soil and aquatic enrichment for metal resistance as consistent with natural heavy-metal exposure, while clinical and gut enrichment for target modification and, to a lesser extent, efflux is consistent with antibiotic selection pressure. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] This interpretation is further motivated by the reported contrast between metal resistance, which was 44% accessory, and efflux, which was 13% core, but the study did not directly measure exposure or selection. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

## Association Within Species and Across Phylogeny

The within-species analysis **supports** an ecological association but does not provide a per-genome causal comparison. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Among 823 species with genomes in at least 2 environments and at least 5 genomes each, the fraction of genomes from clinical sources predicted total AMR cluster count with Spearman rho = 0.465 and p = 2.2×10⁻⁴⁵. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Clinical-dominated species had a mean of 72.9 AMR clusters versus 16.4 in environmental-dominated species, a 4.4× difference with Mann–Whitney U test p = 2×10⁻¹⁸, and had grouped accessory fractions of 93.6% versus 81.7%, with p = 0.004. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The continuous correlation between clinical fraction and accessory percentage was borderline, with rho = 0.065 and p = 0.064. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

Deeply sampled species illustrate the magnitude of accessory variation but cannot by themselves identify its cause. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] *Klebsiella pneumoniae* had 13,637 genomes, 1,115 AMR clusters, 7 core clusters, and 1,108 accessory clusters, with 99% accessory AMR and clinical dominance at 80%. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] *Staphylococcus aureus* had 13,274 genomes, 642 clusters, 9 core clusters, and 633 accessory clusters, with 99% accessory AMR and clinical dominance at 85%. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] *Salmonella enterica* had 10,097 genomes, 836 clusters, 11 core clusters, and 825 accessory clusters, with host-associated dominance at 31%. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] *Streptococcus pneumoniae* had 7,944 genomes, 59 clusters, 1 core cluster, and 58 accessory clusters, with 98% accessory AMR and clinical dominance at 99.6%. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* had 6,673 genomes, 44 clusters, 4 core clusters, and 40 accessory clusters, with 91% accessory AMR and clinical dominance at 98%. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

Phylogenetic controls **refine** the association by showing that it is not entirely explained by broad taxonomic composition. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Five of six major phyla—Pseudomonadota, Bacillota_A, Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, and Bacillota—showed significant within-phylum environment effects on AMR, while Chloroflexota was non-significant. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Bacteroidota had the largest within-phylum effect, with η² = 0.130. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] At the family level, 20 of 141 testable families, or 14%, showed significant within-family environment effects after FDR correction, including Enterobacteriaceae at q = 3×10⁻²¹, Bacteroidaceae at q = 1.3×10⁻¹⁷, Lachnospiraceae at q = 2.5×10⁻¹², and Pseudomonadaceae at q = 3.9×10⁻¹². [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

## Continuous Environmental Evidence

Environmental embeddings **support** the discrete environment analysis but do not make the ecological variables mechanistically interpretable. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Among 2,659 species with both AMR data and AlphaEarth environmental embeddings, 52 of 64 embedding dimensions significantly correlated with AMR diversity at FDR < 0.05, with dimension A34 having rho = +0.24. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] A Mantel test found that environmental distance, measured as cosine distance between embeddings, predicted AMR-profile distance, measured as Bray–Curtis distance between mechanism fractions, with r = 0.098 and p = 0.001. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Stratified correlations were r = 0.177 for clinical species, r = 0.129 for soil species, and r = 0.061 for aquatic species. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Embedding coverage was 28% of genomes, and the dimensions were opaque, limiting causal interpretation. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

## Tensions

The strongest tension is between a robust ecological association and the absence of a direct exposure-to-selection test. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Environment and phylogeny remain deeply entangled, some families are nearly entirely clinical, and only 14% of tested families showed significant within-family effects. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] NCBI sampling overrepresents clinical isolates while soil and aquatic species are undersampled, which could inflate the clinical-versus-environmental contrast. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The direction of causality is unresolved: clinical exposure could increase AMR, AMR could contribute to clinical isolation, or both could reflect virulence-resistance co-selection. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The majority-vote species classification also collapses within-species variation, although sensitivity analyses at 60–90% thresholds partly mitigated this issue. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The reported species-level proxy is therefore compatible with causal resistance selection but cannot distinguish it from structured sampling, ecological sorting, or correlated host-associated traits. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

A second tension concerns whether observed mechanism differences reflect biology or annotation. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] AMRFinderPlus-focused annotation may underestimate novel environmental resistance and bias results toward clinical enrichment, and 15,550 AMR clusters, or 18.7%, could not be assigned to a mechanism from gene name or product annotation. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The 95% prevalence threshold used to define core AMR is also dependent on species genome count, making core/accessory labels imprecise for sparsely sampled species. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] These limitations connect this concept to [[concepts/annotation-dependent-resistome-inference]] and [[concepts/metadata-resolution-and-within-species-heterogeneity]].

## What Would Establish Causal Selection?

A causal claim would require resistance variation to track measured ecological exposure while accounting for shared ancestry, sampling, and gene mobility. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The current analysis explains 2–13% of variance in AMR composition across tested mechanisms, with η² = 0.02–0.13, while phylogeny likely explains more. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Thus, the results are strong evidence for environment-associated structure but only a hypothesis-generating basis for causal selection. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The pangenome-scale design is valuable because it links core/accessory status, resistance mechanisms, ecological metadata, and continuous environmental embeddings in one analysis. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Its causal limits mean that the next step is not simply a larger association test, but a design that compares closely related genomes or populations across measured exposure gradients and tests whether resistance acquisition, persistence, or fitness changes follow those gradients. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

## Open Directions

- Use the existing per-genome AMR calls, NCBI environment metadata, and phylogenetically matched comparisons to perform environment-specific gene identification, then ask which resistance clusters remain enriched after controlling for lineage and sampling intensity. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Use the AMR mechanism profiles and environmental classifications with PCoA and PERMANOVA, a permutation-based multivariate analysis of variance, to test whether environment explains multivariate resistance composition beyond taxonomic structure. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Reanalyze the 293K-genome collection with metagenome-assembled genomes and isolate genomes separated, then ask whether the clinical-accessory AMR contrast persists when genome-recovery and isolate-sampling processes are modeled explicitly. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Combine the 2,659 species with AlphaEarth embeddings and measured exposure metadata, using partial correlation or phylogenetically controlled models, to test whether interpretable environmental gradients account for the A34 association and the Mantel result of r = 0.098. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Reconstruct within-species comparisons from per-genome AMR profiles rather than the species-level clinical-fraction proxy, then test whether genomes from environments with higher antibiotic or metal exposure contain more acquired resistance after matching on lineage. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Expand mechanism annotation for the 15,550 unclassified clusters using independent annotation resources, then test whether the environment-dependent mechanism fractions change when the 18.7% unassigned fraction is included. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
