# Environmental structuring of the bacterial resistome

The bacterial resistome—the collection of antimicrobial-resistance (AMR) genes in a population—varies among ecological settings in abundance, diversity, core/accessory composition, resistance mechanism, and association with mobile genomic structure. These conclusions are conditional on catalog scope: the [[entities/amrfinderplus]] reference catalog includes stress-response genes alongside classical antibiotic-resistance genes, so catalog-defined AMR is partly a broader stress-resistance landscape rather than a narrow count of antibiotic resistance. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The central analyses examined 82,908 AMR gene clusters across 14,723 bacterial species and 293K genomes, while a uniform Bakta–AMRFinderPlus analysis identified 83,008 hits across 14,723 species; 53.2% of species carried at least one hit. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_pangenome_atlas]

The five most abundant AMRFinderPlus families were bla beta-lactamases with 6,115 hits, merA mercury reductase with 4,506, arsD arsenic metallochaperone with 2,611, merP mercury-binding protein with 2,222, and vanR vancomycin response regulator with 1,929. Mercury-resistance families contributed approximately 15,000 hits and 18% of annotations, and arsenic-resistance families another approximately 6,000. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Thus environment-associated AMR abundance and mechanism profiles are catalog-dependent measurements, not direct counts of clinically defined antibiotic resistance. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The analyses establish environment-associated structure, not environmental causation: exposure, phylogeny, sampling, isolation practices, annotation, and correlated host-associated traits remain alternative explanations. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] AMRFinderPlus-focused annotation may underestimate novel environmental resistance and favor clinical enrichment because clinical resistance genes are better characterized. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The 15,550 mechanism-unassigned clusters, 18.7% of all AMR clusters, are especially important because their environmental distribution is unknown. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

## Catalog scope and detection

AMRFinderPlus detection methods were HMM at 51.5%, BLASTP at 22.7%, EXACTP at 13.0%, PARTIALP at 9.7%, and ALLELEP at 3.0% of hits. Bakta product annotations and eggNOG hits were present for 93.0% of AMR clusters, while 7.0% had Bakta annotations only. Among sparsely annotated one-source clusters, 55.4% were singletons compared with 34.6% of two-source clusters. Zero AMR clusters had Pfam hits in the bakta_pfam_domains table. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] These results support [[concepts/bioinformatic-representation-coverage-bias]] and [[concepts/structural-annotation-gap]]: singleton calls may be true species-specific genes or annotation artifacts, and detection modality affects observed resistome composition. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

Mechanism classification in the atlas used keyword matching against AMRFinderPlus product descriptions rather than CARD Antibiotic Resistance Ontology mapping. Its table contained 18,448 Other/Unclassified hits (22.2%), 15,755 enzymatic-inactivation hits (19.0%), 14,338 efflux hits (17.3%), 12,824 beta-lactamase hits (15.4%), 6,955 target-modification hits (8.4%), 6,852 oxidoreductase hits (8.3%), 4,964 cell-wall-modification hits (6.0%), and 2,848 regulatory hits (3.4%). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The environmental-resistome analysis instead classified 15,550 of 82,908 clusters (18.7%) as mechanism-unassigned and excluded them from mechanism fractions; these percentages arise from different datasets and classification tables, not a numerical contradiction. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_pangenome_atlas]

The broader atlas covered 27,690 pangenome species and found AMR diversity associated with environment, prevalence class, phylogeny, lineage-associated ecotypes, and tightly co-inherited resistance islands. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas, amr_strain_variation] The within-species analysis examined 1,305 species and 180,025 genomes, producing 37,444 AMR gene-species records. Among them, 51.3% of AMR genes were rare, occurring in <=5% of strains; 41.3% were variable, occurring in 5–95%; and 7.5% were fixed, occurring in >=95%. The median variability index was 0.526 and median pairwise Jaccard distance—a distance based on shared versus differing gene repertoires—was 0.435. [src: amr_strain_variation]

This prevalence-based analysis supports the atlas-scale observation that AMR is relatively accessory: AMR was 30.3% core versus 46.8% for the pangenome baseline, the auxiliary genome was 2.2x enriched for AMR (33.6% versus 15.3%), and 63.7% of 4,252 species with at least 5 AMR clusters had AMR less core than their species baseline (Wilcoxon p = 1.1e-130; mean difference −0.102). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] It also refines that observation by showing how species-level conservation classes distribute among strains: 77.3% of Core AMR genes were fixed, 78.7% of Singletons were rare, and Auxiliary genes were 57.3% variable and 42.7% rare. AMR variability was weakly anti-correlated with pangenome openness (Spearman rho = -0.193, p = 2.2e-12), possibly because open-pangenome species accumulate more rare or singleton AMR genes below the 5% threshold. [src: amr_strain_variation]

## Environmental burden and metadata resolution

Clinical-source species had a median of 5 AMR gene clusters, compared with 2 for soil, aquatic, and host-associated species. The environment effect was significant (Kruskal–Wallis H = 781.9, p = 9.4×10⁻¹⁶⁷, η² = 0.056), with 13 of 15 pairwise comparisons significant after false-discovery-rate (FDR), a correction for multiple testing; the largest effect was clinical versus aquatic (rank-biserial r = −0.49). Robustness to 50%, 60%, 75%, and 90% majority-vote thresholds produced η² values from 0.044 to 0.056. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] This concerns annotated and classifiable AMR, not total resistance diversity, and does not show that individual genomes within a species share the majority environment or AMR profile. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The atlas supports clinical enrichment at broader scale: Human/Clinical species averaged 10.6 AMR clusters per species, compared with 4.6 for Soil/Terrestrial, 3.9 for Aquatic, and 3.0 for Animal species (Kruskal–Wallis H = 440, p = 7.0e-93). This used 7,838 of 14,723 AMR-carrying species with non-Other/Unknown labels; 46.8% remained Other/Unknown because of sparse and inconsistent free-text isolation_source metadata. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The environmental-resistome dataset contained 280,337 genomes with NCBI environment metadata; 93.5% were classified per genome and species-level majority voting covered 95% of species, or 13,981 species. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Majority voting can erase heterogeneity when genomes from one species occur in multiple source categories. In the within-species resource, NCBI and rule-based BacDive classifiers found more AMR genes per genome in host-associated species than terrestrial or aquatic environments, with human-clinical isolates highest. The NCBI classifier assigned environments to 1,190/1,307 species (91%), the BacDive approximation classified 459/1,307 (35%), and 52.7% of genomes had no classifiable isolation_source. [src: amr_strain_variation]

Among 823 species with genomes in at least 2 environments and at least 5 genomes per environment, clinical genome fraction predicted total AMR cluster count (Spearman rho = 0.465, p = 2.2×10⁻⁴⁵). Clinical-dominated species had a mean of 72.9 AMR clusters versus 16.4 in environmental-dominated species, a 4.4× difference (Mann–Whitney U p = 2×10⁻¹⁸), and higher grouped accessory fraction (93.6% versus 81.7%, p = 0.004); the continuous clinical-fraction/accessory-percentage correlation was borderline (rho = 0.065, p = 0.064). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] These are species-level summaries rather than direct genome-level environment-by-AMR comparisons; the planned per-genome Fisher’s exact test was replaced by this proxy because billion-row joins were computationally costly. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

Among 2,684 species with at least 3 genomes and AlphaEarth embeddings, environmental diversity predicted AMR count (Spearman rho = 0.466, p = 1.6e-144), while environmental diversity and AMR core fraction were negatively correlated (rho = −0.173, p = 1.8e-19). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] AlphaEarth coverage was only 28.4% of genomes in the ecotype analysis. Among 13,381 genomes spanning 224 species, only 172 species produced correlation results; its median partial correlation for environment was 0.0025 versus 0.0143 for phylogeny, and no significant environmental effect was detected for 156 of 172 species (90.7%). It found no significant difference between environmental and host-associated bacteria (p=0.66), while noting that host-associated coordinates may describe collection sites rather than organisms’ microenvironments. [src: ecotype_analysis]

The environmental-resistome evidence therefore supports an association between environmental composition and AMR burden, but does not identify which individual genomes acquired resistance, whether environmental transitions drive acquisition, or how much AMR variation exists among genomes sharing a species name. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Only 2 species had sufficient within-species environmental diversity for chi-squared testing after strict expected-frequency criteria. [src: amr_strain_variation] The strain analysis nevertheless found that 190 of 974 species with at least 15 genomes formed at least 2 distinct AMR ecotypes using UMAP, a nonlinear dimensional-reduction method, followed by DBSCAN; median silhouette score was 0.620. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_strain_variation] Case-study plots for [[entities/klebsiella-pneumoniae]], [[entities/staphylococcus-aureus]], and [[entities/salmonella-enterica]] showed visible environmental structuring, but sparse metadata make this a hypothesis rather than a general environment–ecotype finding. [src: amr_strain_variation]

## Core, accessory, and mobile AMR

Core AMR denotes clusters present in at least 95% of a species’ sampled genomes; accessory AMR denotes the remainder, and both labels depend on genome sampling. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Clinical species had 68% accessory AMR versus 43% in soil, with an environment effect (Kruskal–Wallis H = 506.0, p = 4×10⁻¹⁰⁷, η² = 0.036). Mean core/accessory fractions were 57.1%/42.9% in soil, 54.4%/45.6% aquatic, 49.7%/50.3% host-associated, 40.4%/59.6% other environmental, 32.4%/67.6% clinical, and 19.7%/80.3% human-gut. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The strain analysis supports the same graded interpretation: among 37,444 AMR gene-species records, 51.3% were rare, 41.3% variable, and 7.5% fixed; median variability index was 0.526 and median pairwise Jaccard distance was 0.435. [src: amr_strain_variation] Within the catalog, beta-lactamases were 54.9% core with p=7.7e-74 for enrichment versus baseline, regulatory genes were 6.5% core, and blaTEM, tet(C), and ant(2'')-Ia were 0% core. Intrinsic efflux pumps such as emhABC were greater than 95% core, whereas acquired efflux genes were accessory. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] This supports [[concepts/intrinsic-versus-acquired-resistance]], but the distinction depends on catalog and mechanism labels. Conservation differences do not imply different baseline knockout-fitness costs: among 801 AMR genes with fitness measurements, core/intrinsic genes (N = 638) and accessory/acquired genes (N = 163) had identical mean fitness values of −0.024, Cohen’s d = 0.002, and Mann–Whitney U p = 0.33. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The discoveries digest supports a graded rather than binary interpretation of conservation: across 7,609 metal-important gene records, 4,177 (55%) were genuinely metal-specific, 38% general sick, and 7% metal-plus-stress; metal-specific genes were 84.8% core versus 90.2% for general-sick genes (Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel p=0.011). The detailed analysis found pooled core fractions of 84.8% (2,969/3,500), 90.2% (2,183/2,420), and 94.3% (467/495), respectively. [src: discoveries, metal_specificity] All categories were core-enriched, but metal-specific genes were less enriched than general-sick genes. Metal-specific genes matched metal-resistance keywords at 12.2% versus 7.8% for general-sick genes (Fisher exact OR=1.64, p=2.4e-8). Approximately 14% of protein-coding genes, estimated at approximately 82% core, were putatively essential and absent from fitness data, biasing all categories toward core enrichment and making reported deltas conservative. [src: metal_specificity]

Resistance islands provide a structural explanation for some accessory organization. They were detected in 705 of 1,305 species (54%), with 1,517 islands overall, mean size 6.2 genes, median size 4, maximum size 43, and mean phi=0.827; phi is a pairwise co-occurrence coefficient, so co-occurrence indicates linkage rather than co-selection or functional synergy. [src: discoveries, amr_strain_variation] Of the islands, 1,343 (88%) contained multiple resistance mechanisms. Efflux pumps occurred in 954 and enzymatic inactivation in 698; complete mechanism counts were Other/Unclassified 1,026, Efflux 954, Enzymatic inactivation 698, Oxidoreductase 694, Regulatory 502, Beta-lactamase 341, Target modification 293, and Cell wall modification 137. [src: amr_strain_variation] These modules support [[concepts/cofitness-network-architecture]] and complement [[concepts/resistance-island-coinheritance]], but do not establish shared selection.

Prophage-marker density was positively associated with AMR repertoire breadth across 4,770 species (Spearman rho=0.572, p<10^-300), while across 27,702 species, 14,669 (52.9%) carried both AMR and prophage markers. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] Proximity was weaker: AMR genes within 10 genes of a prophage marker were 67.6% accessory versus 65.5% for distal genes (OR=1.10, one-sided p=0.005, bootstrap 95% CI [1.024, 1.185]); ORs were 0.78 at 3 genes, 0.92 at 5, 1.10 at 10, 1.19 at 15, and 1.28 at 50, with only 33 of 74 testable species above 1 and median species-level OR=0.85. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

The Gene Function Ecological Agora found, across 18,989 GTDB-r214 species representatives, 17,073,194 Sankoff-parsimony gain events and recent-to-ancient acquisition ratios of 24.5× for CRISPR-Cas, 10.3× for TCS histidine kinases, 9.0× for β-lactamases, and 2.3× for clean tRNA-synthetase controls. [src: gene_function_ecological_agora] Its MGE-machinery baseline was 1.37% of KO-bearing gene clusters, versus 0.57% for Mycobacteriaceae mycolic-acid KOs and 0.00% for both Cyanobacteriia PSII and Bacteroidota PUL KOs; PSII neighborhoods had 10.91% of focal features with an MGE neighbor within ±5 kb versus a Poisson expectation of 10.6%. PUL and mycolic-acid scans were deferred because of memory failures. [src: gene_function_ecological_agora]

The T4SS–CAZy analysis extended the mobility framework beyond prophages. Among 30,497 high-quality environmental MAGs, 6,652 (21.8%) carried T4SS or conjugative machinery under a multi-marker definition. Ninety-two CAZy families showed elevated co-occurrence with T4SS loci within <=10 kb; [[entities/gt2-glycosyltransferases]] was the top hit and occurred in 767 genomes with average length 5,041 bp. Associations were enriched in marine sediment (OR=5.5, q<10⁻⁹⁸), barley rhizosphere (OR=10.4), and maize rhizosphere (OR=4.1). [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] CAZy genes were not detected on plasmids by ICEfinder, while 12 integrative mobilizable elements occurred among the top 100 accumulators; T4SS-positive genomes had 10× higher mobile genetic element density (p<0.001). GT2 trees contained 77 detected HGT events, including 32 normalized high-confidence cross-phylum events; Node_4915 spanned 8 phyla at maximum divergence 4.843 and contained 35 genes, 82.9% syntenic. Sixty-five events spanned 2 phyla and 12 spanned at least 3; divergence and synteny were negatively correlated (Spearman ρ = −0.615, p<0.001). [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] These findings support environmental organization of mobility-related content but do not demonstrate T4SS-mediated transfer.

## Environment-associated mechanisms and metal responses

The assembled dataset contained 26,220 clusters assigned to enzymatic inactivation, 22,223 to metal resistance, 11,067 to target modification, and 5,224 to efflux; 15,550 clusters (18.7%) were unassigned and excluded from mechanism fractions. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] All four tested mechanism classes differed significantly among environments after BH-FDR correction. Metal resistance accounted for 45.0% of AMR in aquatic species and 44.0% in soil but 6.1% in human-gut species (η² = 0.107); target modification accounted for 43.6% in human-gut and 27.5% in clinical species but 6.2% in aquatic species (η² = 0.100). Statistics were H = 1498.4, p ~ 0, η² = 0.107 for metal resistance; H = 1394.9, p = 1.7×10⁻²⁹⁹, η² = 0.100 for target modification; H = 768.0, p = 9.8×10⁻¹⁶⁴, η² = 0.055 for efflux; and H = 266.0, p = 2.0×10⁻⁵⁵, η² = 0.019 for enzymatic inactivation. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

Across clinical, human gut, host-associated, soil, aquatic, and other environmental species, efflux fractions were 6.1%, 7.0%, 3.7%, 2.0%, 1.1%, and 2.7%; enzymatic inactivation 28.2%, 24.1%, 44.1%, 34.3%, 41.2%, and 33.6%; metal resistance 19.7%, 6.1%, 12.7%, 44.0%, 45.0%, and 24.9%; and target modification 27.5%, 43.6%, 22.8%, 10.1%, 6.2%, and 25.1%. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] These patterns are consistent with distinct selection regimes, but exposure and selection were not directly measured and incomplete annotation could distort mechanism profiles.

The global biogeography analysis geographically refines the soil-associated metal pattern. Among 30,497 host-associated-filtered environmental MAGs, 22,356 had usable coordinates and 2.8% carried at least one metal-resistance type. In a 5° grid, 11 hotspots and 3 coldspots were significant after Fisher testing and BH-FDR correction; the top hotspot was the Atacama/Andean region at lat=-25°, lon=-70° (21.8% prevalence, OR=9.83, q=7.6e-12, n=101 MAGs). Soil was enriched (5.8%, OR=5.05, q=1.8e-82), marine samples depleted (1.2%, OR=0.20, q=9.2e-80), and rhizosphere prevalence was 1.9% (OR=0.66, q=0.30, NS). [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]

Across 51,748 soil samples and nine metals—copper, cobalt, chromium, nickel, zinc, lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury—the soil functional-genomics analysis identified 2,355 significant COG–metal associations at FDR < 0.05. Chromium and lead produced the strongest signals, while ABC and RND transporters and biosynthesis genes dominated top hits. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] A db-RDA, distance-based redundancy analysis, yielded R² = 0.799 and p = 0.005 using 999 permutations; after conditioning on batch and project effects, metal concentrations explained 80% of community COG profiles. Unconditional metal-only R² was not reported and may be substantially lower. Biome-stratified PGLS found distinct metal–COG relationships in soil, marine, and wastewater environments. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

Across 452 metal experiments involving 37 organisms and 14 metals, 98.1% of 317 organism–metal-pair gene-level correlations were positive (311/317), and 99.1% statistically significant (p < 0.05); all 15 pairs tested in at least 5 organisms had greater than 90% sign consistency, with no systematically negative pair. [src: metal_cross_resistance] A separate classification found 4,177 (54.9%) of 7,609 metal-important records metal-specific, 2,888 (38.0%) general sick, and 544 (7.2%) metal+stress. Specificity fractions were 60.6% manganese (20/33), 60.5% molybdenum (185/306), 56.5% tungsten (173/306), 46.4% selenium (64/138), 55.9% cadmium (52/93), 51.9% copper (1,346/2,594), 50.2% cobalt (1,167/2,324), 49.3% chromium (132/268), 48.6% uranium (88/181), 47.2% zinc (843/1,786), 43.7% nickel (993/2,271), 42.4% aluminum (752/1,772), 32.7% mercury (35/107), and 21.9% iron (144/659). Seven of 31 metal-tested organisms could not be processed because locusIds did not match the fitness-matrix index; the 24 included organisms accounted for 7,609 of 12,838 records (59.3%), leaving 5,229 excluded. [src: metal_specificity]

Candidate prioritization identified UCP030820 (OG01015) as metal-specific in 2/3 organisms across 7 metals (67%) with mean sick rate 0.021; YebC (OG01383) in 7/12 organisms across 6 metals (58%) with mean sick rate 0.056; and DUF1043/YhcB (OG03264) in 3/6 organisms across 5 metals (50%) with mean sick rate 0.054. YebC’s proposed translation-bottleneck mechanism is a hypothesis. Among 149 novel metal-candidate families, 45.6% had dominant specificity of metal-specific versus 58.2% of annotated families (Fisher exact OR=0.60, p=0.003); ICA, independent component analysis, identified 0 metal-specific modules, attributed to scale mismatch and sparse experiments rather than absence. [src: metal_specificity]

## Phylogeny, ecotypes, and integration

Environment effects persisted within 5 of 6 tested major phyla—Pseudomonadota, Bacillota_A, Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, and Bacillota—while Chloroflexota was non-significant; Bacteroidota had the largest within-phylum effect (η² = 0.130). At family level, 20 of 141 testable families (14%) showed significant within-family effects after FDR correction, including Enterobacteriaceae (q = 3×10⁻²¹), Bacteroidaceae (q = 1.3×10⁻¹⁷), Lachnospiraceae (q = 2.5×10⁻¹²), and Pseudomonadaceae (q = 3.9×10⁻¹²). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] These controls refine rather than eliminate confounding.

Within-species phylogenetic structure was assessed by comparing average nucleotide identity (ANI) distance matrices with AMR Jaccard distance matrices using a Mantel test, which evaluates correlation between two distance matrices. Across 1,261 species, 701 (55.6%) showed significant signal at FDR < 0.05; the median Mantel r was 0.247 and 87.8% were positive. Non-core putatively acquired AMR had stronger median Mantel r than core genes (0.222 versus 0.117; paired t-test t = -8.35, p = 7.0e-16, n = 489). [src: amr_strain_variation] This supports the hypothesis that acquired resistance elements can become stably maintained and vertically transmitted within particular lineages, but core genes are nearly universal by definition and therefore have little Jaccard-distance variance, which can suppress distance-based correlations independently of biology. The pattern establishes structure, not whether it reflects vertical inheritance, repeated acquisition, loss, or lineage-specific sampling. [src: amr_strain_variation]

ANI extraction was limited to species with <=500 genomes, excluding [[entities/escherichia-coli]], with 15,388 genomes, and [[entities/klebsiella-pneumoniae]], with 14,240 genomes. Consequently, the phylogenetic proportions do not describe all species or genomes. [src: amr_strain_variation] The result is relevant to [[concepts/phylogenetic-confounding-of-pangenome-associations]], because environment or functional associations may partly reflect shared ancestry.

Across 606 genera, MicrobeAtlas found Pagel’s λ = 0.260 for total AMR cluster count, λ = 0.441 for core AMR fraction, and λ = 0.335 for metal-type diversity, compared with λ = 0.787 for niche breadth; nitrification showed stronger conservation (λ = 0.939 in bacteria and λ = 1.000 in archaea). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Among 2,659 species with AMR data and AlphaEarth embeddings, 52 of 64 dimensions correlated with AMR diversity at FDR < 0.05, with A34 top (rho = +0.24). Environmental cosine distance predicted AMR-profile Bray–Curtis distance (Mantel r = 0.098, p = 0.001), strongest for clinical species (r = 0.177), moderate for soil (r = 0.129), and weakest for aquatic species (r = 0.061); embedding coverage was 28% of genomes and dimensions were opaque. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The ecotype analysis found phylogeny dominated gene-content similarity in 60.5% of species, while environment dominated in 39.5%; environmental adaptation may act on specific gene subsets rather than whole-genome content. [src: ecotype_analysis] This is compatible with the AMR findings but limits inference that species-wide gene-content structure directly represents environmental differentiation.

The BERDL Data Atlas inventories 1,011,650,903 KBase pangenome gene rows spanning 293,059 genomes and 27,690 GTDB species clades or pangenomes, alongside 27,410,721 FitnessBrowser measurements, 83,287 AlphaEarth embeddings indexed to KBase genomes, and 97,334 BacDive strain phenotype profiles. It identifies 536 schema-level cross-tenant bridges across 29 canonical join keys, but only the FitnessBrowser–AlphaFold bridge was sample-validated. [src: berdl_data_atlas]

BacDive–GTDB matching found 42,227 of 97,334 strains (43.4%) mapped to 6,426 GTDB species with metal-tolerance scores; contamination-associated isolates generally had higher scores. A separate analysis matched 37,368 strains (38.4%) to 5,647 GTDB species. The full predictive model reached R² = 0.633 after adding n_metal_clusters, whereas taxonomy alone explained 35.4%, phenotype features alone 16.3%, and taxonomy plus phenotype 34.5%; in 5-fold phylogenetic-blocked cross-validation, the full model had R² = 0.633 and RMSE = 0.028, taxonomy alone R² = 0.354 and RMSE = 0.038, and phenotype alone R² = 0.163 and RMSE = 0.043. [src: bacdive_metal_validation, bacdive_phenotype_metal_tolerance]

The PGP pangenome analysis extends environmental gene-distribution evidence beyond AMR. Among 27,702 species, 11,272 carried at least one of 13 PGP markers; acdS, pqqC, and hcnC were enriched in soil/rhizosphere species, whereas nifH was depleted. acdS prevalence was 15.8% versus 2.6% in other environments (OR = 7.02, q = 5.1e-62), pqqC 43.8% versus 21.2% (OR = 2.90, q = 2.8e-53), hcnC 11.3% versus 6.4% (OR = 1.85, q = 6.1e-08), and nifH 12.9% versus 19.7% (OR = 0.60, q = 5.5e-08). All 13 PGP genes had higher core fractions than the 46.8% genome-wide baseline; pqqC was 81.5% core and acdS 70.4% core, while mean PGP accessory fraction was 29.7% versus 53.2% genome-wide. PGP richness correlated negatively with pangenome openness (Spearman ρ = −0.195, p = 2.0e-97, n = 11,272). This contrasts with AMR, for which only 30.3% of genes were core. [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology, amr_pangenome_atlas]

## Temporal and sampling constraints

Among 513 species with >=20 genomes spanning >=3 years after 1990, none showed a significant temporal trend in AMR gene count after Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction. Slopes were approximately symmetrically distributed, with 251 positive and 262 negative slopes. [src: amr_strain_variation] This null result is not evidence that AMR accumulation lacks temporal trends because collection dates were sparse and noisy and only 70% of genomes had parseable dates.

The GTDB/NCBI collection was heavily biased toward clinical and human-associated isolates, particularly for [[entities/klebsiella-pneumoniae]], [[entities/staphylococcus-aureus]], and [[entities/escherichia-coli]], while environmental species were underrepresented. AMRFinderPlus therefore missed novel mechanisms absent from its database, and collection bias may affect both lineage structure and generalization to environmental populations. [src: amr_strain_variation] The environmental-resistome analysis reinforces this caveat: NCBI sampling overrepresents clinical isolates, AMRFinderPlus may favor well-characterized clinical resistance, and only 14% of tested families showed significant within-family environment effects after phylogenetic control. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

## Tensions

The strongest tension is between robust ecological association and the absence of a direct exposure-to-selection test. Environment effects explain 2–13% of AMR-composition variance (η² = 0.02–0.13), but phylogeny, sampling, isolation, gene mobility, and annotation coverage may explain part of these effects. Clinical exposure could increase AMR, AMR could contribute to clinical isolation, or both could reflect correlated host-associated traits. Majority-vote classification collapses within-species variation. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The environment–AMR association persisted after majority-vote thresholds and phylum- and family-level controls, supporting an ecological signal beyond a simple species-label artifact. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] However, only 20 of 141 testable families (14%) showed significant within-family effects after FDR correction, and whole-genome ecotype analysis found environmental effects significant and positive in 12 species (7.0%), significant and negative in 4 species (2.3%), and absent in 156 species (90.7%). [src: amr_environmental_resistome, ecotype_analysis] The strain analysis found AMR ecotypes in 19.5% of eligible species, but metadata were sparse and only 2 species passed strict testing criteria. [src: amr_strain_variation] Thus broad ecological association remains compatible with weak or unresolved within-species environmental structure.

The positive relationship between AMR and phylogenetic distance in 701/1,261 species may reflect lineage-associated acquisition or uneven sampling of lineages across environments; available analyses do not separate these explanations. Geographic coordinates were often missing or imprecise, and partial correlations assume linear relationships between distance matrices, so weak environmental signal cannot distinguish absent adaptation from inadequate environmental representation. [src: amr_strain_variation, ecotype_analysis]

The broad AMRFinderPlus catalog and narrow antibiotic-resistance language remain in tension: the catalog detects metal and stress systems, while the narrow interpretation is weakened by mercury- and arsenic-resistance families and the 22.2% Other/Unclassified category. The environmental-resistome analysis reports 15,550 unassigned clusters (18.7%), not 18,448 (22.2%); differing denominators and vocabularies must not be silently reconciled. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas, amr_environmental_resistome]

Metal validation also remains in tension. BacDive reports higher metal-tolerance scores among contamination-associated isolates, including Cohen’s d = +1.00 for heavy-metal contamination (n = 10), whereas cross-resistance found no species-scale correlation between multi-metal tolerance and metal-associated isolation (Spearman rho approximately −0.02, p > 0.8), leaving only 20 independent species. [src: bacdive_metal_validation, metal_cross_resistance] The global MAG result is related but non-equivalent: soil had 5.8% prevalence and OR=5.05, marine samples 1.2% and OR=0.20; MicrobeAtlas found metal-type diversity associated with groundwater prevalence (ρ = +0.112, p = 0.0019) but not groundwater-specific fold enrichment (ρ = +0.042, p = 0.242). [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography, microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

Soil COG associations versus metal-specific interpretation are unresolved because chromium, copper, lead, and zinc co-vary in many industrial soils. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] The discoveries digest reports efflux at 21% of AMR in human-gut species and 1% in aquatic species, while the environmental-resistome analysis reports 7.0% and 1.1%; the 21% versus 7.0% and related 13% versus 44% core/accessory values require harmonized entity sets, filters, annotations, and denominators. [src: discoveries, amr_environmental_resistome, amr_fitness_cost]

Prophage density and local proximity provide non-equivalent evidence: density was strongly associated with AMR breadth (rho=0.572; partial rho=0.464 after controlling for genome count), whereas local proximity was modest and heterogeneous, with median species-level OR=0.85. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] T4SS–CAZy evidence supports dissemination as a hypothesis rather than demonstrated transfer: it reports 92 elevated co-occurrences, 77 GT2 HGT events, 32 normalized high-confidence cross-phylum events, and 10× higher MGE density in T4SS-positive genomes, but no CAZy genes on plasmids by ICEfinder and only 12 IMEs among the top 100 accumulators. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

Finally, DvH field-stress and field-core genes were enriched in the core genome, but field or lab fitness alone predicted poorly and ICA-module conservation was not associated with field-condition activity (rho=0.071, p=0.62). Fitness importance and gene length were more informative; heavy-metal-important genes were 71.2% core and lab-antibiotic-important genes 73.4%. The DvH analysis also found 678 essential genes at 80.1% core but lacking transposon fitness data, versus a 76.3% core baseline among 2,725 analyzed non-essential genes. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

## Open Directions

- Map the 83,008 AMRFinderPlus hits to CARD ARO terms; separate classical antibiotic-resistance genes from mercury-, arsenic-, and other stress-response genes; and repeat mechanism counts, core fractions, environmental contrasts, the 30.3% versus 46.8% core comparison, and the 2.2x auxiliary-genome enrichment. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]
- Re-annotate the 15,550 mechanism-unassigned clusters with broader homology, profile-HMM, and structure-based searches; compare independent resistance resources; quantify false-negative rates by environment and mechanism; and test whether the 18.7% unassigned fraction changes mechanism effect sizes. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Validate singleton calls with homology, domain, and synteny analyses, asking whether the 55.4% singleton rate among sparsely annotated one-source clusters represents artifacts or genuine resistance. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]
- Use the 280,337 genomes with NCBI environment metadata and a scalable per-genome AMR-by-environment contingency analysis, such as the originally planned Fisher’s exact test, to determine whether AMR enrichment remains after conditioning on species. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Use the 823 species in the multi-environment subset and hierarchical or mixed-effects models to separate within-species environment effects from between-species clinical representation, asking whether the 72.9-versus-16.4 mean AMR-cluster contrast persists within species. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Use deeply sampled species including [[entities/klebsiella-pneumoniae]], [[entities/staphylococcus-aureus]], and [[entities/salmonella-enterica]], with genome-level environment labels and accessory-cluster matrices, to test whether the reported 99%, 99%, and 99% accessory fractions are concentrated in particular environments. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Reanalyze the 190 species with at least 2 AMR ecotypes using curated isolation_source labels, phylogeny, and genome-level AMR matrices to test whether ecotypes correspond to environments after lineage composition is controlled. [src: amr_strain_variation]
- Subsample deeply represented species and species with >500 genomes, repeat ANI–AMR Mantel analyses, and use phylogeny-aware models to separate lineage inheritance from repeated acquisition and loss. [src: amr_strain_variation]
- Curate collection dates and isolation_source metadata, then apply temporal regression and adequately powered within-species association tests to distinguish AMR change through time from sampling composition. [src: amr_strain_variation]
- Use the 1,305 genome-by-AMR matrices and genomic-context mapping to test whether the 1,517 resistance islands reside on plasmids, chromosomes, integron boundaries, or insertion sequences, and whether physical context predicts persistence across lineages. [src: amr_strain_variation]
- Combine AMR ecotype assignments with virulence-factor profiles and metabolic pathway variation to test whether resistance-defined lineages also differ in pathogenicity-associated or ecological functions, and whether existing co-inheritance patterns predict future AMR gene co-acquisition. [src: amr_strain_variation]
- Reanalyze AMR repertoires with resistance databases beyond AMRFinderPlus and replace keyword-based mechanism labels with CARD ARO mappings to quantify effects on rare, variable, fixed, core-fraction, and ecotype estimates. [src: amr_strain_variation, amr_pangenome_atlas]
- Reanalyze the 172 ecotype-analysis species using direct environmental metadata and alternative embedding distances, then test COG functional subsets rather than whole-genome gene content. [src: ecotype_analysis]
- Harmonize core/accessory thresholds, mechanism annotations, genome sampling, the 21% versus 7.0% efflux estimates, and all denominator differences; test whether the clinical 68% versus soil 43% accessory contrast persists under matched sampling. [src: discoveries, amr_environmental_resistome, amr_fitness_cost]
- Compare catalog-defined gradients against NMDC and MGnify metagenomes after CARD ARO harmonization and metadata-stratified modeling. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas, amr_strain_variation]
- Map the resistance islands to plasmids, chromosomes, integrons, ICEs, and insertion sequences, then test whether mechanism composition predicts persistence or acquisition after controlling for phylogeny. [src: amr_strain_variation, discoveries]
- Match BacDive accessions directly to pangenome genome_ids, expand MIC and growth-inhibition extraction, and apply PGLS or phylogenetic PCA; resolve the metal-isolation tension with matched metadata, KEGG/PFAM signatures, concentration-normalized fitness, non-metal stress controls, and independent validation. [src: bacdive_metal_validation, bacdive_phenotype_metal_tolerance, metal_cross_resistance, metal_specificity]
- Fit partial-correlation models such as COG ~ Cr | Cu + Zn + Pb, classify COGs into resistance, stress, membrane, energy, and unknown categories, report conditional and unconditional db-RDA R², and test Moran’s I and spatial residuals. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]
- Validate global biogeography with sample-accession checks, prevalence normalization by log(n_MAGs), biome-specific missingness tests, and sampling-effort correction; reanalyze MicrobeAtlas with finer habitats, alternative phylogenetic or mixed models, primer-detection correction, rarefaction, and experimental validation. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography, microbeatlas_metal_ecology]
- Replace broad ENIGMA COG-fraction proxies with curated metal-stress gene sets while controlling for abundance, depth, location, date, and compositional structure; load registered SSO geochemistry samples into CORAL and repeat AMR profiling. [src: enigma_contamination_functional_potential, enigma_sso_asv_ecology]
- Apply field/lab fitness–conservation analyses to additional organisms while controlling for gene length and essential-gene exclusion. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Execute value-space-validated NMDC–PROTECT and ENIGMA–PhageFoundry bridges and harmonize pangenome, AlphaEarth, BacDive, and environmental identifiers. [src: berdl_data_atlas]
- Test whether PGP soil-enrichment signals and AMR mechanism profiles persist after matched phylum, genome-size, sampling-effort, and environment-label controls; analyze pqqC–acdS and hcnA–hcnC neighborhoods and phylogenies. [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology]
- Reanalyze genomes from [[entities/klebsiella-pneumoniae]], [[entities/acinetobacter-baumannii]], [[entities/pseudomonas-aeruginosa]], and [[entities/escherichia-coli]] with dedicated prophage predictors, scaffold-based distances, exhaustive sampling, and explicit plasmid/ICE controls; apply geNomad or PHASTER and test whether density associations persist after controlling for pangenome openness, genome count, phylogeny, plasmids, and ICEs. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]
- Run the pending T4SS–CAZy synteny-threshold permutation test, validate Node_4915 by BLAST against NCBI nr, establish a housekeeping-gene null, calculate θ = OR(T4SS-CAZy) / [OR(T4SS) × OR(CAZy)], and perform experimental transfer assays with base-pair-resolution neighborhoods.
