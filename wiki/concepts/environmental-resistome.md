# Environmental structuring of the bacterial resistome

The bacterial resistome—the collection of antimicrobial-resistance (AMR) genes in a population—varies among ecological settings in abundance, diversity, core/accessory composition, resistance mechanism, and association with mobile genomic structure. The central pangenome analysis examined 82,908 AMR gene clusters across 14,723 bacterial species and 293K genomes; a uniform Bakta–[[entities/amrfinderplus]] analysis identified 83,008 AMRFinderPlus hits across 14,723 species, with 53.2% of species carrying at least one hit. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_pangenome_atlas] These results establish environment-associated structure, not environmental causation: exposure, phylogeny, sampling, isolation practices, annotation, and correlated host-associated traits remain alternative explanations. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

Ecological comparisons are also comparisons of what annotation pipelines can recognize. AMRFinderPlus-focused annotation may underestimate novel environmental resistance and favor clinical enrichment because resistance genes from clinical contexts are better characterized. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Thus an apparent environmental resistome pattern can be strengthened, weakened, or redirected when unrecognized genes and incomplete functional labels are included, linking this analysis to [[concepts/bioinformatic-representation-coverage-bias]] and [[concepts/structural-annotation-gap]]. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The 15,550 mechanism-unassigned clusters, representing 18.7% of all AMR clusters, are particularly important because their environmental distribution is unknown. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The broader atlas covered 27,690 pangenome species and found that AMR diversity was associated with environment, prevalence class, phylogeny, lineage-associated ecotypes, and tightly co-inherited resistance islands. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas, amr_strain_variation] Prophage analysis **refines** this framework by adding genome structure: across 27,702 species, 14,669 (52.9%) carried both AMR and prophage markers, and prophage-marker density was positively associated with AMR repertoire breadth across 4,770 species (Spearman rho=0.572, p<10^-300). [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] This does not show that prophages caused or transferred AMR genes; shared recombination potential or open pangenomes could increase both quantities. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

The [[entities/type-iv-secretion-system]]–CAZy analysis **extends** the genome-structure framework beyond prophage markers. Among 30,497 high-quality environmental MAGs, 6,652 (21.8%) carried T4SS or conjugative machinery under a multi-marker definition. Ninety-two CAZy families showed elevated co-occurrence with T4SS loci within ≤10 kb, with [[entities/gt2-glycosyltransferases]] the top hit; GT2 occurred in 767 genomes with an average length of 5,041 bp. Associations were enriched in marine sediment (OR=5.5, q<10⁻⁹⁸), barley rhizosphere (OR=10.4), and maize rhizosphere (OR=4.1). [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] This **supports** environmental organization of mobility-related content, but the associations remain observational and threshold validation is pending. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

## Environment-associated AMR diversity

Clinical-source species had a median of 5 AMR gene clusters, compared with 2 for soil, aquatic, and host-associated species. The overall environment effect was significant (Kruskal–Wallis H = 781.9, p = 9.4×10⁻¹⁶⁷, η² = 0.056), with 13 of 15 pairwise comparisons significant after false-discovery-rate (FDR) correction; the largest effect was clinical versus aquatic (rank-biserial r = −0.49). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The association was robust to majority-vote thresholds of 50%, 60%, 75%, and 90%, with η² values from 0.044 to 0.056. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] However, this is a result about annotated and classifiable AMR, not a direct estimate of total resistance diversity, because clinically characterized genes may be easier to recognize than novel environmental genes. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The atlas **supports** clinical enrichment at broader scale: Human/Clinical species averaged 10.6 AMR clusters per species, compared with 4.6 for Soil/Terrestrial, 3.9 for Aquatic, and 3.0 for Animal species (Kruskal–Wallis H = 440, p = 7.0e-93). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] This used 7,838 of 14,723 AMR-carrying species with non-Other/Unknown labels, so categories and denominators are not interchangeable. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Among 2,684 species with at least 3 genomes and AlphaEarth embeddings, environmental diversity predicted AMR count (Spearman rho = 0.466, p = 1.6e-144), while environmental diversity and AMR core fraction were negatively correlated (rho = −0.173, p = 1.8e-19). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

Within-species analysis **supports** the host-associated direction but not a per-genome causal comparison. Rule-based BacDive and NCBI classifiers found more AMR genes per genome in host-associated species than terrestrial or aquatic environments, with human-clinical isolates highest; the NCBI classifier assigned environments to 1,190/1,307 species (91%), the BacDive approximation classified 459/1,307 (35%), and 52.7% of genomes had no classifiable isolation_source. [src: amr_strain_variation]

Among 823 species with genomes in at least 2 environments and at least 5 genomes per environment, clinical genome fraction predicted total AMR cluster count (Spearman rho = 0.465, p = 2.2×10⁻⁴⁵). Clinical-dominated species had a mean of 72.9 AMR clusters versus 16.4 in environmental-dominated species, a 4.4× difference (Mann–Whitney U p = 2×10⁻¹⁸), and higher grouped accessory fraction (93.6% versus 81.7%, p = 0.004); the continuous clinical-fraction/accessory-percentage correlation was borderline (rho = 0.065, p = 0.064). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] This species-level proxy is compatible with causal resistance selection, but also with structured sampling, ecological sorting, virulence–resistance co-selection, and differential annotation coverage. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

Deeply sampled species illustrate accessory variation: [[entities/klebsiella-pneumoniae]] had 13,637 genomes and 1,115 AMR clusters, of which 7 were core and 1,108 accessory (99%); [[entities/staphylococcus-aureus]] had 13,274 genomes and 642 clusters, of which 9 were core and 633 accessory (99%); [[entities/salmonella-enterica]] had 10,097 genomes and 836 clusters, of which 11 were core and 825 accessory (99%); [[entities/streptococcus-pneumoniae]] had 7,944 genomes and 59 clusters, of which 1 was core and 58 accessory (98%); and [[entities/mycobacterium-tuberculosis]] had 6,673 genomes and 44 clusters, of which 4 were core and 40 accessory (91%). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Among 974 species with at least 15 genomes, 190 (19.5%) formed at least 2 distinct AMR ecotypes using UMAP, a nonlinear dimensional-reduction method, followed by DBSCAN; median silhouette score was 0.620. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The MicrobeAtlas result **refines** these patterns by showing that the relevant predictor was metal-type breadth rather than total AMR burden. The raw OLS correlation between mean metal type diversity and Levins’ B_std was r ≈ 0.21, while PGLS, a phylogenetic generalized least-squares regression, remained positive after accounting for ancestry, pangenome coverage, genome size, and species count. With genome size included, metal type diversity had β = +0.0218, SE = 0.0061, p = 3.6×10⁻⁴, and the full model was favored by ΔAIC = 10.8; in the three-covariate model using 527 genera, metal types had p = 3.5×10⁻⁴ and BH-FDR q = 0.003. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The niche measure is nevertheless a sequencing-effort proxy rather than confirmed ecological range. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

GT2-neighborhood MAGs **support but qualify** the environmental-breadth interpretation: n=376 had a mean of 0.045 metal-resistance types, compared with 0.004 for non-GT2 MAGs (n=260,276; Mann–Whitney p=8.6e-27), and genomes with GT2 in T4SS-proximal neighborhoods carried 11× more metal-resistance genes. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] This is not equivalent to the MicrobeAtlas genus-level association and does not establish that T4SS or GT2 transfer causes metal resistance. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

## Core and accessory AMR

Core AMR denotes clusters present in at least 95% of a species’ sampled genomes; accessory AMR denotes the remainder, and both labels depend on genome sampling. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Clinical species had 68% accessory AMR versus 43% in soil species, with a significant environment effect (Kruskal–Wallis H = 506.0, p = 4×10⁻¹⁰⁷, η² = 0.036). Mean core/accessory fractions were 57.1%/42.9% in soil, 54.4%/45.6% aquatic, 49.7%/50.3% host-associated, 40.4%/59.6% other environmental, 32.4%/67.6% clinical, and 19.7%/80.3% human-gut. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The 95% threshold is dependent on species genome count, so sparsely sampled species have imprecise core/accessory labels; the clinical-versus-soil contrast may therefore reflect both biology and unequal sampling or recognition of resistance genes. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The atlas **supports** this gradient: only 30.3% of AMR genes were core versus 46.8% for the pangenome baseline (OR = 0.49, chi-squared = 23,117, p≈0), while the auxiliary genome was 2.2x enriched for AMR (33.6% versus 15.3%). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] In 4,252 species with at least 5 AMR clusters, 63.7% had AMR less core than their species baseline (Wilcoxon p = 1.1e-130; mean difference −0.102). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Among 37,444 AMR gene-species records, 51.3% were rare, 41.3% variable, and 7.5% fixed; the median variability index was 0.526 and median pairwise Jaccard distance was 0.435. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The discoveries digest **supports** the accessory interpretation while separating metal-specific from general stress responses: across 7,609 metal-important gene records, 4,177 (55%) were genuinely metal-specific, 38% general sick, and 7% metal-plus-stress; metal-specific genes were 84.8% core versus 90.2% for general-sick genes (Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel p=0.011). [src: discoveries] The detailed analysis found, among 22 organisms with pangenome links, pooled core fractions of 84.8% (2,969/3,500) for metal-specific genes, 90.2% (2,183/2,420) for general-sick genes, and 94.3% (467/495) for metal+stress genes. [src: metal_specificity] All categories were significantly core-enriched above baseline, but metal-specific genes were less enriched than general-sick genes. [src: metal_specificity] This supports a graded model in which broadly stressful functions are more core-enriched than specialized metal-resistance functions, while both remain strongly core-associated. [src: metal_specificity]

Metal-specific genes matched metal-resistance keywords at 12.2% versus 7.8% for general-sick genes (Fisher exact OR=1.64, p=2.4e-8). [src: metal_specificity] Approximately 14% of protein-coding genes, estimated at approximately 82% core, were putatively essential and absent from fitness data, biasing all categories toward core enrichment and making reported deltas conservative. [src: metal_specificity]

The Fitness Browser–pangenome analysis **refines** this interpretation: among 801 AMR genes with fitness measurements, core/intrinsic genes (N = 638) and accessory/acquired genes (N = 163) had identical mean fitness values of −0.024, Cohen’s d = 0.002, and Mann–Whitney U p = 0.33. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Conservation differences therefore do not imply different baseline knockout-fitness costs. The DvH analysis likewise found 678 essential genes at 80.1% core but lacking transposon fitness data, versus a 76.3% core baseline among 2,725 analyzed non-essential genes. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

Prophage proximity **refines** the accessory interpretation: AMR genes within 10 genes of a prophage marker were 67.6% accessory, compared with 65.5% for distal AMR genes; Fisher’s exact test gave OR=1.10, one-sided p=0.005, with bootstrap 95% CI [1.024, 1.185]. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] The effect was threshold-dependent: ORs were 0.78 at 3 genes, 0.92 at 5, 1.10 at 10, 1.19 at 15, and 1.28 at 50; only 33 of 74 testable species had species-level ORs above 1, and the median species-level OR was 0.85. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

The T4SS–CAZy findings **refine** mobile-structure interpretation in a different direction. In 376 genomes, T4SS occurred in 503 neighborhood entries and GT2 in 495; GH23 was the second most common CAZy family in GT2 neighborhoods, with 106 occurrences. CAZy genes were not detected on plasmids by ICEfinder, while 12 integrative mobilizable elements occurred among the top 100 accumulators. T4SS-positive genomes had 10× higher mobile genetic element density than other genomes (p<0.001). [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] This is compatible with chromosomal or integrative transfer rather than a simple plasmid effect, but does not demonstrate transfer. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

## Environment-associated resistance mechanisms

The assembled dataset contained 26,220 clusters assigned to enzymatic inactivation, 22,223 to metal resistance, 11,067 to target modification, and 5,224 to efflux. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The remaining 15,550 clusters (18.7%) could not be assigned to a mechanism from gene name or product annotation and were excluded from mechanism fractions. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Because these excluded clusters may not be distributed evenly across environments, the mechanism comparisons are best treated as comparisons of annotated and classifiable AMR rather than a complete census. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

All four tested mechanism classes differed significantly among environments after BH FDR correction. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Metal resistance accounted for 45.0% of AMR in aquatic species and 44.0% in soil, but 6.1% in human-gut species (η² = 0.107); target modification accounted for 43.6% in human-gut and 27.5% in clinical species, but 6.2% in aquatic species (η² = 0.100). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The corresponding statistics were H = 1498.4, p ~ 0, η² = 0.107 for metal resistance; H = 1394.9, p = 1.7×10⁻²⁹⁹, η² = 0.100 for target modification; H = 768.0, p = 9.8×10⁻¹⁶⁴, η² = 0.055 for efflux; and H = 266.0, p = 2.0×10⁻⁵⁵, η² = 0.019 for enzymatic inactivation. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

Across clinical, human gut, host-associated, soil, aquatic, and other environmental species, efflux fractions were 6.1%, 7.0%, 3.7%, 2.0%, 1.1%, and 2.7%; enzymatic inactivation 28.2%, 24.1%, 44.1%, 34.3%, 41.2%, and 33.6%; metal resistance 19.7%, 6.1%, 12.7%, 44.0%, 45.0%, and 24.9%; and target modification 27.5%, 43.6%, 22.8%, 10.1%, 6.2%, and 25.1%. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] These patterns are consistent with distinct selection regimes—natural heavy-metal exposure in soil and aquatic settings, and antibiotic selection in clinical and gut settings—but exposure and selection were not directly measured, and incomplete annotation could distort the apparent mechanism profile. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The global biogeography analysis **supports and geographically refines** the soil-associated metal pattern. Among 30,497 host-associated-filtered environmental MAGs, 22,356 had usable coordinates and 2.8% carried at least one metal-resistance type. In a 5° grid, 11 hotspots and 3 coldspots were significant after Fisher testing and BH FDR correction; the top hotspot was the Atacama/Andean region at lat=-25°, lon=-70° (21.8% prevalence, OR=9.83, q=7.6e-12, n=101 MAGs). Soil was enriched (5.8%, OR=5.05, q=1.8e-82), marine samples were depleted (1.2%, OR=0.20, q=9.2e-80), and rhizosphere prevalence was 1.9% (OR=0.66, q=0.30, NS). [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography] Hotspot interpretation remains preliminary pending sampling-effort correction and expedition-artifact checks. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]

The soil functional-genomics analysis **extends** the mechanism evidence from detected resistance genes to broader functional content. Across 51,748 soil samples and nine metals—copper, cobalt, chromium, nickel, zinc, lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury—it identified 2,355 significant COG–metal associations at FDR < 0.05; COG means a Cluster of Orthologous Groups functional category and FDR means false discovery rate. Chromium and lead produced the strongest signals, while ABC and RND transporters and biosynthesis genes dominated top hits. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] These COG shifts cannot be counted as direct resistance genes because metals co-vary and mechanistic classification was incomplete. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

A db-RDA, or distance-based redundancy analysis, yielded R² = 0.799 and p = 0.005 using 999 permutations; after conditioning on batch and project effects, metal concentrations explained 80% of the variance in community COG profiles. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] This conditional R² is not total variance explained: unconditional metal-only R² was not reported and may be substantially lower. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] Biome-stratified PGLS found distinct metal–COG relationships in soil, marine, and wastewater environments, supporting environment-specific responses rather than a universal resistance programme. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

The metal analyses **refine** the mechanism distinction. Across 452 metal experiments involving 37 organisms and 14 metals, 98.1% of 317 organism–metal-pair gene-level correlations were positive (311/317), and 99.1% were statistically significant (p < 0.05); all 15 pairs tested in at least 5 organisms had greater than 90% sign consistency, with no systematically negative pair. [src: metal_cross_resistance] This supports a shared stress-response layer but does not establish environmental selection or distinguish metal stress from general stress. A separate classification found 4,177 (54.9%) of 7,609 metal-important records metal-specific, 2,888 (38.0%) general sick, and 544 (7.2%) metal+stress under a 5% non-metal sick-rate threshold. [src: metal_specificity]

Specificity fractions were 60.6% for manganese (20/33), 60.5% for molybdenum (185/306), 56.5% for tungsten (173/306), 46.4% for selenium (64/138), 55.9% for cadmium (52/93), 51.9% for copper (1,346/2,594), 50.2% for cobalt (1,167/2,324), 49.3% for chromium (132/268), 48.6% for uranium (88/181), 47.2% for zinc (843/1,786), 43.7% for nickel (993/2,271), 42.4% for aluminum (752/1,772), 32.7% for mercury (35/107), and 21.9% for iron (144/659). Seven of 31 metal-tested organisms could not be processed because locusIds did not match the fitness-matrix index format; the 24 included organisms accounted for 7,609 of 12,838 records (59.3%), leaving 5,229 excluded. [src: metal_specificity]

Candidate prioritization identified UCP030820 (OG01015) as metal-specific in 2/3 organisms across 7 metals (67%) with mean sick rate 0.021; YebC (OG01383) in 7/12 organisms across 6 metals (58%) with mean sick rate 0.056; and DUF1043/YhcB (OG03264) in 3/6 organisms across 5 metals (50%) with mean sick rate 0.054. [src: metal_specificity] YebC’s proposed translation-bottleneck mechanism is explicitly a hypothesis. [src: metal_specificity] Among 149 novel metal-candidate families, 45.6% had dominant specificity of metal-specific versus 58.2% of annotated families (Fisher exact OR=0.60, p=0.003); ICA, or independent component analysis, identified 0 metal-specific modules, a result attributed to scale mismatch and sparse metal experiments rather than absence of such modules. [src: metal_specificity]

The resistance annotation gap also affects inference about novel environmental genes. The atlas reported 7.0% of AMR clusters with Bakta annotations only, and 55.4% of sparsely annotated one-source clusters were singletons versus 34.6% of two-source clusters. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The discoveries digest found resistance islands in 705/1,305 species (54%), with 1,517 islands, mean phi=0.827, 88% containing multiple mechanisms, and a maximum of 43 genes; phi is a pairwise co-occurrence coefficient, and co-occurrence does not establish co-selection or functional synergy. [src: discoveries, amr_strain_variation]

The Gene Function Ecological Agora **refines** interpretation through acquisition depth and gene context. Across 18,989 GTDB-r214 species representatives, it assigned 17,073,194 Sankoff-parsimony gain events and found recent-to-ancient acquisition ratios of 24.5× for CRISPR-Cas, 10.3× for TCS histidine kinases, 9.0× for β-lactamases, and 2.3× for clean tRNA-synthetase controls. [src: gene_function_ecological_agora] These signatures do not identify donors or establish environmental causation. Its MGE-machinery baseline was 1.37% of KO-bearing gene clusters, versus 0.57% for Mycobacteriaceae mycolic-acid KOs and 0.00% for both Cyanobacteriia PSII and Bacteroidota PUL KOs; PSII neighborhoods had 10.91% of focal features with an MGE neighbor within ±5 kb versus a Poisson expectation of 10.6%. PUL and mycolic-acid scans were deferred because of memory failures. [src: gene_function_ecological_agora]

The T4SS–CAZy analysis adds a candidate dissemination route. The GT2 gene tree contained 77 detected HGT events, including 32 normalized high-confidence cross-phylum events; the strongest event, Node_4915, spanned 8 phyla at a maximum divergence of 4.843. Among the 77 events, 65 spanned 2 phyla and 12 spanned ≥3 phyla; Node_4915 contained 35 genes, 82.9% syntenic. Divergence and synteny were negatively correlated (Spearman ρ = −0.615, p<0.001). [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] These results support cross-phylum GT2 HGT, but T4SS-mediated dissemination remains a hypothesis requiring validation. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

## Phylogenetic controls and environmental embeddings

Environment effects persisted within 5 of 6 tested major phyla—Pseudomonadota, Bacillota_A, Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, and Bacillota—while Chloroflexota was non-significant; Bacteroidota had the largest within-phylum effect (η² = 0.130). At family level, 20 of 141 testable families (14%) showed significant within-family effects after FDR correction, including Enterobacteriaceae (q = 3×10⁻²¹), Bacteroidaceae (q = 1.3×10⁻¹⁷), Lachnospiraceae (q = 2.5×10⁻¹²), and Pseudomonadaceae (q = 3.9×10⁻¹²). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] These controls **refine** rather than eliminate confounding: only 14% of families showed significant effects, and environment and phylogeny remain deeply entangled. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

A Mantel test, which assesses correlation between two distance matrices, compared [[entities/average-nucleotide-identity]] distance with AMR Jaccard distance across 1,261 species. [src: amr_strain_variation] 701/1,261 species (55.6%) showed significant phylogenetic signal at FDR < 0.05, median Mantel r was 0.247, and 87.8% were positive. Non-core putatively acquired AMR had stronger median Mantel r than core genes (0.222 versus 0.117; paired t-test t = -8.35, p = 7.0e-16, n = 489). [src: amr_strain_variation]

Environmental embeddings **support** the discrete analysis but do not make ecological variables mechanistically interpretable. Among 2,659 species with AMR data and AlphaEarth embeddings, 52 of 64 dimensions significantly correlated with AMR diversity at FDR < 0.05, with A34 top (rho = +0.24). Environmental cosine distance predicted AMR-profile Bray–Curtis distance (Mantel r = 0.098, p = 0.001), strongest for clinical species (r = 0.177), moderate for soil (r = 0.129), and weakest for aquatic species (r = 0.061). Embedding coverage was 28% of genomes and the dimensions were opaque. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The metal study **refines** the phylogenetic picture: cross-resistance was broadly positive across Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria. Co-Ni had median r approximately 0.58 across 28 organisms, Al-Co and Al-Ni approximately 0.30; Mantel tests across 351 organism pairs produced mean r = 0.23 with 62% positive values, and leave-one-out consensus prediction had mean r = 0.41. [src: metal_cross_resistance] However, metal-label permutation was non-significant (p = 0.42), only 2/28 organisms reached individual leave-one-out significance, and no negative controls were tested. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

Among 606 genera, MicrobeAtlas found Pagel’s λ = 0.260 for total AMR cluster count, λ = 0.441 for core AMR fraction, and λ = 0.335 for metal type diversity, compared with λ = 0.787 for niche breadth; nitrification showed stronger conservation (λ = 0.939 in bacteria and λ = 1.000 in archaea). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] These results are consistent with vertical inheritance plus horizontal transfer, but do not identify donors or prove that metal-resistance diversity causes ecological generalism. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

## Data integration and validation context

The BERDL Data Atlas **supports** the scale and integrability of this evidence: it inventories 1,011,650,903 KBase pangenome gene rows spanning 293,059 genomes and 27,690 GTDB species clades or pangenomes, alongside 27,410,721 FitnessBrowser measurements, 83,287 AlphaEarth environment embeddings indexed to KBase genomes, and 97,334 BacDive strain phenotype profiles. [src: berdl_data_atlas] It identifies 536 schema-level cross-tenant bridges across 29 canonical join keys, but only its FitnessBrowser–AlphaFold bridge was sample-validated; nominal joins do not establish valid value-space overlap. [src: berdl_data_atlas]

BacDive provides an independent validation layer. The BacDive–GTDB bridge matched 42,227 of 97,334 strains (43.4%) to 6,426 GTDB species with metal-tolerance scores; contamination-associated isolates generally had higher scores. [src: bacdive_metal_validation] A separate phenotype analysis matched 37,368 strains (38.4%) to 5,647 GTDB species and showed that classical phenotypes often track lineage rather than independently predicting genome-based metal tolerance. [src: bacdive_phenotype_metal_tolerance] The full predictive model reached R² = 0.633 after adding n_metal_clusters, whereas taxonomy alone explained 35.4%, phenotype features alone 16.3%, and taxonomy plus phenotype 34.5%; in 5-fold phylogenetic-blocked cross-validation, the full model had R² = 0.633 and RMSE = 0.028, taxonomy alone R² = 0.354 and RMSE = 0.038, and phenotype alone R² = 0.163 and RMSE = 0.043. [src: bacdive_phenotype_metal_tolerance]

The earlier BacDive analysis **supports** a contamination-associated metal signal: heavy-metal contamination isolates had median metal score 0.240, mean 0.236, median delta +0.053, Cohen’s d = +1.00, Mann–Whitney p = 0.006, and n = 10, versus environmental-baseline median 0.187 and mean 0.195. The gradient was heavy metal (+1.00) > waste/sludge (+0.57) > all contamination (+0.43) > industrial (+0.20); waste/sludge had n = 305, all-contamination n = 176, and industrial n = 796. [src: bacdive_metal_validation]

The PGP pangenome analysis **extends** environmental gene-distribution evidence beyond AMR. Among 27,702 species, 11,272 carried at least one of 13 PGP markers; acdS, pqqC, and hcnC were enriched in soil/rhizosphere species, whereas nifH was depleted. acdS prevalence was 15.8% versus 2.6% in other environments (OR = 7.02, q = 5.1e-62), pqqC was 43.8% versus 21.2% (OR = 2.90, q = 2.8e-53), hcnC was 11.3% versus 6.4% (OR = 1.85, q = 6.1e-08), and nifH was 12.9% versus 19.7% (OR = 0.60, q = 5.5e-08). [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology] All 13 PGP genes had higher core fractions than the 46.8% genome-wide baseline; pqqC was 81.5% core and acdS 70.4% core, while mean PGP accessory fraction was 29.7% versus 53.2% genome-wide. PGP gene richness correlated negatively with pangenome openness (Spearman ρ = −0.195, p = 2.0e-97, n = 11,272 species with ≥2 genomes). [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology] This contrasts with AMR, in which only 30.3% of AMR genes were core versus 46.8% for the pangenome baseline. [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology, amr_pangenome_atlas]

## Tensions

The strongest tension is between robust ecological association and the absence of a direct exposure-to-selection test. The analyses report environment effects explaining 2–13% of AMR-composition variance (η² = 0.02–0.13), but phylogeny, sampling, isolation, gene mobility, and annotation coverage may explain part of these effects. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Clinical exposure could increase AMR, AMR could contribute to clinical isolation, or both could reflect correlated host-associated traits. Majority-vote species classification also collapses within-species variation, despite sensitivity analyses at 60–90% thresholds. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

A second tension concerns metal isolation validation. BacDive reports higher metal-tolerance scores among contamination-associated isolates, including Cohen’s d = +1.00 for heavy-metal contamination (n = 10), while the cross-resistance analysis found no species-scale correlation between multi-metal tolerance and metal-associated isolation (Spearman rho approximately −0.02, p > 0.8), after which only 20 independent species remained. [src: bacdive_metal_validation, metal_cross_resistance] These results cannot be silently reconciled because scopes, scores, and denominators differ and the latter is underpowered. The global MAG result is related but non-equivalent: soil had 5.8% prevalence and OR=5.05, while marine samples had 1.2% and OR=0.20. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography] MicrobeAtlas further found metal-type diversity associated with groundwater prevalence (ρ = +0.112, p = 0.0019) but not groundwater-specific fold enrichment (ρ = +0.042, p = 0.242). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

A third tension concerns soil COG associations versus metal-specific interpretation. The soil study reports 2,355 significant COG–metal associations and distinct biome-stratified relationships, but chromium, copper, lead, and zinc co-vary in many industrial soils. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] It therefore remains unresolved whether individual associations are metal-specific or reflect multi-metal contamination.

Annotation also limits interpretation. AMRFinderPlus-focused annotation may underestimate novel environmental resistance, 15,550 clusters (18.7%) were unassigned to mechanism, 7.0% of AMR clusters had Bakta annotations only, and core/accessory labels depend on sampling. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_pangenome_atlas] These limitations mean that ecological structure may be real while its observed magnitude and mechanism profile remain annotation-dependent; they connect to [[concepts/bioinformatic-representation-coverage-bias]], [[concepts/structural-annotation-gap]], [[concepts/functional-dark-matter]], and [[concepts/pangenome-integration]]. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

Several denominator tensions remain unresolved. The environmental-resistome analysis reports efflux as 13% core while metal resistance is 44% accessory; the fitness-cost analysis instead reports 13% of efflux genes accessory alongside 44% of metal-resistance genes. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_fitness_cost] The discoveries digest reports efflux at 21% of AMR in human-gut species and 1% in aquatic species, while the environmental-resistome analysis reports 7.0% and 1.1%. [src: discoveries, amr_environmental_resistome] These values require harmonization of entity sets, filters, annotations, and denominators.

The prophage evidence contains an internal tension: density was strongly associated with AMR breadth (rho=0.572; partial rho=0.464 after controlling for genome count), whereas local proximity was modest and heterogeneous, with median species-level OR=0.85 and only 33 of 74 species above 1. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] The results cannot be treated as equivalent evidence for phage-mediated transfer.

The T4SS–CAZy evidence likewise supports a hypothesis rather than demonstrated transfer: it reports 92 elevated co-occurrences, 77 detected GT2 HGT events, 32 normalized high-confidence cross-phylum events, and 10× higher MGE density in T4SS-positive genomes, but CAZy genes were not detected on plasmids by ICEfinder and only 12 IMEs occurred among the top 100 accumulators. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] Pending threshold validation, BLAST validation of Node_4915, a housekeeping-gene null baseline, and biome-enrichment factorization prevent a causal transfer claim.

Finally, DvH field-stress and field-core genes were enriched in the core genome, but field or lab fitness alone predicted poorly and ICA-module conservation was not associated with field-condition activity (rho=0.071, p=0.62). [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] This tensions with treating field relevance alone as a predictor of conservation; fitness importance and gene length were more informative, while heavy-metal-important genes were 71.2% core and lab-antibiotic-important genes 73.4%. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

## Open Directions

- Use per-genome AMR calls and environment metadata with stratified contingency analyses, phylogenetically matched comparisons, PCoA and PERMANOVA, to test whether clinical, soil, aquatic, and host-associated differences persist within species and beyond taxonomic structure. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Separate MAGs from isolate genomes and model genome-recovery, clinical oversampling, sampling intensity, and metadata missingness explicitly. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_pangenome_atlas]
- Harmonize core/accessory thresholds, mechanism annotations, genome sampling, and the 21% versus 7.0% human-gut efflux estimates. [src: discoveries, amr_environmental_resistome]
- Re-annotate the 15,550 mechanism-unassigned clusters with broader homology, profile-HMM, and structure-based searches; compare AMRFinderPlus calls with independent resistance-gene resources and manually reviewed environmental candidates; quantify false-negative rates by environment and mechanism; and test whether environment-dependent mechanism fractions and effect sizes change when the 18.7% unassigned fraction is included. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Recalculate core and accessory AMR after stratifying species by genome-count depth, then test whether the clinical 68% versus soil 43% accessory contrast persists under matched sampling. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Use metagenome-assembled genomes and isolate genomes with comparable annotation quality to test whether clinical enrichment remains after reducing differences in sampling and annotation coverage. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Perform environment-specific gene identification on annotated and previously unclassified clusters, asking whether novel environmental candidates explain part of the observed metal-resistance enrichment. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]
- Map the 1,517 resistance islands to plasmids, chromosomes, integrons, ICEs, and insertion sequences. [src: amr_strain_variation, discoveries]
- Match BacDive GCA accessions directly to pangenome genome_ids, expand MIC and growth-inhibition extraction, and apply PGLS or phylogenetic PCA. [src: bacdive_metal_validation, bacdive_phenotype_metal_tolerance]
- Resolve the metal-isolation tension with matched metadata, KEGG/PFAM gene signatures, phylogenetic correction, concentration-normalized fitness, non-metal stress controls, and independent phenotype validation. [src: metal_cross_resistance, metal_specificity, bacdive_metal_validation]
- Fit partial-correlation models such as COG ~ Cr | Cu + Zn + Pb, classify COGs into resistance, stress, membrane, energy, and unknown categories, and report conditional and unconditional db-RDA R² values; test Moran’s I and spatial residuals. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]
- Validate global biogeography by checking sample-accession prefixes, normalizing prevalence by log(n_MAGs), testing biome-specific coordinate missingness, and testing whether hotspots remain after sampling-effort correction. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]
- Reanalyze MicrobeAtlas with finer habitat categories, alternative phylogenetic count or mixed models, explicit primer-detection correction, rarefaction, and experimental validation of priority OTUs. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]
- Replace broad ENIGMA COG-fraction proxies with curated metal-stress gene sets while controlling for abundance, depth, location, date, and compositional structure; load registered SSO geochemistry samples into CORAL and repeat AMR profiling. [src: enigma_contamination_functional_potential, enigma_sso_asv_ecology]
- Apply field/lab fitness–conservation analyses to additional organisms while controlling for gene length and essential-gene exclusion. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Execute value-space-validated NMDC–PROTECT and ENIGMA–PhageFoundry bridges and harmonize pangenome, AlphaEarth, BacDive, and environmental identifiers. [src: berdl_data_atlas]
- Test whether PGP soil-enrichment signals and AMR mechanism profiles persist after matched phylum, genome-size, sampling-effort, and environment-label controls; analyze pqqC–acdS and hcnA–hcnC neighborhoods and phylogenies. [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology]
- Reanalyze genomes from [[entities/klebsiella-pneumoniae]], [[entities/acinetobacter-baumannii]], [[entities/pseudomonas-aeruginosa]], and [[entities/escherichia-coli]] with dedicated prophage predictors, scaffold-based distances, exhaustive sampling, and explicit plasmid/ICE controls. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]
- Apply geNomad or PHASTER, compare prophage-marker calls with Bakta/Pfam calls, and test whether the density association persists after controlling for pangenome openness, genome count, phylogeny, plasmids, and ICEs. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]
- Run the pending T4SS–CAZy synteny-threshold permutation test, validate Node_4915 by BLAST against NCBI nr, establish a housekeeping-gene null, calculate θ = OR(T4SS-CAZy) / [OR(T4SS) × OR(CAZy)], and perform experimental transfer assays with base-pair-resolution neighborhoods. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]
