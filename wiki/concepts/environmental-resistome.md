# Environmental structuring of the bacterial resistome

The bacterial resistome—the collection of antimicrobial-resistance (AMR) genes in a population—varies with ecological environment in diversity, core/accessory composition, resistance mechanism, and association with mobile genomic structure. The main evidence comes from a pangenome-scale analysis of the [[entities/kbase-ke-pangenome]] collection covering 14,723 bacterial species and 293K genomes. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] A uniform Bakta–[[entities/amrfinderplus]] analysis extended this to 27,690 pangenome species, identifying 83,008 AMRFinderPlus hits across 14,723 species; 53.2% of species carried at least one hit. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Within-species analysis added 1,305 species and 180,025 genomes, showing that AMR diversity is structured by environment, prevalence class, tightly co-inherited resistance islands, phylogeny, and lineage-associated ecotypes. [src: amr_strain_variation] Prophage analysis **refines** this framework by adding genome structure: across 27,702 species, 14,669 (52.9%) carried both AMR and prophage markers, and prophage-marker density was positively associated with AMR repertoire breadth across 4,770 species (Spearman rho=0.572, p<10^-300). [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] This association is not evidence that prophages caused or transferred the AMR genes; it may also reflect species with high recombination potential acquiring genes from multiple mobile elements. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

The new [[entities/type-iv-secretion-system]]–CAZy analysis **extends** the genome-structure framework beyond prophage markers to conjugative machinery and carbohydrate-active enzyme neighborhoods. Among 30,497 high-quality environmental MAGs, 6,652 (21.8%) carried T4SS or conjugative machinery under a multi-marker definition including VirB4/6/8/9/10/11, VirD4, TraI/D, TrwB, and TraG. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] Ninety-two CAZy families showed elevated co-occurrence with T4SS loci within ≤10 kb, with [[entities/gt2-glycosyltransferases]] the top hit; GT2 occurred in 767 genomes with an average length of 5,041 bp. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] These associations were enriched in marine sediment (OR=5.5, q<10⁻⁹⁸), barley rhizosphere (OR=10.4), and maize rhizosphere (OR=4.1). [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] Thus, the new result **supports** the view that environmental genomic structure organizes mobility-related functional content, while the CAZy associations remain observational and threshold validation is pending. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

BacDive analyses provide an independent ecological validation layer. The earlier BacDive–GTDB bridge matched 42,227 of 97,334 strains (43.4%) to 6,426 GTDB species with metal-tolerance scores; contamination-associated isolates generally had higher scores. [src: bacdive_metal_validation] A separate phenotype analysis matched 37,368 strains (38.4%) to 5,647 GTDB species and showed that classical phenotypes often track lineage rather than independently predicting genome-based metal tolerance. [src: bacdive_phenotype_metal_tolerance]

The global biogeography analysis **supports and geographically refines** this framework. Among 30,497 host-associated-filtered environmental MAGs extracted from MGnify, 22,356 had usable coordinates, and 2.8% carried at least one metal-resistance type. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography] In a 5° grid, 11 hotspots and 3 coldspots were significant after Fisher’s exact testing and Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate (BH FDR) correction; the top hotspot was the Atacama/Andean region at lat=-25°, lon=-70° (21.8% prevalence, OR=9.83, q=7.6e-12, n=101 MAGs). [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography] Soil was enriched (5.8% prevalence, OR=5.05, q=1.8e-82), marine samples were depleted (1.2%, OR=0.20, q=9.2e-80), and rhizosphere prevalence was 1.9% (OR=0.66, q=0.30, NS). [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography] Hotspot interpretation remains preliminary pending sampling-effort correction and checks for expedition-level artifacts. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]

The gene-resolution metal analysis **refines** this framework by separating broadly conserved cross-resistance from chemistry-dependent magnitude. Across 452 metal experiments involving 37 organisms and 14 metals, 98.1% of 317 organism–metal-pair gene-level correlations were positive (311/317), and 99.1% were statistically significant (p < 0.05); all 15 pairs tested in at least 5 organisms had greater than 90% sign consistency, with no systematically negative pair. [src: metal_cross_resistance] This supports a shared stress-response layer but does not establish environmental selection or distinguish metal stress from general stress. A separate classification of 7,609 metal-important gene records across 24 organisms found 4,177 (54.9%) to be metal-specific, 2,888 (38.0%) general sick, and 544 (7.2%) metal+stress under a 5% non-metal sick-rate threshold. [src: metal_specificity] Thus, the positive cross-resistance pattern **refines** rather than eliminates a distinction between specialized metal determinants and broadly pleiotropic stress genes. [src: metal_specificity]

The soil functional-genomics analysis **extends** this environment-linked framework from detected resistance genes to broader functional gene content. Across 51,748 soil samples and nine metals—copper, cobalt, chromium, nickel, zinc, lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury—it identified 2,355 significant COG–metal associations at FDR < 0.05; COG means a Cluster of Orthologous Groups functional category and FDR means false discovery rate. Chromium and lead produced the strongest signals, while transporters, including ABC and RND systems, and biosynthesis genes dominated the top hits. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] This supports the existing evidence that soil chemistry is associated with metal-related functional composition, but it does not by itself establish AMR selection or metal-specific resistance because the associations are observational and metals co-vary. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

The soil analysis also **refines** interpretation after statistical control. A distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA), which models multivariate community composition against predictors, yielded R² = 0.799 and p = 0.005 using 999 permutations; after conditioning on batch and project effects, metal concentrations explained 80% of the variance in community COG profiles. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] This conditional R² must not be treated as total variance explained: the unconditional R² for metals alone was not reported and may be substantially lower if project effects explain much of the community variation. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] Biome-stratified phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS), a comparative regression method that accounts for phylogenetic relationships, found distinct metal–COG relationships in soil, marine, and wastewater environments, supporting environment-specific functional responses rather than a universal resistance programme. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

The plant-growth-promoting (PGP) pangenome analysis **extends** this environmental gene-distribution framework beyond AMR. Among 27,702 species, 11,272 carried at least one of 13 PGP markers; acdS, pqqC, and hcnC were enriched in soil/rhizosphere species, whereas nifH was depleted. acdS prevalence was 15.8% versus 2.6% in other environments (OR = 7.02, q = 5.1e-62), pqqC was 43.8% versus 21.2% (OR = 2.90, q = 2.8e-53), and hcnC was 11.3% versus 6.4% (OR = 1.85, q = 6.1e-08); nifH prevalence was 12.9% versus 19.7% (OR = 0.60, q = 5.5e-08). [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology] These results refine interpretation of environmental structuring: environment-associated gene distributions are not restricted to resistance mechanisms, although the PGP results do not measure AMR. [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology]

The PGP analysis also **refines** the core/accessory comparison. All 13 PGP genes had higher core fractions than the 46.8% genome-wide baseline; pqqC was 81.5% core and acdS 70.4% core, while the mean PGP accessory fraction was 29.7% versus 53.2% genome-wide. PGP gene richness correlated negatively with pangenome openness (Spearman ρ = −0.195, p = 2.0e-97, n = 11,272 species with ≥2 genomes). [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology] This contrasts with the AMR pattern in which only 30.3% of AMR genes were core versus 46.8% for the pangenome baseline and clinical AMR was especially accessory. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The comparison suggests that environment-associated functional genes can have different inheritance architectures; it does not imply that PGP genes are AMR genes or that all soil-associated functions are core. [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology, amr_pangenome_atlas]

The BERDL Data Atlas **supports** the scale and integrability of this evidence: it inventories 1,011,650,903 KBase pangenome gene rows spanning 293,059 genomes and 27,690 GTDB species clades or pangenomes, alongside 27,410,721 FitnessBrowser measurements, 83,287 AlphaEarth environment embeddings indexed to KBase genomes, and 97,334 BacDive strain phenotype profiles. [src: berdl_data_atlas] It identifies 536 schema-level cross-tenant bridges across 29 canonical join keys, but only its FitnessBrowser–AlphaFold bridge was sample-validated, so nominal joins do not establish valid value-space overlap. [src: berdl_data_atlas]

The 2026 discoveries digest **supports** this broad structure: across 14,723 species, efflux pumps constituted 21% of AMR in human-gut species but 1% in aquatic species (η²=0.127), metal resistance constituted 45% of AMR in soil/aquatic species but 6% in human-gut species (η²=0.107), clinical species had 68% accessory AMR versus 43% in soil, and within 823 multi-environment species, clinical strain fraction predicted total AMR at rho=0.465, p=2.2e-45. [src: discoveries] These observational analyses do not demonstrate that environmental exposure causes resistance. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The Gene Function Ecological Agora **refines** interpretation by linking environment to phylogenetic acquisition depth and gene context. Across 18,989 GTDB-r214 species representatives, it assigned 17,073,194 Sankoff-parsimony gain events and found recent-to-ancient acquisition ratios of 24.5× for CRISPR-Cas, 10.3× for TCS histidine kinases, 9.0× for β-lactamases, and 2.3× for clean tRNA-synthetase controls. [src: gene_function_ecological_agora] These signatures do not identify donors or establish environmental causation. Its MGE-machinery baseline was 1.37% of KO-bearing gene clusters, versus 0.57% for Mycobacteriaceae mycolic-acid KOs and 0.00% for both Cyanobacteriia PSII and Bacteroidota PUL KOs; PSII neighborhoods had 10.91% of focal features with an MGE neighbor within ±5 kb, versus a Poisson expectation of 10.6%. [src: gene_function_ecological_agora] PUL and mycolic-acid scans were deferred because of memory failures. [src: gene_function_ecological_agora] The prophage analysis **extends** this gene-context perspective but finds only a modest and heterogeneous local association: among 36,041 AMR gene instances from 1,953 genomes sampled across 100 species, 20,073 (55.7%) were on contigs carrying strict prophage markers, whereas 3,731 (10.4%) were within 10 genes and 1,991 (5.5%) within 5 genes; the median nearest-marker distance among co-localized AMR genes was 34 genes. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

## Environment-associated AMR diversity

Clinical-source species had a median of 5 AMR gene clusters, compared with 2 for soil, aquatic, and host-associated species; human-gut species also had a median of 5. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The overall environment effect was significant (Kruskal–Wallis H = 781.9, p = 9.4×10⁻¹⁶⁷, η² = 0.056), with 13 of 15 pairwise comparisons significant after FDR correction; the largest effect was clinical versus aquatic (rank-biserial r = −0.49). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The association was robust to majority-vote thresholds of 50%, 60%, 75%, and 90%, with η² values from 0.044 to 0.056. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The atlas **supports** clinical enrichment at broader scale: Human/Clinical species averaged 10.6 AMR clusters per species, compared with 4.6 for Soil/Terrestrial, 3.9 for Aquatic, and 3.0 for Animal species (Kruskal–Wallis H = 440, p = 7.0e-93). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] This used 7,838 of 14,723 AMR-carrying species with non-Other/Unknown labels, so categories and denominators are not interchangeable. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Among 2,684 species with at least 3 genomes and AlphaEarth embeddings, environmental diversity predicted AMR count (Spearman rho = 0.466, p = 1.6e-144), while environmental diversity and AMR core fraction were negatively correlated (rho = −0.173, p = 1.8e-19). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas]

The within-species study **supports** the host-associated direction: rule-based BacDive and NCBI classifiers found more AMR genes per genome in host-associated species than terrestrial or aquatic environments, with Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.05; human-clinical isolates had the highest burden. [src: amr_strain_variation] The NCBI classifier assigned environments to 1,190/1,307 species (91%), whereas the BacDive approximation classified 459/1,307 (35%); 52.7% of genomes had no classifiable isolation_source. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The MicrobeAtlas result **refines** these environment-associated patterns by showing that the relevant predictor was metal-type breadth rather than total AMR burden. The raw OLS correlation between mean metal type diversity and Levins’ B_std was r ≈ 0.21, while the PGLS association remained positive after accounting for ancestry, pangenome coverage, genome size, and species count in sensitivity analyses. With genome size included, metal type diversity had β = +0.0218, SE = 0.0061, p = 3.6×10⁻⁴, and the full model was favored by ΔAIC = 10.8; in the three-covariate model using 527 genera, metal types had p = 3.5×10⁻⁴ and BH-FDR q = 0.003. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] This supports a distinction between ecological breadth and simple AMR gene burden, but the niche measure is a sequencing-effort proxy rather than confirmed ecological range. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

The new T4SS–CAZy result **supports and qualifies** the environmental-breadth interpretation. GT2-neighborhood MAGs (n=376) had a mean of 0.045 metal-resistance types, compared with 0.004 for non-GT2 MAGs (n=260,276; Mann–Whitney p=8.6e-27); genomes with GT2 in T4SS-proximal neighborhoods carried 11× more metal-resistance genes. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] This links a putative environmental HGT niche with resistance breadth, but it is not equivalent to the MicrobeAtlas genus-level association and does not establish that T4SS or GT2 transfer causes metal resistance. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

The new soil study **supports but qualifies** the soil-specific metal component of these results. Its 2,355 significant COG–metal associations and environment-stratified PGLS relationships show that soil metal concentrations are associated with broad functional gene shifts, including transport and biosynthesis categories, but chromium, copper, lead, and zinc co-vary in many industrial soils. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] Thus, the soil signal cannot yet distinguish metal-specific associations from a generic multi-metal contamination response, and it should not be equated with the metal-type breadth association measured in MicrobeAtlas. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

The metal-specificity analysis **refines** interpretation of metal-important genes. Specificity fractions were 60.6% for manganese (20/33), 60.5% for molybdenum (185/306), 56.5% for tungsten (173/306), 46.4% for selenium (64/138), 55.9% for cadmium (52/93), 51.9% for copper (1,346/2,594), 50.2% for cobalt (1,167/2,324), 49.3% for chromium (132/268), 48.6% for uranium (88/181), 47.2% for zinc (843/1,786), 43.7% for nickel (993/2,271), 42.4% for aluminum (752/1,772), 32.7% for mercury (35/107), and 21.9% for iron (144/659). [src: metal_specificity] Seven of 31 metal-tested organisms could not be processed because locusIds did not match the fitness-matrix index format; the 24 included organisms accounted for 7,609 of 12,838 records (59.3%), leaving 5,229 excluded. [src: metal_specificity]

The earlier BacDive analysis **supports** a contamination-associated metal signal: heavy-metal contamination isolates had a median metal score of 0.240, mean of 0.236, median delta of +0.053, Cohen’s d = +1.00, Mann–Whitney p = 0.006, and n = 10, versus an environmental-baseline median of 0.187 and mean of 0.195. [src: bacdive_metal_validation] The gradient was heavy metal (+1.00) > waste/sludge (+0.57) > all contamination (+0.43) > industrial (+0.20); waste/sludge had n = 305, all-contamination n = 176, and industrial n = 796. [src: bacdive_metal_validation] The heavy-metal estimate is imprecise because n = 10 versus approximately 5,000 environmental-baseline strains gave a minimum detectable effect at 80% power of approximately d = 0.93. [src: bacdive_metal_validation]

The MicrobeAtlas groundwater validation **supports** a broad prevalence association but **does not establish** groundwater-specific enrichment. Among 1,624 groundwater samples and 767 genera with groundwater detections and AMR data, metal type diversity correlated with groundwater prevalence (Spearman ρ = +0.112, p = 0.0019); top-Q4 versus bottom-Q1 genera differed in prevalence with Mann–Whitney p = 0.007, with median prevalence 0.81% versus 0.62% (1.31×). However, groundwater-specific fold enrichment was null (ρ = +0.042, p = 0.242; top-Q4 versus bottom-Q1 p = 0.181). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

## Core and accessory AMR

Core AMR denotes clusters present in at least 95% of a species’ sampled genomes; accessory AMR denotes the remainder, and this distinction depends on genome sampling. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Clinical species had 68% accessory AMR versus 43% in soil species, with a significant environment effect (Kruskal–Wallis H = 506.0, p = 4×10⁻¹⁰⁷, η² = 0.036). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Mean core/accessory fractions were 57.1%/42.9% in soil, 54.4%/45.6% aquatic, 49.7%/50.3% host-associated, 40.4%/59.6% other environmental, 32.4%/67.6% clinical, and 19.7%/80.3% human-gut. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The atlas **supports** this gradient: only 30.3% of AMR genes were core versus 46.8% for the pangenome baseline (OR = 0.49, chi-squared = 23,117, p≈0), while the auxiliary genome was 2.2x enriched for AMR (33.6% versus 15.3%). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] In 4,252 species with at least 5 AMR clusters, 63.7% had AMR less core than their species baseline (Wilcoxon p = 1.1e-130; mean difference −0.102). [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] Among 37,444 AMR gene-species records, 51.3% were rare, 41.3% variable, and 7.5% fixed; the median variability index was 0.526 and median pairwise Jaccard distance was 0.435. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The discoveries digest **supports** the accessory interpretation but separates metal-specific from general stress responses: across 7,609 metal-important gene records, 4,177 (55%) were genuinely metal-specific, 38% general sick, and 7% metal-plus-stress; metal-specific genes were 84.8% core versus 90.2% for general-sick genes (Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel p=0.011). [src: discoveries] The new analysis **refines** this result: among 22 organisms with pangenome links, metal-specific genes had pooled core fraction 84.8% (2,969/3,500), organism-mean core fraction 88.0%, and mean delta versus baseline +6.9%; 19/22 organisms had positive deltas and 12/22 significant enrichment. [src: metal_specificity] General sick genes had pooled core fraction 90.2% (2,183/2,420), organism-mean 90.2%, and 21/21 positive deltas, while metal+stress genes had pooled and organism-mean fractions of 94.3% (467/495) and 93.6%. [src: metal_specificity] All categories were significantly core-enriched above baseline, but metal-specific genes were less enriched than general sick genes (Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel p=0.011). [src: metal_specificity]

This supports a modest two-tier model in which broadly stressful functions are more core-enriched than specialized metal-resistance functions, while both remain strongly core-associated. [src: metal_specificity] Metal-specific genes matched metal-resistance keywords at 12.2% versus 7.8% for general sick genes (Fisher exact OR=1.64, p=2.4e-8), supporting the biological distinction; general-stress keyword matches were 13.7% for metal-specific genes, 6.5% for metal+stress genes, and 11.5% for general sick genes. [src: metal_specificity] Approximately 14% of protein-coding genes, estimated at approximately 82% core, were putatively essential and absent from fitness data, biasing all categories toward core enrichment and making reported deltas conservative. [src: metal_specificity]

The Fitness Browser–pangenome analysis **refines** this picture: among 801 AMR genes with fitness measurements, core/intrinsic genes (N = 638) and accessory/acquired genes (N = 163) had identical mean fitness values of −0.024, Cohen’s d = 0.002, and Mann–Whitney U p = 0.33. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Thus, conservation differences do not imply different baseline knockout-fitness costs. The DvH analysis similarly found 678 essential genes at 80.1% core but lacking transposon fitness data, versus a 76.3% core baseline among 2,725 analyzed non-essential genes. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The prophage analysis **refines** the interpretation of accessory AMR by showing only a modest proximity effect. AMR genes within 10 genes of a prophage marker were 67.6% accessory, compared with 65.5% for distal AMR genes; Fisher’s exact test gave an odds ratio of 1.10, one-sided p=0.005, with a bootstrap 95% confidence interval of [1.024, 1.185]. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] The threshold dependence and species heterogeneity qualify this result: odds ratios were 0.78 at 3 genes, 0.92 at 5 genes, 1.10 at 10 genes, 1.19 at 15 genes, and 1.28 at 50 genes; only 33 of 74 testable species had species-level odds ratios above 1, and the median species-level odds ratio was 0.85. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

The T4SS–CAZy findings **refine** the interpretation of mobile structure in a different direction. In 376 genomes, T4SS occurred in 503 neighborhood entries and GT2 in 495, confirming syntenic co-localization at the contig level; GH23 was the second most common CAZy family in GT2 neighborhoods, with 106 occurrences. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] CAZy genes were not detected on plasmids by ICEfinder, while 12 integrative mobilizable elements (IMEs) occurred among the top 100 accumulators. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] T4SS-positive genomes had 10× higher mobile genetic element (MGE) density than other genomes (p<0.001), consistent with chromosomal or integrative transfer rather than plasmid mobilization. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] This is compatible with the prophage result in showing that mobile-genome associations need not be simple plasmid effects, but it does not demonstrate that T4SS transferred GT2 or resistance genes. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

## Environment-associated resistance mechanisms

All four tested mechanism classes differed significantly among environments after BH FDR correction. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Metal resistance accounted for 45.0% of AMR in aquatic species and 44.0% in soil, but 6.1% in human-gut species (η² = 0.107); target modification accounted for 43.6% in human-gut and 27.5% in clinical species, but 6.2% in aquatic species (η² = 0.100). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Across the order clinical, human gut, host-associated, soil, aquatic, and other environmental, efflux fractions were 6.1%, 7.0%, 3.7%, 2.0%, 1.1%, and 2.7%; enzymatic inactivation 28.2%, 24.1%, 44.1%, 34.3%, 41.2%, and 33.6%; metal resistance 19.7%, 6.1%, 12.7%, 44.0%, 45.0%, and 24.9%; and target modification 27.5%, 43.6%, 22.8%, 10.1%, 6.2%, and 25.1%. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

The soil and aquatic enrichment of metal resistance is consistent with natural heavy-metal exposure, while clinical and gut enrichment of target modification and efflux is consistent with antibiotic selection pressure; these remain ecological interpretations, not causal tests. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The global MAG analysis **supports and geographically refines** the soil component: among 22,356 coordinate-bearing environmental MAGs, metal resistance occurred in 2.8% overall, soil prevalence was 5.8% (OR=5.05, q=1.8e-82), and marine prevalence was 1.2% (OR=0.20, q=9.2e-80). [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]

The soil functional-genomics result **supports** this soil-associated mechanism pattern at a broader functional level: among 2,355 significant COG–metal associations, transporters such as ABC and RND systems and biosynthesis genes dominated the top hits. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] However, its strongest signals came from chromium and lead, and the analysis did not classify all COGs as resistance, stress, membrane, energy, or unknown until planned mechanistic annotation; therefore, these COG shifts cannot be counted as direct metal-resistance genes. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

The phenotype analysis **refines** mechanism prediction: the full model reached R² = 0.633 after adding n_metal_clusters, whereas taxonomy alone explained 35.4%, phenotype features alone 16.3%, and taxonomy plus phenotype 34.5%; in 5-fold phylogenetic-blocked cross-validation, the full model had R² = 0.633 and RMSE = 0.028, taxonomy alone R² = 0.354 and RMSE = 0.038, and phenotype alone R² = 0.163 and RMSE = 0.043. [src: bacdive_phenotype_metal_tolerance]

The metal-specificity analysis **extends** the mechanism distinction to candidate prioritization. UCP030820 (OG01015) was metal-specific in 2/3 organisms across 7 metals (67%) with mean sick rate 0.021; YebC (OG01383) in 7/12 organisms across 6 metals (58%) with mean sick rate 0.056; and DUF1043/YhcB (OG03264) in 3/6 organisms across 5 metals (50%) with mean sick rate 0.054. [src: metal_specificity] These three are prioritized because they combine repeated specificity with lower pleiotropic effects. [src: metal_specificity] UPF0042/RapZ, MlaD, YfdZ, YrbC, DUF39, and YrbE showed lower specificity or higher sick rates and were deprioritized as more pleiotropic or general fitness factors. [src: metal_specificity] YebC’s proposed translation-bottleneck mechanism for metal-homeostasis proteins is explicitly a hypothesis, not an established mechanism. [src: metal_specificity]

Among 149 novel metal-candidate families, 45.6% had dominant specificity of metal-specific versus 58.2% of annotated families (Fisher exact OR=0.60, p=0.003); the report attributes this difference partly to deeply profiled organisms having more opportunities to detect pleiotropy. [src: metal_specificity] ICA (independent component analysis), a method for decomposing activity profiles into modules, identified 0 metal-specific modules; this failure reflects scale mismatch and the small fraction of metal experiments, not evidence that such modules do not exist. [src: metal_specificity]

Of the AMR clusters, 15,550 (18.7%) could not be assigned to a mechanism from gene name or product annotation. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The AMRFinderPlus-focused annotation may underestimate novel environmental resistance; the atlas reports 7.0% of AMR clusters with Bakta annotations only and 55.4% of sparsely annotated one-source clusters as singletons versus 34.6% of two-source clusters. [src: amr_pangenome_atlas] The discoveries digest found resistance islands in 705/1,305 species (54%), with 1,517 islands, mean phi=0.827, 88% containing multiple mechanisms, and a maximum of 43 genes; phi is a pairwise co-occurrence coefficient, and co-occurrence does not establish co-selection or functional synergy. [src: discoveries] [src: amr_strain_variation]

The prophage result **qualifies** interpretation of these resistance islands and co-occurrence patterns. Prophage markers were identified by keyword and Pfam matching in Bakta annotations rather than dedicated prophage predictors, so the marker set may include false positives such as phage-defense systems and may miss divergent prophages. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] Gene distances were parsed from ordinal gene positions in gene_id formats rather than base-pair coordinates, and the 100-species proximity analysis sampled 20 genomes per species rather than all 293K genomes. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] Thus, prophage-associated neighborhoods should not be treated as demonstrated transduction events or as a replacement for plasmid, integron, ICE, and insertion-sequence analyses. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

The T4SS–CAZy analysis **adds** a complementary candidate route for environmental dissemination. The GT2 gene tree contained 77 detected HGT events, including 32 normalized high-confidence cross-phylum events; the strongest event, Node_4915, spanned 8 phyla at a maximum divergence of 4.843. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] Among the 77 events, 65 spanned 2 phyla and 12 spanned ≥3 phyla; Node_4915 contained 35 genes, 82.9% syntenic, and included WOR-3, Desulfobacterota, Patescibacteria, Bacteroidota, Firmicutes_A, Methanobacteriota, Bdellovibrionota, and Acidobacteriota. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] Divergence and synteny were negatively correlated (Spearman ρ = −0.615, p<0.001), and the most-involved phyla were Firmicutes_A (27 events), Pseudomonadota (22), Bacillota_A (19), and Actinomycetota (10). [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] These results provide positive evidence for cross-phylum GT2 HGT, but the report explicitly treats T4SS-mediated dissemination as a hypothesis requiring experimental validation. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

## Within-species clinical representation

Among 823 species with genomes in at least 2 environments and at least 5 genomes per environment, clinical genome fraction predicted total AMR cluster count (Spearman rho = 0.465, p = 2.2×10⁻⁴⁵). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Clinical-dominated species had a mean of 72.9 AMR clusters versus 16.4 in environmental-dominated species, a 4.4× difference (Mann–Whitney U p = 2×10⁻¹⁸), and higher grouped accessory fraction (93.6% versus 81.7%, p = 0.004); the continuous clinical-fraction/accessory-percentage correlation was borderline (rho = 0.065, p = 0.064). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] This is a species-level proxy, not a true per-genome comparison; the planned per-genome Fisher’s exact test was replaced because billion-row joins were costly. [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

Deeply sampled examples were highly accessory: [[entities/klebsiella-pneumoniae]] had 13,637 genomes and 1,115 AMR clusters, of which 7 were core and 1,108 accessory (99%); [[entities/staphylococcus-aureus]] had 13,274 genomes and 642 clusters, of which 9 were core and 633 accessory (99%); [[entities/salmonella-enterica]] had 10,097 genomes and 836 clusters, of which 11 were core and 825 accessory (99%); [[entities/streptococcus-pneumoniae]] had 7,944 genomes and 59 clusters, of which 1 was core and 58 accessory (98%); and [[entities/mycobacterium-tuberculosis]] had 6,673 genomes and 44 clusters, of which 4 were core and 40 accessory (91%). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Among 974 species with at least 15 genomes, 190 (19.5%) formed at least 2 distinct AMR ecotypes using UMAP, a nonlinear dimensional-reduction method, followed by DBSCAN; median silhouette score was 0.620. [src: amr_strain_variation]

The prophage analysis **supports** the importance of species-level structure but does not establish a clinical-specific mechanism: prophage-marker density was associated with AMR breadth across five reported major phyla, including Pseudomonadota (rho=0.54), Bacillota_A (rho=0.55), Bacillota (rho=0.40), Bacteroidota (rho=0.59), and Actinomycetota (rho=0.29). [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] The report interprets this consistency as evidence against a purely phylogenetic explanation, while noting that open pangenomes and recombination could independently increase both prophage and AMR content. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

## Phylogenetic controls and environmental embeddings

Environment effects persisted within 5 of 6 tested major phyla—Pseudomonadota, Bacillota_A, Actinomycetota, Bacteroidota, and Bacillota—while Chloroflexota was non-significant; Bacteroidota had the largest within-phylum effect (η² = 0.130). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] At family level, 20 of 141 testable families (14%) showed significant within-family effects after FDR correction, including Enterobacteriaceae (q = 3×10⁻²¹), Bacteroidaceae (q = 1.3×10⁻¹⁷), Lachnospiraceae (q = 2.5×10⁻¹²), and Pseudomonadaceae (q = 3.9×10⁻¹²). [src: amr_environmental_resistome]

A complementary within-species Mantel test compared [[entities/average-nucleotide-identity]] distance matrices with AMR Jaccard distance across 1,261 species. [src: amr_strain_variation] A Mantel test assesses correlation between two distance matrices; 701/1,261 species (55.6%) showed significant phylogenetic signal at FDR < 0.05, median Mantel r was 0.247, and 87.8% were positive. [src: amr_strain_variation] Non-core putatively acquired AMR had stronger median Mantel r than core genes (0.222 versus 0.117; paired t-test t = -8.35, p = 7.0e-16, n = 489). [src: amr_strain_variation]

The metal study **refines** this phylogenetic picture: cross-resistance was broadly positive across Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria. Co-Ni had median r approximately 0.58 across 28 organisms, Al-Co and Al-Ni approximately 0.30; Mantel tests across 351 organism pairs produced mean r = 0.23 with 62% positive values, and leave-one-out consensus prediction had mean r = 0.41. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The metal-label permutation test was non-significant (p = 0.42), only 2/28 organisms reached individual leave-one-out significance, and no negative controls were tested. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The metal-specificity analysis adds that specificity was measured in 24 organisms after 40.7% gene-record attrition, so excluded organisms may have different profiles. [src: metal_specificity]

The soil analysis **adds a direct phylogenetically controlled test**: biome-stratified PGLS found distinct metal–COG relationships in soil, marine, and wastewater environments, supporting environment-specific functional responses rather than a universal resistance programme. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] This remains preliminary because effect-size and spatial validation are pending, and planned partial-correlation analyses are needed to separate individual metal effects from co-contamination. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

The MicrobeAtlas analysis **supports** separating phylogenetic conservation from environmental association. Among 606 genera, total AMR cluster count had Pagel’s λ = 0.260, core AMR fraction λ = 0.441, and metal type diversity λ = 0.335, compared with λ = 0.787 for niche breadth. Nitrification showed much stronger conservation (λ = 0.939 in bacteria and λ = 1.000 in archaea). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] These results are consistent with a mixture of vertical inheritance and horizontal transfer, but do not identify donors or prove that metal-resistance diversity causes ecological generalism. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

Among 2,659 species with AMR data and AlphaEarth embeddings, 52 of 64 dimensions significantly correlated with AMR diversity at FDR < 0.05, with A34 top (rho = +0.24). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] Environmental cosine distance predicted AMR-profile Bray–Curtis distance (Mantel r = 0.098, p = 0.001), strongest for clinical species (r = 0.177), moderate for soil (r = 0.129), and weakest for aquatic species (r = 0.061). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The global analysis recovered coordinates for 22,356 of 30,497 filtered MAGs (73.3%); ENA retrieval returned 24,511 sample records, of which 16,964 (69.2%) had valid latitude/longitude pairs, while 0 of 532 MAG grid cells lacked ENA coordinate coverage. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]

The prophage analysis **extends** these controls by reporting a partial Spearman correlation of rho=0.464 between prophage density and AMR breadth after controlling for genome count, with p=1.0×10^-253. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] Its log-log regression had slope 0.823 (SE=0.018), p<10^-300, and R²=0.30; the report states that a 10-fold increase in prophage density predicts a ~6.6-fold increase in AMR breadth. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] Because the analysis remains observational and prophage identification was indirect, the result strengthens the case for testing genome-structure covariates but does not remove phylogenetic, sampling, or shared-pangenome-openness confounding. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

The T4SS–CAZy analysis **further extends** the phylogeny–genome-structure comparison. In a 70/30 discovery/validation split, enrichment patterns were reproduced across both sets. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] The GT2 HGT analysis found 32 normalized high-confidence cross-phylum events, while divergence and synteny were negatively correlated (Spearman ρ = −0.615, p<0.001). [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] This strengthens the case that phylogenetic distance and local genome structure jointly organize environmental gene distributions, but the report lists pending synteny-threshold permutation testing, BLAST validation of Node_4915 against NCBI nr, a housekeeping-gene null baseline, and biome-enrichment factorization using θ = OR(T4SS-CAZy) / [OR(T4SS) × OR(CAZy)]. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

## Limitations and tensions

Environment and phylogeny remain deeply entangled, NCBI sampling overrepresents clinical isolates, soil and aquatic species are undersampled, and 46.8% of AMR-carrying species fell into an Other/Unknown environment bin. [src: amr_environmental_resistome, amr_pangenome_atlas] Effect sizes were modest despite small p-values, with environment explaining 2–13% of AMR-composition variance (η² = 0.02–0.13). [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The global analysis adds that 30.8% of ENA records lacked valid coordinates and that hotspot patterns may reflect single-study or expedition artifacts; normalization by log(n_MAGs), distinct sample-accession checks, and production map generation remain pending. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]

The soil functional-genomics analysis adds a parallel statistical limitation: 2,355 discoveries among 3,915 implied tests (nine metals × 435 COGs) represent a 60% discovery rate, but co-contamination makes tests non-independent and BH FDR may be anti-conservative under positive correlation. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] Effect sizes have not been systematically reported, and associations with rho < 0.05 may be statistically significant without substantial biological effect. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] The conditional db-RDA R² = 0.799 likewise requires an unconditional metal-only R² before being interpreted as a total environmental effect. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

The prophage evidence adds a related limitation: the strong species-level density–breadth association coexists with a modest, threshold-dependent local proximity effect. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] The former may reflect direct phage mobilization, shared acquisition propensity, or open pangenomes, while the latter is sensitive to ordinal gene distances, marker false positives, and incomplete genome sampling. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

The PGP analysis introduces a related but non-identical comparison: its soil/rhizosphere enrichment of acdS, pqqC, and hcnC and depletion of nifH supports environment-linked gene-content differentiation, but its predominantly core PGP architecture contrasts with the strongly accessory AMR architecture. [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology, amr_pangenome_atlas] This contrast may reflect functional class, detection rules, or ecological history rather than a universal difference between soil and clinical genomes. PGP environment labels were conservative and noisy: only 1,637 species (5.9% of species with an environment label) were classified as soil/rhizosphere dominant, although 291,279 genomes had isolation-source metadata and 93.5% were classifiable. [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology] PGP detection used exact Bakta gene-name matches, and clusters were not functionally validated for truncations, frameshifts, or pseudogenization. [src: pgp_pangenome_ecology]

The MicrobeAtlas niche breadth is a detection and sequencing-effort proxy rather than confirmed ecological range. Levins’ B_std corrects for unequal environment sizes but not detection-probability differences, and genus aggregation can make a broad score reflect different species occupying different habitats rather than one organism being a generalist. The strict 5% within-environment prevalence analysis reduced the PGLS sample size from 606 to 379 and retained the direction but not significance (β = +0.0166, SE = 0.0099, p = 0.092). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Pangenome coverage was also uneven: sequenced genomes per genus correlated with inferred metal type diversity at r = 0.35, p = 2.6×10⁻¹⁹; coverage adjustment and 200 rarefaction iterations reduced but did not eliminate the association. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

### Tension: metal isolation validation

The earlier BacDive analysis reports higher metal-tolerance scores among contamination-associated isolates, including Cohen’s d = +1.00 for heavy-metal contamination (n = 10), and the phenotype analysis found contamination deltas of +0.040 and +0.035 within Pseudomonadota and Actinomycetota. [src: bacdive_metal_validation] The cross-resistance analysis found no species-scale correlation between multi-metal tolerance and metal-associated isolation (Spearman rho approximately −0.02, p > 0.8), after which only 20 independent species remained. [src: metal_cross_resistance] These results cannot be silently reconciled because scopes, scores, and denominators differ and the latter is underpowered. The global MAG result is related but non-equivalent: soil had 5.8% prevalence and OR=5.05, while marine samples had 1.2% and OR=0.20. [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]

The MicrobeAtlas groundwater result provides a further qualified comparison: prevalence was associated with metal-type diversity (ρ = +0.112, p = 0.0019), but groundwater-specific fold enrichment was not (ρ = +0.042, p = 0.242). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] This refines rather than resolves the tension, because the MicrobeAtlas analysis uses genus-level prevalence and inferred metal-type diversity rather than isolate phenotype scores or metal-contamination labels. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

### Tension: soil COG associations versus metal-specific interpretation

The soil study reports 2,355 significant COG–metal associations and distinct biome-stratified relationships, which supports environment-linked functional differentiation. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] However, chromium, copper, lead, and zinc co-vary in many industrial soils, making it unresolved whether individual associations are metal-specific or reflect multi-metal contamination. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] This remains unresolved until partial-correlation models such as COG ~ Cr | Cu + Zn + Pb, effect-size audits, and spatial residual tests are completed. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

### Tension: efflux conservation labels

The environmental-resistome analysis reports that efflux was 13% core while metal resistance was 44% accessory. [src: amr_environmental_resistome] The fitness-cost analysis instead reports that 13% of efflux genes were accessory, alongside 44% of metal-resistance genes. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This tension may reflect different entity sets, annotation procedures, or denominators and requires harmonization.

### Tension: discoveries versus mechanism-conservation denominators

The discoveries digest reports efflux pumps at 21% of AMR in human-gut species and 1% in aquatic species, while the environmental-resistome analysis reports 7.0% and 1.1%, respectively. [src: discoveries, amr_environmental_resistome] These values cannot be silently reconciled because AMR universes, filters, or classifications may differ.

### Tension: contamination and broad functional potential

BacDive and AMR analyses report contamination- or environment-associated metal-tolerance and mechanism differences. [src: bacdive_metal_validation, amr_environmental_resistome] ENIGMA found no robust confirmatory monotonic association between contamination and broad defense scores: rho = 0.0587 in relaxed_all_clades and rho = 0.0682 in strict_single_clade, with FDR q = 0.862 and 0.849. [src: enigma_contamination_functional_potential] This tension reflects different units, taxonomic resolutions, functional definitions, and sampling frames.

### Tension: field/lab fitness versus ecological condition

The DvH analysis found field-stress and field-core genes enriched in the core genome, but weak predictive performance for field or lab fitness alone and no significant relationship between ICA-module conservation and field-condition activity (rho=0.071, p=0.62). [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] This tensions with interpreting field relevance alone as a predictor of conservation; within DvH, fitness importance and gene length were more informative, while heavy-metal-important genes were 71.2% core and lab-antibiotic-important genes 73.4%. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] The metal-specificity analysis similarly found that metal-specific genes were core-enriched but less so than general sick genes, supporting a graded rather than binary relation between fitness breadth and conservation. [src: metal_specificity]

### Tension: prophage proximity versus prophage-density association

The prophage analysis reports a positive species-level association between prophage marker density and AMR repertoire breadth (Spearman rho=0.572; partial rho=0.464 after controlling for genome count), but the gene-level proximity effect is modest and heterogeneous: the median species-level odds ratio was 0.85, and only 33 of 74 testable species had odds ratios above 1. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization] These findings cannot be treated as equivalent evidence for phage-mediated transfer. The density result may reflect shared acquisition or pangenome openness, while proximity estimates are affected by ordinal distances, prophage-marker classification, and sampling scope. [src: prophage_amr_comobilization]

### Tension: T4SS–CAZy neighborhood association versus demonstrated transfer

The T4SS–CAZy analysis reports 92 elevated co-occurrences within ≤10 kb, 77 detected GT2 HGT events, 32 normalized high-confidence cross-phylum events, and 10× higher MGE density in T4SS-positive genomes. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] However, CAZy genes were not detected on plasmids by ICEfinder, only 12 IMEs occurred among the top 100 accumulators, and the analyses are observational; NB05 threshold validation, BLAST validation of Node_4915, a housekeeping-gene null baseline, and biome-enrichment factorization remain pending. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] These findings support a hypothesis of chromosomal or integrative T4SS-associated dissemination rather than proving T4SS-mediated transfer or a causal connection to metal resistance. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

- Map the 1,517 resistance islands to plasmids, chromosomes, integron boundaries, and insertion sequences. [src: amr_strain_variation]

- Use AlphaFold predictions to examine candidate metal-binding sites in UCP030820, YebC, and DUF1043/YhcB. [src: metal_specificity]
