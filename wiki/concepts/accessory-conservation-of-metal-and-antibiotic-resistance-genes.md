# Accessory conservation of metal and antibiotic resistance genes

Whether genes important under metal or antibiotic stress are less conserved than average, as mobile-element acquisition would predict, has mixed support. A single-organism analysis in Desulfovibrio vulgaris Hildenborough (DvH) found a non-significant trend toward accessory enrichment. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] A 22-organism atlas found the opposite, strong core enrichment. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] A follow-up found only a modest accessory tilt for metal-specific genes. [src: metal_specificity] See also [[concepts/core-genome-enrichment-of-metal-stress-fitness-genes]] and [[concepts/intrinsic-versus-acquired-resistance-gene-conservation]].

## Literature Context

The candidate papers mostly show that antibiotic and metal resistance determinants can travel on mobile genetic elements (MGEs), which would predict accessory, not core, placement. In Salmonella serovars from swine, more than 75% of genes were core in each isolate, and resistance determinants were mainly located on MGEs, on plasmids or integrated into the chromosome. Integrated phages often carried metal or antibiotic resistance genes [PMID 31675365](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31675365/). In 72 co-circulating Staphylococcus aureus genomes, 19 of 30 complete plasmids carried genes for resistance to beta-lactams, aminoglycosides, macrolides and heavy metals. Nearly identical resistance plasmids occurred in isolates from different host species, whatever the core genome relatedness [PMID 42426609](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42426609/). A family of genomic islands in beta- and gamma-Proteobacteria has conserved core genes for transfer, with accessory clusters nested among them that encode antibiotic, metal and antiseptic resistance [PMID 15547285](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15547285/). In Staphylococcus epidermidis, the MSCRAMM gene sesJ sits in MGEs (ACME and SCC), although such genes had previously been seen in the core genome. The abstract notes that antibiotic and heavy metal resistance genes also occur in MGEs [PMID 32071265](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32071265/). Ochrobactrum pan-genomics found MGEs, antibiotic resistance genes and metal resistance genes in all strains, in an open pan-genome [PMID 32428556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32428556/).

Some work links metal and antibiotic resistance and mobility in communities. In a 16-year biosolids soil experiment, resistance gene abundance rose. A follow-up microcosm suggested heavy metals were the primary selective pressure and may potentially have driven the simultaneous enrichment of metal and antibiotic resistance genes. That is a possibility, not an established causal result [PMID 40834540](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40834540/). Metagenomes from feces (753 public samples) showed MGEs with direct positive effects on antibiotic and metal resistance genes [PMID 39208976](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39208976/). A single wild giant panda showed multi-metal resistance genes and the transposase tnpA as the main MGE type [PMID 37478939](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37478939/). In Listeria monocytogenes, cadmium tolerance varied among closely related strains with cadAC variants. The authors saw no clear genomic explanation for the variability, and strains lacking cadA showed low tolerance, possibly from chromosomal soft metal ATPases or czc genes [PMID 39570037](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39570037/). Tn10 tetracycline efflux (TetA) is a tetracycline/metal-proton antiporter, a classic transposon-encoded determinant [PMID 7826010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7826010/).

These papers describe annotated resistance determinants, and none of them measures genome-wide fitness. The corpus's Desulfovibrio vulgaris Hildenborough (DvH) trend is non-significant. Lab-antibiotic genes were 73.4% core (OR 0.86, q=0.49) and heavy-metal genes 71.2% core (OR 0.77, q=0.14). That trend is consistent with the mobile-element picture, but the DvH report only hypothesized it and did not test it. The literature offers candidate mechanisms for that hypothesis. It does not confirm it for DvH.

The atlas result is different. Metal-defect genes were 87.4% core versus 76.9% baseline (OR=2.08, p=4.3e-162). This is in tension with the literature on mobile resistance determinants, though the atlas's own reconciliation fits it. Studies of annotated efflux and czc-type genes see accessory elements, whereas any-defect fitness genes are mostly core stress functions. The small accessory tilt for metal-specific genes (88% core versus 90.2% for general sick genes) extends this picture modestly. No candidate paper tests fitness-based gene sets directly, so that comparison is novel to the corpus.

The corpus's open direction on mobile elements and sharing with Oak Ridge species could use the plasmid-sharing designs above [PMID 42426609](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42426609/).

## DvH: resistance genes trend accessory

In DvH, the experiment classification included 55 Heavy-metals experiments (7.3%; cobalt, nickel, zinc, copper, manganese, selenium) [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] and 43 Lab-antibiotic experiments (5.7%; antibiotics and respiratory inhibitors). [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] The report treated these metals as "field" because DvH meets them at Oak Ridge FRC sites. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

Lab-antibiotic important genes (109) were 73.4% core, with OR (odds ratio) 0.86 versus baseline and FDR (false discovery rate) q=0.49. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Heavy-metals important genes (198) were 71.2% core, with OR 0.77 and FDR q=0.14. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Both trend below baseline but neither is significant. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] The report nonetheless called these the least conserved classes, below the 76.3% baseline. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] It suggested that resistance functions are disproportionately accessory, consistent with recently acquired traits on mobile genetic elements. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] This is a hypothesis, not a tested result. The report also hypothesized that metal-specific efflux pumps and metal-binding proteins are accessory, while uranium and mercury responses use core pathways such as DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) repair and sulfate reduction. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] It described sulfate reduction, lactate/formate/pyruvate use, and uranium, mercury and nitrate stress responses as deeply conserved. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] A separate report said its costly_dispensable_genes project found metal resistance genes enriched in the accessory genome. [src: lab_field_ecology] The DvH report proposes testing whether accessory antibiotic and heavy-metal resistance genes sit on mobile elements, were recently acquired, or are shared with co-occurring Oak Ridge species. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

## Atlas: the pattern reverses

Across 22 organisms and 14 metals, genes with significant metal-stress fitness defects were 87.4% core versus 76.9% baseline (OR=2.08, p=4.3e-162). [src: metal_fitness_atlas] This reverses the common assumption of accessory enrichment. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] It contradicts the atlas's initial hypothesis H1a, which was based on the DvH 71.2% figure. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] The atlas reads this as evidence that metal tolerance rests on core genome robustness rather than accessory acquisition. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]

The report offers a reconciliation. The DvH analysis isolated condition-specific genes, important only for metals. The atlas captures all genes with any metal fitness defect, which are dominated by core stress-response functions. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] It also notes that studies supporting the accessory view focused on annotated resistance genes, such as efflux pumps and czc operons, which are indeed accessory. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]

## Metal-specific genes: a modest accessory tilt

All gene specificity categories were significantly core-enriched above baseline. Metal-specific genes were the least enriched, consistent with a modest tendency for specialized mechanisms to be accessory. [src: metal_specificity] A Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test across organisms confirmed that metal-specific genes are less core-enriched than general sick genes (p=0.011). [src: metal_specificity] The report concludes that specialized metal resistance genes are slightly more likely to be accessory than general stress genes. [src: metal_specificity] They are still 88% core, and the 2% gap from general sick genes (90.2%) is read as a detectable accessory contribution. [src: metal_specificity]

## Tensions

- DvH reported metal and antibiotic genes as the least conserved classes (71.2%, 73.4%) [src: field_vs_lab_fitness], yet the differences were not significant. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] The atlas found 87.4% core for metal-defect genes. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]
- The atlas attributes the discrepancy to condition-specific versus any-defect gene selection. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] The metal-specificity result shows only a small gap. [src: metal_specificity]

## Open Directions

- Characterize accessory resistance genes in DvH for mobile elements, recent acquisition, and sharing with Oak Ridge species, closing the gap left by the unexamined mobile-element hypothesis.
- Test antibiotic-important genes across many organisms with the atlas design, to see whether the DvH antibiotic trend holds.
- Separate annotated efflux and czc-type genes from other metal-important genes within the atlas, to test whether the accessory signal sits in annotated resistance genes.
