# Decoupling between pangenome conservation and fitness cost

Pangenome conservation and measured fitness cost are distinct properties: an AMR gene's prevalence across sampled genomes does not, in this dataset, predict the relative fitness effect of disrupting it. The evidence comes from integrating [[entities/kescience-fitnessbrowser]] measurements with pangenome conservation labels, as described in the [[summaries/amr_fitness_cost__REPORT]]. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

## Core–accessory AMR genes show similar fitness distributions

Core, or intrinsic, AMR genes (N=638) and accessory, or acquired, AMR genes (N=163) had virtually identical mean fitness values of −0.024, with Cohen's d = 0.002 and Mann-Whitney U p = 0.33. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This **supports** a decoupling between pangenome conservation status and baseline AMR fitness cost: being broadly conserved did not correspond to a measurably different disruption phenotype from being accessory in the analyzed data. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The broader stratification analysis likewise found no conservation difference (p = 0.33), no Tier 1-versus-Tier 2 difference (p = 0.26), and no antibiotic-versus-metal resistance-type difference (p = 0.87). [src: amr_fitness_cost] These null results refine [[concepts/environmental-resistome]] by separating the persistence or distribution of resistance genes from the metabolic burden measured when those genes are disrupted. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

A separate pangenome integration provides a **refining** genome-wide comparison rather than a contradiction: among 33 organisms, 27,693 putative essential genes were 86.1% core versus 81.2% for non-essential genes, with a median odds ratio of 1.56; 18 of 33 organisms showed significant enrichment after Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR) correction. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] Essentiality was inferred from RB-TnSeq, a random-barcode transposon sequencing approach, under the library construction and growth conditions represented in the Fitness Browser. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] Thus, essentiality showed a modest conservation association in this broader comparison, while the AMR subset showed no difference in averaged baseline knockout fitness.

A genome-wide comparison **qualifies** this AMR-specific null result rather than generalizing it to all genes: across approximately 194,000 genes from 43 diverse bacteria, essential and strongly fitness-affecting genes were more often core than always-neutral genes, although the association was weak (Spearman rho=0.086, p=8.1e-230). [src: fitness_effects_conservation] Essential genes were 82% core, whereas always-neutral genes were 66% core. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] The new 33-organism result **supports** the direction of this association but reports a smaller essential-versus-non-essential contrast, emphasizing that conservation–fitness coupling depends on the definition of fitness category, organism set, and pangenome sampling. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] Thus, conservation can track fitness importance when fitness categories and breadth are considered across the genome, while AMR core/accessory labels did not distinguish baseline mean knockout fitness in the narrower analysis. [src: fitness_effects_conservation]

## Mechanism predicts distribution, not measured cost

Resistance mechanism was strongly associated with pangenome conservation status (χ² = 69.3, p = 1.4×10⁻¹³): 44% of metal-resistance genes were accessory, compared with 13% of efflux genes and 16% of enzymatic-inactivation genes. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Thus, mechanism **supports** prediction of where an AMR gene occurs in the pangenome, but it did not predict baseline fitness cost: efflux (N=254), enzymatic inactivation (N=304), metal resistance (N=144), and unknown (N=74) mechanisms showed no significant baseline-cost difference (Kruskal-Wallis H = 0.65, p = 0.89). [src: amr_fitness_cost]

This separation indicates that evolutionary or ecological processes shaping gene prevalence need not produce corresponding differences in laboratory knockout fitness. The report proposes, as an interpretation rather than a directly measured process, that horizontal transfer may preferentially retain cost-optimized genes or that compensation may reduce costs after acquisition. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The genome-wide analysis **refines** this interpretation by showing that core genes had heavier tails in both negative and positive fitness effects and that 24.4% of core genes were ever beneficial versus 19.9% of auxiliary genes. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] Conservation therefore may be associated with broader, condition-dependent functional involvement without implying a uniformly larger baseline resistance burden. [src: fitness_effects_conservation]

The conservation_vs_fitness integration further **supports** this distinction: essential-core genes were more enzyme-rich and better annotated than essential-auxiliary genes, whereas essential-auxiliary genes were 38.2% hypothetical and essential-unmapped genes were 44.7% hypothetical. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] These categories show that a gene can be classified as essential under the assayed condition without being broadly conserved or functionally characterized, so conservation status remains an imperfect proxy for measured fitness importance. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]

## Scope and limitations of the decoupling

The comparison used Fitness Browser data from 25 lab-adapted organisms, so the null result may not represent newly acquired resistance or wild populations. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The analysis also classified a gene as core using a ≥95% prevalence threshold, while the median number of GTDB genomes per Fitness Browser species was 9, with a range of 2–399. [src: amr_fitness_cost] Consequently, a gene present in all 9 sampled genomes could be labeled core despite having lower prevalence under broader sampling, making the core–accessory null result especially cautious for species with fewer than 20 genomes. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

The genome-wide comparison provides a related but non-identical test: it used fitness-effect magnitude, breadth, and condition-specific annotations rather than only the AMR baseline mean, and it found that genes with strong condition-specific effects were 77.3% core versus 70.3% without such annotations (OR=1.78 and p=1.8e-97). [src: fitness_effects_conservation] This **refines** the scope of the decoupling: broad conservation categories may fail to explain averaged AMR knockout cost while still tracking breadth or conditional functional importance. [src: fitness_effects_conservation]

The new analysis **supports** this caution but adds specific sampling and assay limitations: 44 of 48 Fitness Browser organisms mapped to pangenome clades, only 33 were used downstream, and clades with only 2 genomes can have trivially high core fractions. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] Its essentiality calls were made under library construction and growth conditions, and the report notes that short genes, low-complexity regions, or scaffold edges can lack insertions without being essential. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] The main Escherichia coli clade was also absent because it contained too many genomes, while Keio mapped to a small clade at only 26.1% coverage. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]

The finding therefore does not establish that conservation is unrelated to fitness under every ecological or genetic context; it shows that the available pangenome labels did not explain the measured baseline knockout-fitness distributions in this dataset. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This qualification connects the concept to [[concepts/pangenome-integration]] and [[concepts/condition-specific-fitness]], where denser sampling and condition-specific measurements could reveal relationships hidden by broad conservation categories or averaged fitness values. [src: amr_fitness_cost] The detailed cross-dataset integration is documented in [[summaries/conservation_vs_fitness__REPORT]].

## Tensions

The data contain an apparent tension: mechanism strongly predicted conservation status, yet mechanism did not predict baseline fitness cost. [src: amr_fitness_cost] This is not a statistical contradiction because conservation status and knockout fitness are different response variables, but it means that a mechanism-associated distribution pattern cannot be treated as evidence for a mechanism-associated fitness burden. [src: amr_fitness_cost]

A second scope-dependent tension is that the AMR analysis found virtually identical baseline fitness distributions for core and accessory AMR genes (mean fitness −0.024 for both; p = 0.33), whereas the genome-wide analysis found a positive, though weak, conservation gradient from essential or broadly fitness-affecting genes to always-neutral genes (82% versus 66% core). [src: amr_fitness_cost, fitness_effects_conservation] The AMR result supports decoupling for the analyzed resistance subset and baseline measure; the genome-wide result supports an association with fitness importance when effect breadth and conditional phenotypes are included. [src: amr_fitness_cost, fitness_effects_conservation]

The new 33-organism integration presents the same directional association with a smaller effect—86.1% core essential genes versus 81.2% core non-essential genes and median odds ratio 1.56—rather than resolving the tension. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] The difference may reflect AMR biology, averaging, organism composition, essentiality definitions, or pangenome sampling; resolving it requires matched, condition-specific comparisons.

## Open Directions

- Use the 144 metal-resistance genes with fitness measurements and compare their standard-condition costs with responses in the metal fitness atlas, using metal-specific fitness analyses to ask whether genes that appear costly under standard conditions are protective under metal stress. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Recalculate core/accessory labels from expanded GTDB sampling and test the association with fitness using prevalence thresholds and species-stratified models, asking whether the null result persists when the median sample of 9 genomes is replaced by broader within-species coverage. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Analyze mechanism effects within organisms with many AMR genes, including Cup4G11 (77) and BFirm (50), using genetic-background-controlled comparisons to ask whether conservation–cost relationships emerge within species. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Extend the analysis from 25 Fitness Browser organisms to all 293K BERDL genomes by predicting AMR cost from gene-cluster conservation patterns, then test whether predicted cost varies with pangenome prevalence outside the lab-adapted strains. [src: amr_fitness_cost]
- Reanalyze the AMR subset using fitness-effect breadth and condition-specific annotations, asking whether the genome-wide conservation gradient appears when baseline means are replaced by conditional importance. [src: fitness_effects_conservation]
- Recompute the essential-versus-core association with expanded within-species pangenomes and matched condition-specific fitness effects, asking whether the 1.56 median odds ratio persists after correcting for clade size, gene-length bias, and assay coverage. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]
