# Separating Shared Stress Responses from Stressor-Specific Fitness Requirements

Fitness profiles measured under different stressors can contain both a shared cellular-stress component and requirements specific to the tested stressor. This distinction is important for interpreting condition-specific fitness: overlap between stressor responses does not by itself show that the delivery vehicle or counter ion caused the signal, and a salt-associated fitness signal should not automatically be interpreted as a specific response to the intended metal. [^counter_ion_effects] This framework refines [condition-specific-fitness](condition-specific-fitness.md) by requiring metal fitness profiles to be decomposed into shared-stress and metal-specific components before mechanistic interpretation. [^counter_ion_effects]

## Evidence for a Shared-Stress Component

The [counter_ion_effects__REPORT](../summaries/counter_ion_effects__REPORT.md) provides a direct comparison of metal and NaCl fitness data. Across 71 NaCl experiments, 10,821 metal-important gene records, 86 organism × metal pairs, 14 metals, and 19 organisms, 4,304 records—39.8%—were also NaCl-important. [^counter_ion_effects] The analysis therefore supports a substantial shared-stress component, while retaining a larger metal-specific component of 6,517 records—60.2%. [^counter_ion_effects]

NaCl-important genes overlapped with metal-important genes for every metal, with overlap ranging from 9.2% for molybdenum to 57.6% for manganese. [^counter_ion_effects] The complete per-metal values were manganese 57.6% (1 organism), cadmium 50.5% (1), aluminum 46.1% (12), zinc 44.6% (12), cobalt 41.3% (18), copper 41.0% (16), nickel 39.3% (17), uranium 37.2% (2), iron 33.3% (1), chromium 29.2% (2), selenium 26.8% (1), mercury 23.4% (1), tungsten 10.8% (1), and molybdenum 9.2% (1). [^counter_ion_effects]

Removing the *Synechococcus elongatus* outlier reduced overlap from 39.8% to 36.7%, or from 4,304/10,821 to 3,739/10,183 records, so the overall signal was not driven by that organism. [^counter_ion_effects] The estimate is nevertheless especially sensitive to *S. elongatus*, which contributed 565/638 shared genes (88.6%) from 12 NaCl dose-response experiments. [^counter_ion_effects]

Shared-stress genes were important for a mean of 4.1 metals per gene, compared with 2.5 metals per metal-specific gene. [^counter_ion_effects] In *Desulfovibrio vulgaris* Hildenborough (DvH), 495 unique metal-important genes comprised 73 shared-stress genes, or 14.7%, and 422 metal-specific genes, or 85.3%. [^counter_ion_effects] This supports the interpretation that shared-stress genes tend to recur across more metal conditions, whereas metal-specific genes contribute more narrowly distributed fitness requirements. [^counter_ion_effects]

The [metal_cross_resistance__REPORT](../summaries/metal_cross_resistance__REPORT.md) **supports** this shared component from a different angle: across 317 organism–metal-pair observations involving 28 organisms and 85 unique metal pairs, 98.1% of gene-level fitness correlations were positive (311/317), and 99.1% were statistically significant (p < 0.05). [^metal_cross_resistance] However, the metal-label permutation test was non-significant (p = 0.42), and the study included no non-metal stress controls; thus, this **refines** rather than independently identifies a shared-stress mechanism. [^metal_cross_resistance]

The [metal_fitness_atlas__REPORT](../summaries/metal_fitness_atlas__REPORT.md) **supports** this decomposition at atlas scale: across 559 experiments, 31 organisms, and 16 metals, metal-important genes were predominantly core-genome genes, with 87.4% core versus 76.9% for baseline genes (OR=2.08, p=4.3e-162). [^metal_fitness_atlas] The atlas interprets this as a broad core cellular-stress component, while distinguishing it from accessory specialized resistance functions such as efflux, sequestration, and enzymatic detoxification. [^metal_fitness_atlas] This **refines** the overlap analysis by showing that shared stress does not imply that the entire metal fitness signal is nonspecific: the atlas's two-tier model places general stress functions in Tier 1 and specialized metal-resistance functions in Tier 2. [^metal_fitness_atlas]

The functional interpretation is consistent with shared cellular pressures involving cell-envelope integrity, DNA repair, and ion homeostasis, but these categories were proposed from fitness profiles rather than tested with formal functional-enrichment analyses or directly tested biochemically. [^counter_ion_effects] These mechanistic assignments should therefore be treated as hypotheses rather than established pathway findings. [^counter_ion_effects]

The [metal_specificity__REPORT](../summaries/metal_specificity__REPORT.md) **supports** this decomposition with an independent classification of 7,609 metal-important gene records: 4,177 (54.9%) were metal-specific, 2,888 (38.0%) were general sick across non-metal experiments, and 544 (7.2%) were metal+stress. [^metal_specificity] Its metal-specific category used a stricter non-metal sick-rate criterion, so it is not a replacement estimate for the 39.8% NaCl-overlap fraction. [^metal_specificity] The counter-ion analysis found 39.8% overlap, whereas the stricter analysis found 14.7% sick under osmotic stress; this **refines** the shared-stress estimate by showing that the fraction depends materially on thresholds and condition scope. [^metal_specificity]

## Evidence for Stressor-Specific Requirements

The overlap did not account for most metal-important records: 6,517 records, or 60.2%, were classified as metal-specific. [^counter_ion_effects] In DvH, 90.5% of metal-specific genes had SEED annotations, compared with 78.1% of shared-stress genes, although this comparison was descriptive rather than a formal enrichment test. [^counter_ion_effects]

The metal-specificity analysis **supports** a stressor-specific interpretation because metal-specific genes were enriched for metal-resistance keywords relative to general-sick genes (12.2% versus 7.8%; Fisher exact OR=1.64, p=2.4e-8). [^metal_specificity] The DvH whole-genome Pearson correlations with NaCl also formed a heterogeneous hierarchy rather than a uniform shared-stress response: zinc r=0.715, manganese r=0.545, copper r=0.532, cobalt r=0.498, mercury r=0.478, nickel r=0.446, aluminum r=0.420, molybdenum r=0.396, uranium r=0.350, selenium r=0.342, chromium r=0.318, tungsten r=0.298, and iron r=0.086. [^counter_ion_effects]

DvH supplied six NaCl experiments for comparison with profiles for 13 metals. [^counter_ion_effects] Higher correlations for zinc, manganese, copper, cobalt, mercury, and nickel are consistent with broad toxicity through essential-cofactor displacement or disruption of multiple cellular systems, while lower correlations for molybdenum, uranium, selenium, chromium, tungsten, and iron are consistent with more pathway-specific effects. [^counter_ion_effects] These mechanistic interpretations are extrapolations from fitness profiles, not direct biochemical findings, and remain hypotheses. [^counter_ion_effects] The DvH result supports examining shared-condition architecture in [cofitness-network-architecture](cofitness-network-architecture.md), but a whole-genome correlation cannot identify whether shared genes respond to chloride, sodium, osmolarity, the metal, or interactions among these stresses. [^counter_ion_effects]

The metal cross-resistance study **refines** the two-tier model by identifying three gene classes among 8,162 metal-important genes: 1,484 (18.2%) general stress genes, 2,306 (28.3%) metal-shared genes, and 4,372 (53.6%) metal-specific genes. [^metal_cross_resistance] Their mean pangenome core fractions were 92.0%, 91.0%, and 89.8%, respectively, while the percentages fully core at at least 95% were 57.2%, 50.4%, and 45.7%. [^metal_cross_resistance] This supports a gradient from broadly conserved stress functions toward more specialized requirements, while showing that even stressor-specific fitness requirements can remain highly conserved. [^metal_cross_resistance]

The atlas **supports** the existence of stressor-specific requirements but **refines** their interpretation: essential-metal tolerance genes for Fe, Mo, W, Se, and Mn had a mean core-fraction delta of +0.148, compared with +0.081 for toxic metals (Mann-Whitney U=39, p=0.015). [^metal_fitness_atlas] Thus, stressor-specific fitness requirements need not be accessory resistance genes; many can be conserved functions whose vulnerability differs among metals. [^metal_fitness_atlas] The atlas identified 149 conserved metal-biology candidates without full functional annotation, but these remain function predictions based solely on cross-species fitness data. [^metal_fitness_atlas]

DvH supplied six NaCl experiments for comparison with profiles for 13 metals. [^counter_ion_effects] Higher correlations for zinc, manganese, copper, cobalt, mercury, and nickel are consistent with broad toxicity through essential-cofactor displacement or disruption of multiple cellular systems, while lower correlations for molybdenum, uranium, selenium, chromium, tungsten, and iron are consistent with more pathway-specific effects. [^counter_ion_effects] These mechanistic interpretations are extrapolations from fitness profiles, not direct biochemical findings, and remain hypotheses. [^counter_ion_effects] The DvH result supports examining shared-condition architecture in [cofitness-network-architecture](cofitness-network-architecture.md), but a whole-genome correlation cannot identify whether shared genes respond to chloride, sodium, osmolarity, the metal, or interactions among these stresses. [^counter_ion_effects]

## Salt Chemistry and the Counter-Ion Test

The shared signal was not explained by chloride dose. Zinc sulfate delivered 0 mM chloride yet had 44.6% NaCl overlap, exceeding cobalt at 41.3% despite cobalt delivering up to 500 mM chloride, copper at 41.0%, and nickel at 39.3%. [^counter_ion_effects] Chloride-delivered metals had a mean overlap of 41.6%, compared with 37.8% for non-chloride metals. [^counter_ion_effects] In DvH, zinc sulfate also had the highest NaCl correlation, r=0.715, despite delivering zero chloride. [^counter_ion_effects]

These results support the conclusion that shared fitness signals primarily reflect common cellular stress biology rather than chloride contamination, but they do not show that counter ions are universally negligible. [^counter_ion_effects] [sodium-chloride](../entities/sodium-chloride.md) is not a pure chloride control because it supplies both Na⁺ and Cl⁻ and produces osmotic stress. [^counter_ion_effects] The evidence therefore argues against chloride as the primary explanation while leaving broader salt, sodium, osmotic, and condition-specific effects unresolved. [^counter_ion_effects]

The only within-organism comparison of copper salts was in psRCH2 and was severely confounded: CuCl₂ was tested anaerobically and CuSO₄ aerobically. [^counter_ion_effects] The cross-salt correlation was r=0.439, compared with within-replicate correlations of r=0.720 for CuCl₂ and r=0.859 for CuSO₄. [^counter_ion_effects] CuSO₄ had a higher NaCl correlation than CuCl₂, r=0.450 versus r=0.212, despite supplying no chloride. [^counter_ion_effects] This argues against chloride as the primary confound, but the aerobic/anaerobic difference could independently affect hundreds of genes, so the comparison cannot isolate counter-ion effects. [^counter_ion_effects]

## Consequences for Atlas-Level Inference

Removing shared-stress genes preserved Metal Fitness Atlas core-genome enrichment for 12 of 14 metals. [^counter_ion_effects] Corrected enrichment strengthened for molybdenum, changing from +0.132 to +0.145; tungsten, from +0.129 to +0.134; mercury, from +0.116 to +0.133; selenium, from +0.115 to +0.131; nickel, from +0.088 to +0.098; chromium, from +0.056 to +0.069; and uranium, from +0.031 to +0.040. [^counter_ion_effects]

Aluminum weakened from +0.099 to +0.068, zinc from +0.145 to +0.115, and copper from +0.090 to +0.084, while manganese remained +0.182 and cobalt remained +0.076. [^counter_ion_effects] Cadmium changed from -0.008 to -0.108, and iron changed from -0.040 to +0.182. [^counter_ion_effects] Cadmium had n=92 genes and iron had n=9 genes, each from one organism, while manganese had n=30 genes from one organism; its 100% core fraction is a ceiling result. [^counter_ion_effects]

The original conclusion that metal-important genes were 87.4% core, with OR=2.08, was not attributable to shared NaCl-stress genes. [^counter_ion_effects] This supports [gene-essentiality](gene-essentiality.md): removing a broad shared-stress component does not eliminate the atlas-level conservation signal. Approximately 14.3% of protein-coding genes were classified as putative essential genes because they lacked transposon insertions and were absent from both NaCl and metal-fitness data; these genes were 82% core, and their exclusion affected the original and corrected conservation analyses equally. [^counter_ion_effects]

The atlas also found that a simple gene-presence repertoire score failed to predict metal fitness, **supporting** the conclusion that metal importance cannot be inferred from repertoire alone and likely requires regulatory or expression-based models. [^metal_fitness_atlas]

## Scope, Tensions, and Caveats

The 39.8% overlap depends on the NaCl-importance threshold of fit < -1 or n_sick ≥ 1, so changing that threshold could change the estimated shared-stress fraction. [^counter_ion_effects] NaCl experiments identified 4,648 NaCl-important genes across 25 organisms, whereas the overlap analysis included 19 organisms and 86 organism × metal pairs. [^counter_ion_effects] Manganese, cadmium, selenium, mercury, iron, molybdenum, and tungsten were each tested in only 1 organism, limiting cross-organism replication for those overlap statistics. [^counter_ion_effects]

The cross-resistance study likewise cautions that metal concentrations differed among experiments, organisms ranged from 3 to 112 metal experiments, and the 28 organisms were not phylogenetically independent; formal PGLS (phylogenetic generalized least squares) or independent contrasts would strengthen the conservation claim. [^metal_cross_resistance] Its leave-one-out consensus prediction had mean r = 0.41, but only 2/28 organisms reached individual significance, so the conserved chemistry-specific magnitude layer remains less secure than the universal positive direction. [^metal_cross_resistance]

The stricter metal-specificity analysis also had substantial representation limits: 7,609 of 12,838 metal-important gene records were retained, while 5,229 were excluded because of locusId-format mismatches, and seven metal-tested organisms could not be processed. [^metal_specificity] This **refines** interpretation of cross-condition fractions by showing that both classification thresholds and analyzable organism sets can shape the apparent balance between shared and stressor-specific requirements. [^metal_specificity]

The overlap analysis cannot by itself distinguish chloride, sodium, osmotic stress, or other shared physiological effects because NaCl contains both Na⁺ and Cl⁻. [^counter_ion_effects] The shared-stress versus metal-specific classification also lacked formal functional-enrichment tests, and the SEED annotation comparison was descriptive. [^counter_ion_effects] Unmeasured essential genes cannot therefore be assigned confidently to either shared-stress or stressor-specific fitness requirements, connecting this decomposition problem to [essentiality-assay-discordance](essentiality-assay-discordance.md) and [gene-essentiality](gene-essentiality.md). [^counter_ion_effects]

## Tensions

The two analyses report different per-metal conservation estimates after shared-stress treatment: the counter-ion analysis reports manganese at +0.182, zinc at +0.115, and iron at +0.182, whereas the primary atlas reports manganese at +0.198, zinc at +0.151, and iron at +0.116. [^counter_ion_effects] [^metal_fitness_atlas] These values should not be reconciled by averaging because the analyses differ in record sets, organism coverage, and correction procedure; matched-data reanalysis is required to determine whether the discrepancy is methodological or biological. [^counter_ion_effects][^metal_fitness_atlas]

The metal cross-resistance study reports that all tested metal-pair correlations were positive and interprets this as a universal directional layer, whereas the counter-ion analysis shows that metal–NaCl correlations vary substantially across metals, including iron r=0.086 and zinc r=0.715. [^metal_cross_resistance] [^counter_ion_effects] This is not a direct contradiction because the analyses compare different condition pairs, but it **refines** the interpretation: positivity across metals does not establish a uniform response to NaCl or identify which shared stressor generates the signal. [^metal_cross_resistance][^counter_ion_effects]

## Open Directions

- Compare matched KCl, choline chloride, and NaCl controls using RB-TnSeq—random barcode transposon sequencing—to determine whether shared fitness signals track chloride, sodium, osmolarity, or the combined salt condition; choline chloride would provide a more specific chloride control than NaCl. [^counter_ion_effects]
- Repeat matched CuCl₂/CuSO₄, ZnCl₂/ZnSO₄, and CoCl₂/CoSO₄ assays under identical aerobic conditions, metal concentrations, media, and culture conditions to isolate counter-ion effects from oxygen-regime confounding. [^counter_ion_effects]
- Generate DvH NaCl dose-response data at 0.1, 1, 10, 100, and 500 mM to match effective chloride doses and test whether the metal–NaCl correlation hierarchy changes with dose or follows effective chloride exposure. [^counter_ion_effects]
- Perform formal COG, KEGG, and PFAM enrichment tests on shared-stress versus metal-specific genes to test whether cell-envelope, DNA-repair, and ion-homeostasis categories are genuinely overrepresented. [^counter_ion_effects]
- Apply independent component analysis (ICA), a method that decomposes correlated measurements into latent components, and module-level comparisons to separate shared salt-stress programs from metal-specific fitness modules. [^counter_ion_effects] The metal-specificity analysis identified 0 metal-specific modules using per-module z-normalization, but its raw module-condition scale differed from the precomputed z-scored profiles that identified 600 metal-responsive module records; rerunning the comparison on the latter profiles would test whether the negative result is scale-dependent. [^metal_specificity]
- Reanalyze the 10,821 metal-important records with stricter and more permissive NaCl-importance thresholds, and use condition-specific models with matched salt controls, to quantify how classification rules affect the 39.8% shared-stress fraction and determine which genes remain metal-associated after accounting for shared stress. [^counter_ion_effects]
- Resolve the metal-specificity locusId mismatches and repeat its classification across the excluded organisms to test whether attrition changes the shared-versus-specific balance. [^metal_specificity]
- Recompute the counter-ion-corrected and atlas conservation statistics on identical organism, metal, and gene-record sets to resolve the reported manganese, zinc, and iron discrepancies. [^counter_ion_effects][^metal_fitness_atlas]
- Test whether the 149 conserved, incompletely annotated metal-biology candidates are shared-stress or stressor-specific by combining matched salt-control fitness profiles with functional annotation and expression measurements. [^metal_fitness_atlas]
- Apply PGLS or independent contrasts to the cross-resistance matrices, normalize metal concentrations relative to MIC, and test non-metal stress controls to determine whether positive cross-resistance reflects universal stress biology or conserved metal-pair chemistry. [^metal_cross_resistance]
- Map cross-resistance gene signatures with KEGG/PFAM across 27K species and compare them with matched salt-control profiles to test whether the three gene tiers predict metal-specific rather than merely shared-stress fitness. [^metal_cross_resistance]
