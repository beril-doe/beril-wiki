# Environmental versus phylogenetic drivers of gene content

Within bacterial species, phylogeny explains more gene-content variation than environment does, but both effects are weak. The evidence is mainly the ecotype_analysis results as recorded in the discoveries digest and the ecotype_analysis report. [src: discoveries] The report concludes that for most species phylogenetic history is a stronger predictor of gene content than environmental similarity. [src: ecotype_analysis] A reanalysis found that clinical sampling bias does not change this conclusion. [src: discoveries] Separate AlphaEarth satellite-embedding results show that the share of human-associated genomes affects how well pooled embeddings reflect geographic signal. [src: discoveries]

## Literature Context

The candidate papers describe gene content within species as fluid and shaped substantially by horizontal gene transfer (HGT, movement of genes between genomes). A review of pangenome ecology defines the pangenome as the set of all genes in a species, split into core and accessory genomes. It says large gene-content differences can occur among genomes of one prokaryotic species, and that pangenomes arise through HGT and differential gene loss [PMID 31639358](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31639358/). It also calls the current view of pangenome variation phenomenological and incomplete. Analysis of over one hundred prevalent gut bacterial species found that gene content divergence scales predictably with core-genome SNP divergence, with substantial variability across species. Accessory genes show faster linkage decay than core SNPs, and mobile genetic elements show minimal linkage to core SNPs [PMID 42090264](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42090264/).

Several case studies show a mix of vertical and horizontal signal. In Rhizobium leguminosarum, two plasmid types track the chromosomal phylogeny, while type I and the symbiosis plasmid (pSym) vary in size and gene content and have phylogenies consistent with frequent horizontal transmission. The study used 62 genomes from one natural population [PMID 41212030](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41212030/). In 37 Polynucleobacter asymbioticus strains from three Alpine sites, seventy-five percent of the flexible genome is clustered in genomic islands. Two island variants, for aromatic-compound degradation and nitrate assimilation, were functional in ecophysiological tests, which suggests microniche partitioning [PMID 27836842](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27836842/). In Brucella, 214 genes were differentially distributed, and parallel gain and loss of genomic islands across lineages was linked to host interactions [PMID 22923103](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22923103/). Environmental plasmids also remodel readily through transposable elements [PMID 42485076](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42485076/). The Phytophthora and dinoflagellate papers concern eukaryotic HGT and are not direct evidence on bacterial environment-versus-phylogeny effects.

The corpus finding that phylogeny outranks environment is a weak effect: median partial correlations are 0.0143 for phylogeny and 0.0025 for environment across 172 species. Phylogeny dominates in 60.5% of species. This is broadly consistent with the gut-species result that gene content tracks core-genome divergence [PMID 42090264](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42090264/). It is also consistent with the view that much accessory content moves independently of the chromosome, which would weaken genome-wide associations with either phylogeny or environment [PMID 31639358](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31639358/). The hub's hypothesis that environmental adaptation acts on specific gene subsets rather than the whole genome is not tested in the corpus. The Polynucleobacter islands are an example of locus-level, ecologically relevant variation in one species [PMID 27836842](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27836842/). The corpus's per-species null (no significant environment effect in 156 species, 90.7%) therefore does not contradict such locus-specific adaptation. Testing function-restricted gene sets such as COG categories (clusters of orthologous groups) would extend this literature.

The corpus null results on openness (rho=-0.05, p=0.54 for environment; rho=0.03, p=0.73 for phylogeny) have no direct counterpart in these papers. The pangenome_openness report's reading, that HGT is opportunistic rather than ecologically directed, is the corpus's own interpretation and is not drawn from these papers. The clinical-sampling-bias reanalysis (p=0.83), the AlphaEarth embedding results and the data-quality checks are novel relative to these candidates, which do not address satellite embeddings or sampling bias.

## Phylogeny versus environment in gene content

The ecotype correlation analysis examined 172 species with sufficient environmental and phylogenetic data. [src: ecotype_analysis] The median partial correlation was 0.0025 for environment [src: discoveries, ecotype_analysis] and 0.0143 for phylogeny. [src: discoveries, ecotype_analysis] Phylogeny dominates in 60.5% of species and environment in 39.5%. [src: discoveries, ecotype_analysis] The related gc_ecotype_ecology project cites the same result: phylogeny dominant in 60.5% of 172 species. [src: gc_ecotype_ecology] The report reads this as suggesting that vertical inheritance dominates the gene-content signal. [src: ecotype_analysis] See [[entities/partial-correlation-of-environment-and-gene-content-distance]] and [[concepts/phylogenetic-structure-versus-ecological-adaptation-in-gene-content-patterns]].

Per-species tests of the environment effect were mostly null. A significant positive environment effect (p<0.05) appeared in 12 species (7.0%) [src: ecotype_analysis] and a significant negative one in 4 species (2.3%). [src: ecotype_analysis] No significant environment effect was found in 156 species (90.7%). [src: ecotype_analysis]

The report regards phylogenetic dominance as consistent with Garud et al. (2019) and Shapiro et al. (2012), described as showing that clonal ancestry shapes genome-wide variation more than ecological niche. [src: ecotype_analysis] It relates the weak environmental effects to Polz et al. (2013), described as arguing that horizontal gene transfer (HGT) shapes population structure at specific loci rather than genome-wide. [src: ecotype_analysis] From this it hypothesizes that environmental adaptation may act on specific gene subsets rather than the whole genome. [src: ecotype_analysis] This is a hypothesis, not a tested result. The ecotype_env_reanalysis report makes a similar suggestion, naming transport and secondary metabolism as candidate categories rather than whole-genome Jaccard distance. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The reports propose to test COG (clusters of orthologous groups) categories such as V-Defense and L-Mobile for stronger environment effects [src: ecotype_analysis], and to compare gene content between ecotype clusters for species with identified ecotypes. [src: ecotype_analysis] See [[concepts/functional-differentiation-of-gene-content-ecotypes-across-cog-categories]].

## Sampling bias as an explanation

The ecotype_analysis report found no significant difference between "environmental" bacteria, where latitude/longitude is meaningful, and host-associated bacteria (p=0.66). [src: discoveries, ecotype_analysis]

One hypothesis was that clinical sampling bias explains the weak environment effect. [src: discoveries] The reanalysis did not support it. It stratified 213 species by dominant environment type, with 47% human-associated and 21% environmental. Human-associated species had higher median partial correlations (0.084) than environmental species (0.051), with Mann-Whitney p=0.83. [src: discoveries] The reanalysis concludes that phylogeny dominates regardless of sample environment. It also concludes that the geographic signal in embeddings and the environment-gene content relationship are distinct phenomena. [src: discoveries]

The reanalysis was the first test of whether genome-level environment classification, rather than species-level manual assignment, changes the ecotype_analysis conclusion. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] It states that the original coarse manual comparison gave p=0.66 and that the harmonized reanalysis confirmed the null at p=0.83. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

Group statistics from the reanalysis:

| Group | Species | Median partial correlation | Mean | SD | Range |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Environmental | 37 | 0.051 | 0.073 | 0.299 | [-0.50, 0.78] [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] |
| Human-associated | 93 | 0.084 | 0.110 | 0.226 | [-0.30, 0.73] [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] |
| Mixed/Other | 53 | 0.109 | 0.148 | 0.261 | [-0.38, 0.69] [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] |

The one-sided Mann-Whitney test (environmental vs human-associated) gave U=1536, p=0.83. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The direction is opposite to the hypothesis. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Continuous analyses agree: the fraction of human-associated genomes did not correlate with partial correlation (Spearman rho=0.030, p=0.69) [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis], and the fraction of environmental genomes did not either (rho=-0.085, p=0.25). [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Both binary and continuous analyses found no relationship between environment type and correlation strength. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

The reanalysis had predicted a stronger effect in environmental species because env_embedding_explorer reported more geographic signal in their AlphaEarth embeddings (3.4x vs 2.0x for human-associated). [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The report offers possible explanations, which it does not test. Geographically differentiated embeddings may capture climate, vegetation and land use variation that may not strongly predict which genes a bacterium has. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] Embeddings may also capture regional epidemiological patterns, giving real but non-ecological associations. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The Mixed/Other group's highest median (0.109) may reflect diverse sampling campaigns. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis] The report proposes repeating the analysis with ENVO (Environment Ontology) terms from the env_broad_scale field, which may classify environments more accurately. [src: ecotype_env_reanalysis]

The reanalysis harmonized isolation_source into 12 categories from 5,774 values. [src: discoveries] On that basis 106/224 ecotype species are majority human-associated (gut + clinical). [src: discoveries] Only 47/224 (21%) are majority environmental (Soil, Marine, Freshwater, Extreme, Plant). [src: discoveries] The report calls this more systematic than the original manual species-level categorization. It says the classification confirms the clinical bias but shows that the bias does not confound the ecotype results. [src: discoveries] Related pages: [[concepts/clinical-sampling-bias-in-environment-embedding-analyses]], [[concepts/harmonizing-free-text-isolation-source-and-environment-metadata]].

The env_embedding_explorer report offers a contrary hypothesis. The earlier weak signal (median partial correlation 0.0025) [src: env_embedding_explorer] may be partly explained by clinical bias, because if 38% of compared genomes come from interchangeable hospital environments the relationship would be diluted. [src: env_embedding_explorer] It proposes repeating the ecotype analysis on environmental samples only, where a stronger signal could appear. [src: env_embedding_explorer] It cites the earlier finding that phylogeny dominates environment. [src: env_embedding_explorer] It also notes that its stronger distance-decay for environmental samples is consistent with environmental selection. [src: env_embedding_explorer] The reanalysis above is the test of this idea at the species level and found no support.

## Satellite embeddings as an environment proxy

The embedding data are skewed toward human-associated samples. Of 83,287 genomes with AlphaEarth embeddings, 38% are human-associated (clinical 20%, gut 16%, other 2%). [src: discoveries] Soil, marine and freshwater are 7% each. [src: discoveries] The report attributes this to NCBI's bias toward pathogen sequencing, since clinical isolates have good geographic metadata from epidemiological tracking. [src: discoveries] It concludes the embeddings are most informative for environmental microbiology samples and least informative for clinical isolates. [src: env_embedding_explorer]

The skew changes the apparent geographic signal. Environmental genome pairs show a 3.4x ratio in embedding cosine distance between nearby (<100 km, mean=0.27) and far (>10K km, mean=0.90) pairs. [src: discoveries] Human-associated pairs show only 2.0x (0.37 vs 0.75). [src: discoveries] The report attributes this to hospitals worldwide having similar urban satellite imagery. [src: discoveries] The pooled curve (2.0x), dominated by the 38% human-associated fraction, substantially underestimates the geographic signal. [src: discoveries] The report recommends stratifying by sample type, or at minimum excluding human-associated samples. [src: discoveries] It also proposes testing whether environmental samples with similar embeddings share more accessory genes after controlling for phylogeny. [src: env_embedding_explorer] See [[entities/alphaearth-environmental-embeddings]] and [[concepts/distance-decay-of-environmental-embeddings-with-geographic-distance]].

### Data-quality limits on embeddings

- 30,469 genomes (36.6%) cluster at coordinates with >50 genomes of >10 species. [src: discoveries]
- Some flagged locations are legitimate field sites: Rifle, CO (1,883 genomes, DOE IFRC) [src: discoveries], Saanich Inlet (1,529 genomes) [src: discoveries] and Siberian soda lakes (812 genomes). [src: discoveries] The report says the heuristic needs refinement. It proposes checking isolation_source homogeneity at each location. [src: discoveries]
- 3,838 of 83,287 genomes have NaN (not a number) in at least one of the 64 embedding dimensions. [src: discoveries] The cause is unclear. It may be missing imagery or coordinates outside satellite coverage. These genomes must be filtered before embedding-based analysis. [src: discoveries]

See [[concepts/coordinate-quality-and-institutional-address-artifacts-in-genome-geolocation]].

## Pangenome openness and the environment/phylogeny effects

Pangenome openness, represented by low core fraction, had no significant relationship with the environment effect (rho=-0.05, p=0.54) [src: discoveries, pangenome_openness] or the phylogeny effect (rho=0.03, p=0.73). [src: discoveries, pangenome_openness] The report concludes that open versus closed pangenome does not predict whether environment or phylogeny dominates gene content. [src: pangenome_openness] It reads the lack of correlation with environment effects as consistent with HGT being opportunistic rather than ecologically directed. [src: pangenome_openness] Limits: the effects come from partial correlations that may not fully disentangle confounded variables [src: pangenome_openness], and the upstream ecotype analysis may have limited power for species with few genomes. [src: pangenome_openness] See [[entities/pangenome-openness]].

Other analyses link openness to ecology and metabolism, and they use different data. In 1,872 species with pangenome, GapMind pathway and AlphaEarth data [src: discoveries]:

- Openness correlates weakly with pathway completeness (r=0.107, p=3.6e-6) [src: discoveries] and with pathway variability as standard deviation (r=0.066, p=0.004). [src: discoveries]
- Niche breadth from embedding diversity correlates with pathway completeness (r=0.392, p=7.1e-70). [src: discoveries] Embedding variance gives r=0.412, p=1.8e-77. [src: discoveries] Geographic distance alone gives r=0.360, p=1.8e-58, weaker than the embedding-based metrics. [src: discoveries]
- Openness correlates with niche breadth (r=0.324, p=5.6e-47). [src: discoveries] Core fraction correlates negatively with niche breadth (r=-0.445, p=1.4e-91). [src: discoveries]
- The report attributes embedding diversity's edge over geography to capture of ecological context such as soil, marine and clinical environments. [src: discoveries] It also suggests that accessory genes enable metabolic heterogeneity [src: discoveries] and asserts that broader niches require more complete toolkits [src: discoveries] and that pangenome flexibility enables adaptation. [src: discoveries] These are interpretations of correlations.
- Coverage is limited. Only 6.8% of species (1,872/27,690) have at least 5 genomes with embeddings, because most NCBI genomes, especially clinical isolates, lack latitude/longitude. [src: discoveries]

In a pan-bacterial analysis of 2,810 species with ≥10 genomes each, variable pathways correlated with openness (Spearman rho=0.327, p=7.2e-71). [src: discoveries] The partial Spearman controlling for genome count was rho=0.530, p=2.83e-203. [src: discoveries] The signal held across 18/21 phylogenetic groups tested. [src: discoveries] The report calls pathway variability a stronger predictor of openness than the environmental and phylogenetic predictors that gave a null result in pangenome_openness. [src: discoveries] Amino acid pathways (leu, val, arg, lys, thr) showed the largest accessory dependence gap (~0.14). [src: discoveries]

Clustering 225 species (≥50 genomes, ≥3 variable pathways) gave a median of 4 metabolic ecotypes and a maximum of 8. [src: discoveries] More ecotypes correlated with openness (partial rho=0.322, p=8.0e-07). [src: discoveries] Top ecotyped species were [[entities/alistipes-onderdonkii]] [src: discoveries] and [[entities/barnesiella-intestinihominis]] (8 each). [src: discoveries] See [[concepts/pangenome-openness-and-its-correlates]] and [[concepts/within-species-metabolic-ecotypes-from-pathway-variation]].

## Pangenomes without species clades

Twelve pangenomes have no matching species clades and are mostly obligate symbionts. [src: discoveries] They include Portiera aleyrodidarum, a whitefly symbiont [src: discoveries], Profftella armatura, a psyllid symbiont [src: discoveries], and uncultivated lineages with UBA, TMED and SCGC prefixes. [src: discoveries] They are valid pangenomes but were filtered from species metadata because of single-genome status. [src: discoveries]

## Tensions

- Environment barely explains gene content (median partial correlation 0.0025) [src: discoveries], yet niche breadth from embeddings correlates with pathway completeness (r=0.392). [src: discoveries] These use different response variables and species sets, so they do not directly conflict.
- The env_embedding_explorer entry proposes that clinical bias may partly explain the weak environment signal. [src: discoveries, env_embedding_explorer] The reanalysis found the opposite pattern and a non-significant difference (p=0.83). [src: discoveries, ecotype_env_reanalysis]
- Openness shows no significant relation to the environment or phylogeny effects [src: discoveries], but it correlates with pathway variability. [src: discoveries] The report calls this much stronger than the earlier null result. [src: discoveries]

## Open Directions

- Repeat the partial-correlation analysis using embeddings only from environmental genomes, with NaN and institutional-address genomes filtered. This would test whether the weak environment effect is partly an artifact of the proxy.
- Subset gene content by function (COG categories such as V-Defense and L-Mobile). This would test the gene-subset hypothesis. See [[concepts/defense-and-mobile-element-gene-content-as-drivers-of-ecotype-differences]].
- Compare gene content between ecotype clusters within species. This would test adaptation within species.
- Repeat the reanalysis with ENVO terms from env_broad_scale. This would test whether classification error hides an environment effect.
- Test whether pathway variability explains within-species gene-content variation after phylogeny is removed. This would link the openness results to the environment/phylogeny decomposition.
- Use isolation_source homogeneity to separate field sites from institutional addresses in the flagged locations. This would close the heuristic gap noted in the report.
