# Metal cross-resistance across bacterial fitness landscapes

Metal cross-resistance is a broadly positive, gene-level relationship in which bacterial fitness responses to one metal tend to predict responses to another, while the magnitude of that relationship varies with metal chemistry. [src: metal_cross_resistance] This concept connects [[summaries/metal_cross_resistance__REPORT]] and [[summaries/metal_fitness_atlas__REPORT]] to [[summaries/metal_specificity__REPORT]], [[summaries/microbeatlas_metal_ecology__REPORT]], [[summaries/soil_metal_functional_genomics__REPORT]], [[summaries/t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt__REPORT]], [[concepts/cofitness-network-architecture]], [[concepts/pangenome-integration]], [[concepts/environmental-resistome]], and [[concepts/condition-specific-fitness]].

## Evidence for a universal directional layer

The study analyzed 452 metal experiments across 37 organisms and 14 metals, with 119,561 genes having metal fitness data from 28 organisms tested against at least 3 metals. [src: metal_cross_resistance] Across 317 organism–metal-pair observations involving 28 organisms and 85 unique metal pairs, 98.1% of gene-level fitness correlations were positive (311/317), and 99.1% were statistically significant at p < 0.05. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

All 15 metal pairs tested in at least 5 organisms showed greater than 90% sign consistency, and no metal pair showed systematically negative cross-resistance in any organism. [src: metal_cross_resistance] This supports a universal directional layer in which metal conditions tend to share the direction of gene-importance effects rather than producing consistently opposing fitness landscapes. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The newer [[summaries/metal_fitness_atlas__REPORT]] **supports** the breadth of this interpretation at a larger atlas scale: it assembled 559 metal-related experiments across 31 organisms and 16 metals, with 383,349 gene × metal fitness records. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]

The report attributes this shared direction to common cellular disruptions involving protein stability, DNA integrity, membrane function, and cofactor insertion. [src: metal_cross_resistance] Because the study did not include non-metal stress controls, the evidence cannot distinguish universal metal cross-resistance from a broader general-stress response to all tested conditions. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The atlas likewise finds that broad metal-important genes include general stress functions, so this **refines** rather than resolves the causal interpretation. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] The new specificity analysis **supports** the importance of this distinction: among 7,609 metal-important gene records, 4,177 (54.9%) were metal-specific under a <5% sick-rate threshold across 5,945 non-metal experiments, while 2,888 (38.0%) were general sick and 544 (7.2%) were metal+stress. [src: metal_specificity] Thus, the positive cross-metal layer contains both metal-specific determinants and broadly stress-sensitive genes rather than representing one homogeneous response. [src: metal_specificity]

The MicrobeAtlas analysis **refines** the ecological interpretation of this directional layer rather than testing gene-level correlation directly: across 606 genera, metal type diversity predicted broader inferred niche breadth after phylogenetic correction, whereas total AMR cluster count and core AMR fraction did not. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] This supports the idea that breadth across metal types, rather than resistance-gene burden alone, is the ecologically relevant dimension, but the cross-sectional result cannot establish whether resistance expands niche breadth or broad niches promote resistance acquisition. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

## Chemistry-dependent magnitudes

The strongest consensus associations were Fe–Zn (mean r = 0.61, n = 6 organisms), Co–Ni (r = 0.56, n = 28), Co–Zn (r = 0.52, n = 18), Ni–Zn (r = 0.51, n = 18), Cu–Zn (r = 0.48, n = 16), Cu–Fe (r = 0.45, n = 7), Al–Zn (r = 0.44, n = 13), Co–Fe (r = 0.45, n = 7), Co–Cu (r = 0.43, n = 24), and Cu–Ni (r = 0.43, n = 24). [src: metal_cross_resistance] Al was the most independent metal, with a mean r = 0.34, while Al–Ni and Al–Co were the weakest listed pairs at r = 0.34 and r = 0.30, respectively. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

These results support a second, chemistry-specific layer: divalent cations that compete for binding sites may produce stronger associations, whereas metals with more distinctive toxicity mechanisms may produce weaker associations. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The chemistry-specific layer was moderately conserved across organisms, with leave-one-out consensus prediction yielding r = 0.41 and the Mantel mean yielding r = 0.23. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

The cross-resistance direction remained consistent across organisms from Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria. [src: metal_cross_resistance] Co–Ni had median r approximately 0.58 across 28 organisms, whereas Al–Co and Al–Ni had median r approximately 0.30. [src: metal_cross_resistance] Individual-organism matrices repeatedly placed Ni–Co and Co–Zn among the strongest blocks, while Mo was the most independent metal in the 13-metal DvH dataset. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

Pairwise Spearman correlations of metal-pair rankings across 10 common metal pairs and 12 organisms with at least 5 metals showed mostly positive agreement. [src: metal_cross_resistance] Mantel tests across 351 organism pairs produced a mean r = 0.23, with 62% positive values. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The metal-label permutation test was non-significant (p = 0.42), because all metal pairs were positive and label shuffling did not change the mean. [src: metal_cross_resistance] Leave-one-out consensus prediction had mean r = 0.41, although only 2/28 organisms reached individual significance. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

The atlas **refines** this chemistry-level picture by showing stronger core enrichment for essential metals than toxic metals: the mean core-fraction delta was +0.148 for Fe, Mo, W, Se, and Mn versus +0.081 for toxic metals, with one-sided Mann-Whitney U = 39 and p = 0.015. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] This association is conservation-based rather than a replacement for the pairwise correlation analysis, and rare-metal coverage remains uneven. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] Specificity also varied by metal, ranging from 60.6% for manganese (20/33) and 60.5% for molybdenum (185/306) to 21.9% for iron (144/659), showing that the chemistry-dependent layer extends to the fraction of determinants classified as metal-specific. [src: metal_specificity]

MicrobeAtlas provides a complementary, genus-level result: metal type diversity had a positive PGLS coefficient for Levins' B_std niche breadth (β = +0.021, SE = 0.0056, p = 1.5×10⁻⁴), while total AMR cluster count had β = −0.002, SE = 0.0065, p = 0.789 and core AMR fraction had β = +0.006, SE = 0.0054, p = 0.288. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The association remained after controlling for genome size (metal type β = +0.0218, SE = 0.0061, p = 3.6×10⁻⁴), but the strict 5% within-environment prevalence analysis reduced the sample from 606 to 379 genera and yielded p = 0.092. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] This **supports** a distributed breadth signal while **refining** its strength: inferred ecological range is not equivalent to demonstrated metal-specific adaptation.

## Three-tier gene architecture

Among 8,162 metal-important genes across 28 organisms, 1,484 (18.2%) were classified as general stress genes, 2,306 (28.3%) as metal-shared genes, and 4,372 (53.6%) as metal-specific genes. [src: metal_cross_resistance] Here, a pangenome means the combined gene repertoire represented across the organisms or strains being compared. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

Mean pangenome core fractions were 92.0% for general stress genes, 91.0% for metal-shared genes, and 89.8% for metal-specific genes. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The percentages fully core at at least 95% were 57.2%, 50.4%, and 45.7%, respectively. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The fully core gradient therefore spans 11.5 percentage points.

This gradient supports, but does not by itself prove, an evolutionary model progressing from ancestral general stress defense to shared metal defense and then to more accessory, faster-evolving specialized metal-specific resistance. [src: metal_cross_resistance] General stress genes were enriched for energy/respiration and cell-envelope functions, whereas metal-specific genes were enriched for transporters/efflux and iron/metal-related functions. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

The specificity analysis **refines** this three-tier model using explicit non-metal controls. Across 22 organisms with pangenome links, metal-specific genes had a pooled core fraction of 84.8% (2,969/3,500) and an organism-mean core fraction of 88.0%, with a mean delta versus baseline of +6.9%; general sick genes had pooled and organism-mean core fractions of 90.2% (2,183/2,420) and a mean delta of +9.0%. [src: metal_specificity] Metal-specific genes were less core-enriched than general sick genes, and the Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel comparison was significant (p=0.011), while both categories remained strongly core-associated. [src: metal_specificity] This supports a modest two-tier interpretation within the broader three-tier architecture: broadly stress-sensitive functions are more core-enriched than specialized metal determinants, but specialized determinants are not predominantly accessory.

The atlas **supports** the core-heavy end of this model at genome-wide scale: across 22 organisms and 14 metals, broad metal-important genes were 87.4% core versus 76.9% for baseline genes (OR=2.08, p=4.3e-162). [src: metal_fitness_atlas] It also **refines** the comparison by distinguishing this broad definition from the prior condition-specific heavy-metal result of 71.2% core: the atlas includes general cellular processes vulnerable to metal disruption, whereas specialized resistance is more visible after such functions are excluded. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]

The analysis identified 318 ortholog groups that were metal-shared, meaning important for at least 2 metals, in at least 2 organisms. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The most broadly conserved families spanned up to 14 organisms and included cell-envelope, energy-metabolism, DNA-repair, and ion-homeostasis functions. [src: metal_cross_resistance] These results refine [[concepts/pangenome-integration]] by linking pangenome conservation to tiers of cross-metal gene importance rather than treating all metal-associated genes as one class. [src: metal_cross_resistance] Consistently, the atlas found 1,182 ortholog groups with metal phenotypes conserved across at least 2 organisms and 601 across at least 3, while 149 conserved candidates lacked full functional annotation. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]

Metal-specific genes matched metal-resistance keywords at 12.2% versus 7.8% for general sick genes (Fisher exact OR=1.64, p=2.4e-8), which **supports** their interpretation as a distinct resistance-enriched class. [src: metal_specificity] Candidate prioritization further identified UCP030820, YebC, and DUF1043/YhcB as the strongest novel families because they combined 50–67% metal-specificity with lower pleiotropic effects across multiple species; the proposed YebC translation-bottleneck mechanism remains a hypothesis. [src: metal_specificity]

## Environmental validation and its limits

The study tested whether multi-metal tolerance predicted isolation from metal-associated environments using [[entities/bacdive]] and Fitness Browser data. [src: metal_cross_resistance] Multi-metal tolerance scores did not correlate with BacDive isolation from metal environments at the Fitness Browser species scale (Spearman rho approximately -0.02, p > 0.8). [src: metal_cross_resistance]

After excluding 2 organisms without tier-classification data and collapsing multiple Fitness Browser strains from the same species, the effective sample size was 20 independent species, which the report considers too small for a meaningful correlation test. [src: metal_cross_resistance] Genus-plus-species substring matching was imprecise for organisms identified only to genus level, including Acidovorax sp., so those fuzzy matches require caution. [src: metal_cross_resistance] This result limits the environmental inference in [[concepts/environmental-resistome]] rather than disproving an association between cross-resistance gene signatures and metal-associated habitats. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

The MicrobeAtlas study **supports** this cautious interpretation with a larger environmental comparison, but not with a direct test of isolation phenotype. Across 767 genera detected in 1,624 groundwater samples, metal type diversity correlated with groundwater prevalence (Spearman ρ = +0.112, p = 0.0019), while groundwater-specific fold-enrichment was null (ρ = +0.042, p = 0.242). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] Thus, broader metal repertoires track occurrence in groundwater at a weak prevalence level, but the result does not demonstrate groundwater-specific enrichment or a contamination mechanism. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

A second MicrobeAtlas validation found community-weighted mean metal-type diversity differed across 8 wells (Kruskal–Wallis H = 29.10, p = 0.0001), increased with time point (Spearman ρ = +0.383, p < 0.0001), and increased within FW216 (ρ = +0.576, p = 0.0001). [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] However, only 16.8% of reads were joinable to genus-level metal-diversity data, and the report notes that the dominant Sulfurimonas trend decreased with time, illustrating that ecological dominance can be driven by electron-acceptor availability rather than metal resistance. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] This **refines** the environmental-resistome interpretation: community-level metal-diversity gradients are detectable, but incomplete taxonomic coverage and competing environmental controls prevent treating them as resistance-driven community assembly.

The atlas **extends** the environmental question to pangenome-scale prediction but does not establish a simple gene-presence predictor: a 1,286-KEGG-KO metal signature scored 27,702 pangenome species, and genome-size-normalized scores placed Leptospirillum at the 91st percentile, Acidithiobacillus at the 77th, Marinobacter at the 75th, and Sulfobacillus at the 71st; bioleaching genera were not significantly enriched over background (Mann-Whitney p=0.17). [src: metal_fitness_atlas] The simple repertoire score failed to predict metal fitness, **supporting** the need to distinguish cross-resistance fitness effects from gene presence alone. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]

The new soil functional-genomics analysis **refines** these environmental validations by measuring associations between metal concentrations and community functional gene content rather than isolation or prevalence directly. Across 51,748 soil samples and nine metals—copper, cobalt, chromium, nickel, zinc, lead, arsenic, cadmium, and mercury—it identified 2,355 significant COG–metal associations at FDR < 0.05; COG means a Cluster of Orthologous Groups functional category and FDR means false discovery rate. Chromium and lead produced the strongest signals, with transporters including ABC and RND systems and biosynthesis genes dominating the top hits. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] A db-RDA model, where db-RDA is distance-based redundancy analysis, yielded R² = 0.799 and p = 0.005 using 999 permutations; after conditioning on batch and project effects, metal concentrations explained 80% of variance in community COG profiles. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] Biome-stratified PGLS, or phylogenetic generalized least squares, found distinct metal–COG relationships in soil, marine, and wastewater environments, supporting environment-specific functional responses rather than a universal resistance programme. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

This soil result **supports** the environmental-resistome concept by showing that environmental metal gradients coincide with functional shifts, but it does not validate the fitness-based cross-resistance architecture or establish metal-specific resistance. The analysis is observational; chromium, copper, lead, and zinc co-vary in many industrial soils, so individual associations may reflect generic multi-metal contamination. The conditional db-RDA R² = 0.799 describes residual variance after project accession was removed and may be substantially higher than the unconditional metal-only R²; the latter was not reported. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

The T4SS–CAZy analysis **extends** this environmental evidence from community functional profiles to gene-mobility neighborhoods. In 376 genomes, GT2-neighborhood MAGs had a mean of 0.045 metal-resistance types versus 0.004 for non-GT2 MAGs (n=260,276), with Mann–Whitney p=8.6e-27; genomes with GT2 in T4SS-proximal neighborhoods carried 11× more metal-resistance genes. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] This **supports** a link between environmental horizontal-transfer hubs and resistance breadth, but the association is observational and does not establish that T4SS transfer causes metal-resistance acquisition. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

## Tensions

The species-scale BacDive validation was null, with Spearman rho approximately -0.02 and p > 0.8, whereas the prior [[entities/metal-fitness-atlas]] validation reported Cohen's d = +1.0 from pangenome-scale analysis of 42K strains. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The results therefore leave unresolved whether the discrepancy reflects insufficient power and imprecise matching in the species-scale test or a genuine difference between multi-metal fitness signatures and environmental isolation. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

The MicrobeAtlas groundwater prevalence association (ρ = +0.112, p = 0.0019) **supports** a weak environmental occurrence relationship, but its null groundwater-specific fold-enrichment test (ρ = +0.042, p = 0.242) does not resolve the BacDive tension. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The datasets differ in environmental definition, taxonomic aggregation, and outcome, so they should not be combined into a single validation estimate. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

The soil analysis adds a related tension: its conditional db-RDA model reports R² = 0.799 and p = 0.005 after conditioning on batch and project effects, whereas the BacDive and groundwater tests address environmental occurrence and isolation rather than residual community functional variance. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] These results cannot be treated as convergent effect estimates; the soil signal may partly reflect project structure and has not yet been spatially or effect-size validated. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

The T4SS result **adds** a related observational association rather than resolving this tension: GT2-neighborhood MAGs showed 0.045 versus 0.004 metal-resistance types and an 11× difference in metal-resistance genes, but these data concern syntenic gene neighborhoods and not fitness, isolation, or prevalence outcomes. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] Thus, the new result supports environmental coupling between mobility and resistance breadth while remaining non-comparable to the BacDive, groundwater, and soil effect estimates. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

The report also identifies a tension between strong universal positivity and uncertainty about its cause: all tested metal pairs were positive, but no non-metal stress controls were included. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The [[sources/counter_ion_effects__REPORT]] project is identified as partially addressing this issue, but the present study cannot independently separate metal-specific cross-resistance from a general-stress response. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The specificity analysis **supports** the existence of a substantial metal-specific component, but also found 38.0% general-sick records and 7.2% metal+stress records, so it does not eliminate the causal ambiguity. [src: metal_specificity]

The soil study **reinforces** this causal tension: its 2,355 discoveries among 3,915 implied tests represent a 60% discovery rate, but positive correlation among co-contaminating metals may make Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction anti-conservative and the true FDR higher than reported. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] Effect sizes were not systematically reported, and planned audits will flag associations with rho < 0.05; therefore statistical significance does not yet demonstrate substantial biological effect. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

A further definitional tension is that the three-tier analysis found metal-specific genes to be 89.8% core, while the atlas found broad metal-important genes to be 87.4% core versus 76.9% baseline and explicitly attributes much of its signal to general cellular vulnerability. [src: metal_cross_resistance, metal_fitness_atlas] These values should not be reconciled as a single estimate: they use different gene classes, organism sets, and analytical definitions. [src: metal_cross_resistance, metal_fitness_atlas] The specificity analysis adds a third estimate—84.8% pooled core for metal-specific genes and 90.2% for general sick genes—but likewise uses different inclusion and classification rules, so it **refines** rather than resolves this tension. [src: metal_specificity]

## Limitations

Metal concentrations differed among experiments, so dose-response effects could influence cross-resistance estimates. [src: metal_cross_resistance] Organisms ranged from 3 to 112 metal experiments, which affected the reliability of per-organism matrices. [src: metal_cross_resistance] The 28 organisms were not phylogenetically independent, and formal PGLS, or phylogenetic generalized least-squares, and independent-contrast analyses would strengthen the conservation claim. [src: metal_cross_resistance]

The report gives n = 26 at the Fitness Browser organism scale in its limitations section but reports 20 independent species after matching and collapsing in the validation results. [src: metal_cross_resistance] This discrepancy should be retained rather than reconciled silently, because both values describe different stages or definitions of the validation sample in the source report.

The atlas adds that metal coverage was uneven, concentrations were not normalized by dose or tolerance threshold, and its primary conservation analysis covered 22 of 31 metal-tested organisms; excluding four duplicate *P. fluorescens* FW300 strains still gave OR=2.065 and p=5.9e-141. [src: metal_fitness_atlas] Its broad definition, fit < -1 OR n_sick ≥ 1, captures approximately 3.3% of genes and includes general stress genes, while putatively essential genes—approximately 14.3% of protein-coding genes and approximately 82% core—lack transposon insertions and are absent from fitness data. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]

The specificity analysis excluded 5,229 of 12,838 metal-important gene records because seven of 31 metal-tested organisms had locusId mismatches; 24 included organisms contributed 7,609 records. [src: metal_specificity] Its 5% sick-rate threshold was arbitrary, although results were qualitatively stable across 1–20%, and validation against Fitness Browser `specificphenotype` annotations was not performed. [src: metal_specificity] ICA, or independent component analysis, identified 0 metal-specific modules, but the analysis used raw module-condition scores rather than the precomputed z-scored profiles that identified 600 metal-responsive module records in the Metal Atlas analysis. [src: metal_specificity]

MicrobeAtlas adds that niche breadth is a sequencing-effort proxy rather than confirmed ecological range; genus aggregation can combine different species occupying different habitats; pangenome coverage correlated with inferred metal type diversity at r = 0.35, p = 2.6×10⁻¹⁹; and 83% of Track B reads could not be joined to genus-level AMR annotations. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology] The strict prevalence analysis, heterogeneous environment categories, possible AMRFinderPlus cross-reactivity, and lack of manual validation further limit causal environmental interpretation. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]

The soil analysis adds unresolved confounding and validation limits: co-contamination prevents separating individual-metal effects from generic multi-metal stress; unconditional db-RDA R² was not reported; systematic effect sizes are unavailable; spatial residual autocorrelation has not yet been tested; and the copper-specific analysis matched soil samples to nearby KBase genomes using a 10 km criterion whose attribution requires 5 km and 20 km sensitivity analyses. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics] Planned tests include Moran’s I, with SEVM if spatial autocorrelation is significant, partial correlation of COG ~ Cr | Cu + Zn + Pb, mechanistic classification of COGs, and reruns from the `kescience_mgnify` and `kbase_ke_pangenome` Spark tables. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]

The T4SS–CAZy analysis has corresponding validation limits: its ≤10 kb co-occurrence threshold remains pending validation; CAZy genes were not detected on plasmids by ICEfinder; and the proposed chromosomal or integrative transfer route is consistent with, but does not prove, T4SS-mediated dissemination. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt] The GT2 neighborhood–metal-resistance association is likewise observational and may reflect shared ecological or genomic backgrounds rather than transfer-mediated resistance acquisition. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]

## Open Directions

- Apply KEGG/PFAM mapping to cross-resistance gene signatures across 27K species, then test whether the signatures predict metal-associated isolation at pangenome scale and whether the result reproduces Cohen's d = +1.0. [src: metal_cross_resistance]
- Normalize fitness effects by metal concentration relative to MIC, then retest the 85 metal-pair associations to determine how much of the chemistry-specific magnitude layer is dose-driven. [src: metal_cross_resistance]
- Add non-metal stress controls and use ICA, or independent component analysis, of metal-condition modules to test whether the 98.1% positive correlation pattern is metal-specific or a general-stress axis. [src: metal_cross_resistance]
- Use phylogenetic independent contrasts or PGLS across the 28 organisms to test whether cross-resistance direction and metal-pair ranking remain conserved after accounting for shared ancestry. [src: metal_cross_resistance]
- Use AlphaFold-based structural analysis of the 318 conserved metal-shared ortholog groups to ask whether shared structural features explain their cross-metal importance. [src: metal_cross_resistance]
- Rebuild the BacDive validation with explicit species-level matching, the reported sample definitions, and independent species counts to determine whether environmental isolation is associated with multi-metal gene signatures. [src: metal_cross_resistance]
- Reanalyze atlas genes using a metal-specific definition that excludes genes important for other stresses, then compare core enrichment and cross-metal correlations with the three-tier classes. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]
- Test the atlas's 149 uncharacterized candidates with PaperBLAST, InterPro, and structural prediction, and evaluate whether regulatory or expression features outperform gene-presence scores for predicting metal fitness. [src: metal_fitness_atlas]
- Resolve the specificity-analysis locusId mismatches, validate classifications against Fitness Browser `specificphenotype` annotations, and repeat the counter-ion comparison using fit < -1 without the |t| > 4 requirement. [src: metal_specificity]
- Use precomputed z-scored Metal Atlas module activities and AlphaFold predictions for UCP030820, YebC, and DUF1043/YhcB to test whether candidate-specificity signals reflect coherent modules or plausible metal-binding structures. [src: metal_specificity]
- Reanalyze MicrobeAtlas with finer marine, freshwater, and saline categories, test alternative phylogenetic models, and relate metal type diversity to site-level contamination metadata to distinguish ecological breadth from sampling and habitat effects. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]
- Test whether covered-read fraction biases Track B community-weighted metal diversity and whether the groundwater prevalence signal persists after species-level rarefaction and explicit genome-coverage correction. [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]
- Rerun the soil COG–metal models with partial correlation, including COG ~ Cr | Cu + Zn + Pb, to test whether apparent chromium associations remain after co-contaminating metals are controlled. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]
- Report unconditional and conditional db-RDA R² values, audit Spearman rho effect sizes including rho < 0.05, and test residual Moran’s I with SEVM if needed to separate metal, project, and spatial structure. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]
- Repeat copper genome–soil attribution at 5 km, 10 km, and 20 km, then compare mechanistically classified resistance, stress, membrane, energy, and unknown COGs across biomes. [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]
- Validate the ≤10 kb T4SS–CAZy synteny threshold with permutation tests, test Node_4915 against NCBI nr and a housekeeping-gene null, and determine whether GT2-neighborhood metal-resistance enrichment persists after controlling for genome background and biome. [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]
