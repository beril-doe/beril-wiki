# Gene length confounds fitness-based prediction of pangenome conservation

Gene length is a major confounder when using experimental fitness effects to predict whether a gene is conserved in a pangenome, because length affects both the reliability of transposon-fitness measurement and the probability that a gene is classified as core. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] This means that an apparent relationship between fitness importance and conservation should not be interpreted as independent of gene length unless models explicitly control for it. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

## Key evidence

The analysis combined RB-TnSeq (random barcode transposon sequencing) fitness data from 757 *Desulfovibrio vulgaris* Hildenborough experiments with pangenome classifications for 2,725 non-essential genes that had both fitness measurements and pangenome links. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Of these genes, 76.3% were classified as core, establishing the baseline against which condition-specific conservation was compared. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

Models using field fitness alone had a cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (CV-AUC) of 0.517 with standard deviation 0.052, while lab fitness alone reached 0.531 with standard deviation 0.052. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Combining field and lab fitness increased performance only to 0.548 with standard deviation 0.053. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Adding gene length increased the full-model CV-AUC to 0.645 with standard deviation 0.068, making gene length substantially more predictive of core status than either fitness dimension alone. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The broader Fitness Browser–pangenome analysis **supports** the conclusion that fitness importance is a weak predictor of conservation: across 194,216 protein-coding genes from 43 bacteria, essential genes were 82% core whereas always-neutral genes were 66% core, despite a statistically robust gradient. [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis] This **refines** the interpretation of the single-organism AUC results: a real conservation–fitness association can coexist with limited predictive power and substantial gene-level confounding. [src: fitness_effects_conservation; conservation_fitness_synthesis] The new analysis did not test gene length as a covariate, so it does not displace the stronger length-adjusted prediction observed in *D. vulgaris*. [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis]

The conservation_vs_fitness analysis **supports** the existence of a real but modest conservation–essentiality association: among 33 organisms, essential genes were 86.1% core versus 81.2% for non-essential genes, with a median odds ratio of 1.56; 18 of 33 organisms showed significant enrichment after Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] Because essentiality was inferred from RB-TnSeq under library-construction and growth conditions, this result also **supports** treating perturbation callability and condition coverage as limits on fitness-based prediction rather than interpreting essentiality as a context-free property. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]

The new synthesis further **supports** this calibrated interpretation by identifying 28,017 genes that were both costly in the laboratory and conserved, alongside 5,526 genes that were costly and dispensable; it presents these categories as evidence for, rather than direct measurements of, natural selection. [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis] Thus, integrating conservation with fitness can improve biological interpretation, but these categories do not remove the need to control measurement and genomic-architecture confounding. [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis]

The core_gene_tradeoffs analysis **refines** this interpretation by showing that the burden–conservation relationship is function-specific rather than a uniform property of core genes: core genes were more burdensome in Protein Metabolism, Motility, and RNA Metabolism, whereas non-core Cell Wall genes were more burdensome. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs] It also identified 25,271 condition-dependent trade-off genes, or 17.8% of the genes examined, which were 1.29 times more likely to be core than non-core genes (odds ratio 1.29, p=1.2e-44). [src: core_gene_tradeoffs] This **supports** the view that biological trade-offs coexist with weak predictive power and measurement confounding, but the analysis did not test gene length as a covariate. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs]

This result supports [[concepts/condition-specific-fitness]] by showing that the predictive value of measured fitness is limited when conservation is modeled without accounting for gene-level structural covariates. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] It also refines [[concepts/pangenome-integration]] because integrating fitness with core/auxiliary status requires adjustment for gene length rather than treating fitness as the sole explanatory variable. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] The broader analyses further **refine** this integration problem by showing that fitness breadth and effect magnitude track conservation only weakly, while condition-specific effects can be enriched among core genes rather than being restricted to accessory genes. [src: fitness_effects_conservation; conservation_fitness_synthesis]

## Why the confounding arises

Short genes receive fewer transposon insertions, which can reduce the quality and stability of their fitness estimates. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Core genes also tend to be longer, so gene length is associated with the outcome being predicted as well as with the measurement process. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] The resulting association can make gene length appear to explain conservation directly even when part of its predictive value reflects better fitness callability or other correlated genomic properties. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The conservation_vs_fitness analysis **supports** this measurement-bias interpretation: its validation found that essential genes were slightly shorter on average, consistent with insertion bias in transposon data. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] It therefore provides independent evidence that gene length can affect essentiality calls, although it did not estimate a length-adjusted conservation model and does not establish how much of the observed core enrichment is attributable to length. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]

The exclusion of 678 essential genes further limits interpretation of the fitness models because these genes lacked recovered transposon mutants and therefore had no condition-specific fitness data. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Those excluded essential genes were 80.1% core, compared with the 76.3% core fraction among the 2,725 non-essential genes analyzed with fitness and pangenome data. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Consequently, the reported AUC values describe prediction among genes with usable non-essential-gene fitness measurements, not prediction across the complete pangenome. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] This limitation connects the concept to [[concepts/gene-essentiality]], where absence of perturbation data is itself informative rather than random. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The broader analysis **supports** this concern about missing or uneven perturbation evidence: singleton and novel genes showed near-zero mean fitness under the tested laboratory conditions, but the report notes that apparent neutrality may reflect poor transposon coverage rather than true neutrality. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] It also found that essential genes were 82% core across its broader dataset, a result that is directionally consistent with the 80.1% core fraction among essential genes excluded from the *D. vulgaris* models, although the populations and analyses are different. [src: fitness_effects_conservation; field_vs_lab_fitness] The new analysis similarly cautions that its essential-gene definition is an upper bound because missing fitness data can reflect short genes, low-complexity regions, or scaffold edges rather than essentiality. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]

## Interpretation and scope

The weak performance of field and lab fitness alone indicates that ecological condition class is not sufficient to predict pangenome conservation in this dataset. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] The stronger performance of the model containing gene length indicates that measurement and genomic-architecture effects must be separated from biological fitness effects before claiming that fitness importance predicts conservation. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] This is a single-organism result from *Desulfovibrio vulgaris* Hildenborough, and its generalizability to organisms with larger accessory genomes has not been established. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The broader 43-bacterium analysis **supports** the first part of this interpretation: fitness breadth was positively but weakly associated with core status (Spearman rho=0.086, p=8.1e-230), and the overall gradient spanned approximately 194,000 genes. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] It **refines** the condition-specific interpretation, however, because genes with strong condition-specific effects were 77.3% core versus 70.3% for genes without such annotations, and 4,450 genes (2.7%) were neutral overall but critical in one condition. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] Thus, condition-specific fitness is not by itself evidence of accessory status; length, callability, and pangenome structure remain necessary controls.

The analysis also found that field and lab fitness effects were correlated at approximately r ~ 0.7, so genes that were deleterious in one context were often deleterious in the other. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] This correlation reduces the ability of separate field and lab fitness dimensions to distinguish ecological context, making adjustment for gene length especially important when comparing their predictive contributions. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] The relevant inferential issue is therefore related to [[concepts/statistical-significance-versus-effect-size]]: the full-model performance difference is a predictive effect, but it does not by itself establish a causal role for gene length in evolutionary conservation. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The new cross-organism synthesis **refines** the scope of the inference by showing that conservation is not an inert proxy for laboratory fitness: core genes were more often burdensome when deleted, with 24.4% showing positive fitness compared with 19.9% of accessory genes, and were 1.78x more likely to have strong condition-specific phenotypes. [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis] This pattern does not contradict the length-confounding result; it indicates that biological trade-offs coexist with measurement and architecture effects, so neither conservation nor fitness should be interpreted in isolation. [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis]

The core_gene_tradeoffs analysis **supports** this trade-off interpretation while limiting its evolutionary scope: it classified 28,017 genes as Costly + Conserved and 5,526 as Costly + Dispensable, but treated these groups as signatures consistent with selection rather than direct measurements of selection in nature. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs] Its use of laboratory conditions as an impoverished proxy for nature, together with fitness-burden and condition biases, **reinforces** the need to distinguish ecological interpretation from length- and callability-adjusted prediction. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs]

The new cross-organism analysis also **refines** the scope of the inference by showing that pangenome category itself has technical limits: pangenome coverage varied among clades, and clades with only 2 genomes could have trivially high core fractions; 10 organisms were excluded for <90% DIAMOND coverage, and Dyella79 was excluded because a locus-tag mismatch produced a 0% join rate. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] These limitations reinforce that observed conservation–fitness relationships require control for both gene-level callability and uneven pangenome integration. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]

## Open Directions

- Use the `gene_fitness_conservation.csv` data for the 2,725 analyzed genes, together with insertion counts or other transposon-callability measures, in nested cross-validated models to test how much of gene length's CV-AUC 0.645 contribution remains after measurement quality is modeled explicitly. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Add the 678 essential genes through a missing-fitness or two-stage model, then test whether gene length predicts core status similarly among essential genes and genes with recovered transposon mutants. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Replace binary core/auxiliary labels with quantitative gene-cluster prevalence and fit length-adjusted regression models to ask whether gene length primarily predicts the core boundary or also predicts intermediate prevalence across the pangenome. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Reanalyze fitness across the reported thresholds from -1 to -3 with gene length, insertion coverage, and fitness uncertainty as covariates to test whether length confounding changes condition-specific conservation patterns. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Extend the length- and callability-adjusted analysis across the 194,216-gene, 43-bacterium comparison to test whether the weak fitness–conservation gradient persists after measurement quality and lineage are controlled. [src: fitness_effects_conservation; conservation_fitness_synthesis]
- Reanalyze the 33-organism conservation_vs_fitness dataset with gene length, insertion coverage, pangenome clade size, and quantitative cluster prevalence to determine whether the median odds ratio of 1.56 persists after callability and pangenome-coverage confounding are controlled. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]
- Test whether the 28,017 costly-and-conserved and 5,526 costly-and-dispensable genes show different length, insertion-callability, mobile-element, or prevalence profiles before interpreting them as signatures of natural selection or gene loss. [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis]
- Stratify the 25,271 trade-off genes and the Costly + Conserved and Costly + Dispensable groups by gene length, insertion callability, functional category, and pangenome prevalence to test whether the reported core enrichment persists after structural and measurement adjustment. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs]
