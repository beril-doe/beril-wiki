# Compound identifier ambiguity in linking metabolites to metabolic models

This page is about compound identifier ambiguity in linking metabolites to metabolic models. Exact-name matching gives few but confident links, formula-only matching gives more but ambiguous ones, and some links fail outright. [src: discoveries, webofmicrobes_explorer]

## Literature Context

The candidate papers touch the corpus's topic only indirectly. None of them tests exact-name against formula-only matching of measured metabolites to a biochemistry database, so none directly confirms or contradicts the hub's match rates.

The closest paper is [PMID 32933023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32933023/). It builds compound coloring identifiers from atom-level graph coloring to harmonize compounds across metabolic databases. With these identifiers it detects 8865 correspondences between KEGG and MetaCyc compounds. Of these, 5451 are confirmed by other identifiers provided by the two databases. A further 1848 unconfirmed pairs are validated by commonality in reaction Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers. The authors also report issues and errors in compound representation in KEGG and MetaCyc. The paper does not compare name-based with ID-based linking and does not say either is unreliable. Its relevance is that it takes a structure-derived identifier route to cross-database compound matching. That is the kind of route the hub proposes with InChIKey and CHEBI in place of names. It also shows that database-side representation problems can affect linking. The hub likewise lists database-side gaps, such as sparse SMILES and InChIKey and missing EC mapping on `reaction`.

[PMID 27983783](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27983783/) addresses a different problem, which is identifying metabolites that are absent from databases. Its metabolic pathway extension approach matches mass differences between metabolite pairs to typical metabolic reactions. In a mouse carnitine dosing study, it putatively identified 93 of 97 altered metabolites, while only half of them could be traced from currently available online databases. This is a mouse study with putative identifications. It shows that database lookup alone can leave many measured metabolites unidentified. That is loosely consistent with the 81 unmatched compounds (31.5%) in the hub. The hub does not attribute those failures to database coverage, so the parallel is only suggestive.

Several other candidates map measured metabolites to pathways or databases as a routine step. [PMID 41323972](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41323972/) identified metabolites with HMDB and Metlin in a study of 89 pregnant women, then mapped 528 differential metabolites to KEGG pathways. [PMID 34617154](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34617154/) mapped 39 altered metabolites onto a KEGG-derived human metabolic network. [PMID 40718131](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40718131/) retrieved KEGG pathways for differential metabolites. [PMID 35387615](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35387615/) built disease-metabolite associations from database mappings. These abstracts do not say how metabolites were matched or how ambiguity was handled. They therefore neither support nor contradict the hub's confidence grading. The osteoarthritis NMR review [PMID 33290503](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33290503/) calls for standardization of metabolite identifications under the Metabolomics Standards Initiative. This is consistent with the hub's caution that formula-only matches are candidate sets, not definitive identifications.

Overall, the corpus's findings are largely novel relative to these candidates. They include the 26.8% exact-name rate, the 1:8.4 formula expansion, the leucine/isoleucine substring collision and the failed GapMind link. The candidates give only general context, in the form of structure-based harmonization and calls for standardized identification.

## Exact-name versus formula-only matches

Of 257 identified non-unknown [[entities/web-of-microbes|Web of Microbes]] (WoM) compounds, 69 match ModelSEED molecules by exact name and 107 by formula, totaling 176 (68.5%). [src: discoveries] The exact-name matches (26.8%) are rated high confidence with a 1:1 mapping. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer] The formula-only matches cover 107 WoM compounds (41.6%) and expand to 900 ModelSEED molecules, an average of 1:8.4 and low confidence. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer] A further 81 compounds (31.5%) are unmatched. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer] The 176 links are therefore of mixed confidence. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer] The report rates the linkage as moderate [src: webofmicrobes_explorer] and says only 26.8% are definitive, against 68.5% when ambiguous formula matches are counted. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer]

The ambiguity is concrete. The formula C5H11NO2 matches valine, norvaline, betaine, 5-aminopentanoate and others. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer] Formula matches are therefore candidate sets for manual curation, not definitive identifications. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer] The generated files hold 75 exact-name rows and 900 formula rows. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer] The report uses the [[entities/modelseed-biochemistry|ModelSEED]] molecule table in `kbase_msd_biochemistry` for this matching. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer]

## Failed links

Simple name matching failed to link WoM metabolites to [[entities/gapmind|GapMind]] pathways. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer] GapMind internal pathway names do not contain simple metabolite names, so the link is blocked. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer] The report says a dedicated pathway-to-metabolite table would enable it. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer] It proposes building one by mapping each pathway to substrate and product metabolites from the GapMind documentation. [src: webofmicrobes_explorer]

## Sparse identifiers and string matching in NMDC

Where identifiers are sparse, matching falls back to names. Only ~2% of compounds in `nmdc_arkin.metabolomics_gold` have KEGG compound IDs (identifiers) populated. [src: discoveries, nmdc_community_metabolic_ecology] The table does carry `kegg`, `chebi`, `name`, `inchi`, `inchikey` and `smiles` fields. [src: pitfalls] Pathway matching therefore relies on compound-name substring patterns rather than KEGG-based ontologies. [src: discoveries, nmdc_community_metabolic_ecology]

Substring matching can produce wrong links. The pattern 'leucine' matches inside 'isoleucine', so the report recommends first-match-wins ordering with longer, more specific patterns first. [src: discoveries] Automated review found isoleucine compounds matching the leucine pattern. This was corrected, and reported results reflect the corrected run. [src: nmdc_community_metabolic_ecology] Three misassigned isoleucine compounds had near-zero completeness-versus-metabolite correlation and diluted the leucine signal. [src: nmdc_community_metabolic_ecology] The fix strengthened the leucine signal from r −0.326 to −0.390 and q 0.045 to 0.022. [src: nmdc_community_metabolic_ecology] Some metabolites stay untestable because they were never detected; cysteine, histidine and lysine are examples. [src: nmdc_community_metabolic_ecology] Chorismate is rarely detected. Shikimic acid and 3-dehydroshikimic acid stand in for it, but they reflect precursor availability, not chorismate pool size. [src: discoveries]

## Approximate matches in the FW300-N2E3 crosswalk

Approximate matches were present but changed little. Two Fitness Browser (FB) matches are base-to-nucleoside mappings, Cytosine→Cytidine and Uracil→Uridine, flagged `fb_match_quality=approximate`. [src: fw300_metabolic_consistency] Excluding them reduces testable metabolites from 21 to 19 and shifts mean concordance from 0.937 to 0.930. The report calls this negligible because both were already fully concordant. [src: fw300_metabolic_consistency] A sensitivity analysis confirms the concordance results are unaffected. [src: fw300_metabolic_consistency] Low matching rates there reflect nomenclature differences and scope differences. Many WoM metabolites, such as N-acetylated amino acids and nucleotide derivatives, have not been tested in FB, BacDive or GapMind. [src: fw300_metabolic_consistency] The report cautions that additional matches may have been missed because of nomenclature differences. [src: fw300_metabolic_consistency] It proposes chemical identifiers (InChIKey, CHEBI) rather than name matching to expand the WoM–BacDive overlap beyond 8 metabolites. [src: fw300_metabolic_consistency]

## Database-side identifier gaps

The biochemistry tables also limit linking. `reaction.abbreviation` holds KEGG R-numbers for only ~80% of reactions (44,904 / 56,012), and the other 20% have no external ID. [src: pitfalls] No EC (Enzyme Commission) mapping exists on `reaction`. The report suggests bridging through eggNOG `KEGG_ko` / `KEGG_Reaction`, or using GapMind for pathway-level analysis. [src: pitfalls] BiGG_Reaction appears in eggNOG annotations but not in ModelSEED tables, so there is no BiGG mapping on the biochemistry side. [src: pitfalls] ModelSEED IDs use prefix-colon forms such as `seed.reaction:rxn00001` and `seed.compound:cpd00001`. [src: pitfalls] Many `molecule` rows lack SMILES or InChIKey, so the report advises LEFT JOIN to `structure`. [src: pitfalls] See also [[concepts/join-key-validity-and-identifier-format-mismatches]].

## Open Directions

- Rebuild the WoM–ModelSEED link using InChIKey and CHEBI instead of names. This would test how many of the 107 formula-only compounds resolve to one molecule.
- Build the GapMind pathway-to-metabolite table and count how many WoM compounds then link. This closes the failed WoM–GapMind link.
- Fill the sparse NMDC KEGG IDs by mapping on `inchikey` or `chebi`, and compare the results with the substring-based pathway assignments.
- Audit the other NMDC name patterns for collisions like leucine/isoleucine, and report each one's effect on the results.
