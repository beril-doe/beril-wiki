# Fitness importance predicts genome conservation more strongly than field-versus-lab context

## Core claim

In *Desulfovibrio vulgaris* Hildenborough (DvH), strong fitness importance was more consistently associated with genome conservation than whether a condition was classified as field or laboratory. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] This conclusion used 757 Fitness Browser experiments, 2,725 non-essential genes with both fitness data and pangenome links, and 52 independent component analysis (ICA) fitness modules. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] The source analysis is summarized at [[summaries/field_vs_lab_fitness__REPORT]].

A broader analysis **supports** this interpretation: across approximately 194,000 genes from 43 diverse bacteria, fitness importance and pangenome conservation showed a positive but weak association; essential genes were 82% core versus always-neutral genes at 66% core. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] Fitness breadth also correlated positively with core status (Spearman rho=0.086, p=8.1e-230), indicating that repeated or strong fitness effects carry more conservation signal than a field-versus-lab label. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] The comparison is complementary rather than identical because the broader analysis pooled organisms and conditions while the DvH analysis explicitly tested ecological context.

The conservation_fitness_synthesis analysis **refines** this conclusion by identifying 28,017 genes that were both costly in the laboratory and conserved, alongside 5,526 genes that were costly and dispensable; these categories are evidence for, rather than direct measurements of, natural selection. [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis] Thus, biological fitness–conservation associations coexist with limited predictive power and substantial gene-level confounding.

## Evidence from condition classes

Among genes with fitness below -2, field-stress genes had the highest core-genome fraction at 83.6% across 298 genes (OR=1.58 versus baseline, FDR q=0.026), followed by field-core genes at 82.4% across 376 genes (OR=1.46, q=0.026), lab-other genes at 81.5% across 292 genes (OR=1.37, q=0.073), and lab-nutrient genes at 81.4% across 452 genes (OR=1.36, q=0.037). [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] FDR means false discovery rate, and the reported q values were calculated after Benjamini–Hochberg correction. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

This **supports** [[concepts/condition-specific-fitness]] by connecting condition-specific fitness effects to pangenome conservation, but it **refines** that relationship: field classification did not consistently outperform laboratory classification. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Lab-antibiotic genes were 73.4% core across 109 genes (OR=0.86, q=0.49), and heavy-metal genes were 71.2% core across 198 genes (OR=0.77, q=0.14), lower than the 76.3% baseline for all analyzed non-essential genes but not significantly depleted after correction. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The ranking was stable across thresholds from -1 to -3: field-stress genes were 89.4%, 83.6%, 84.0%, and 82.1% core at thresholds -3.0, -2.0, -1.5, and -1.0, respectively, while heavy-metal genes were 70.6%, 71.2%, 76.7%, and 76.7% at those same thresholds. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Field-stress was highest at every tested threshold, whereas heavy-metals was consistently lowest. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The cross-bacterial analysis **supports** the importance component but qualifies its strength: genes with strong or broad fitness effects were more conserved, although the effect was weak overall, while genes with strong condition-specific annotations were 77.3% core versus 70.3% without such annotations (OR=1.78, p=1.8e-97). [src: fitness_effects_conservation] This **refines** the DvH result because condition-specific effects need not be predominantly accessory; core genes can also have conditional effects. [src: fitness_effects_conservation]

## Evidence from specificity and prediction

The specificity analysis found 50 lab-specific genes that were 96.0% core, 52 field-specific genes that were 88.5% core, 89 field-biased genes that were 83.1% core, 352 universal genes that were 79.8% core, and 2,083 neutral genes that were 74.5% core. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Universal genes were significantly more core than neutral genes (OR=1.35, p=0.033), whereas lab-specific versus field-specific genes did not differ significantly (Fisher exact OR=0.32, p=0.27). [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] These results **support** the interpretation that repeated or strong fitness importance predicts conservation more reliably than the field-versus-lab label.

Logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validated area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (CV-AUC) produced weak performance for field fitness alone (0.517, standard deviation 0.052), lab fitness alone (0.531, 0.052), and field plus lab fitness (0.548, 0.053). [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Adding gene length increased performance to 0.645 (0.068), making length more predictive of core status than either fitness dimension in this model. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] This **refines** [[concepts/pangenome-integration]]: fitness and pangenome status can be integrated, but ecological context alone is not a strong conservation predictor and length must be modeled.

The broader analysis **supports** the direction of this result but qualifies it: essential genes were 82% core, genes affecting 20+ experiments were 79% core, and genes with 0 experiments were 66% core, yet the association between fitness breadth and core status remained weak. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] Singleton genes were near-neutral under tested laboratory conditions, although poor transposon coverage may partly explain that apparent neutrality. [src: fitness_effects_conservation]

The conservation_vs_fitness analysis **supports** a real but modest conservation–essentiality association: among 33 organisms, essential genes were 86.1% core versus 81.2% for non-essential genes, with a median odds ratio of 1.56; 18 of 33 organisms showed significant enrichment after Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] Because essentiality was inferred from RB-TnSeq (random barcode transposon sequencing) under library-construction and growth conditions, this **supports** treating perturbation callability and condition coverage as limits on fitness-based prediction rather than treating essentiality as context-free. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]

## Gene length, callability, and missing fitness data

Gene length is a major confounder because it affects both the reliability of transposon-fitness measurement and the probability that a gene is classified as core. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Short genes receive fewer transposon insertions, reducing the quality and stability of fitness estimates, while core genes tend to be longer. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Length therefore associates with both the measurement process and the predicted outcome; its predictive value may reflect better fitness callability or correlated genomic properties rather than an independent causal role in evolutionary conservation. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The conservation_vs_fitness analysis **supports** this measurement-bias interpretation: essential genes were slightly shorter on average, consistent with insertion bias in transposon data. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] However, it did not estimate a length-adjusted conservation model and does not establish how much of the observed core enrichment is attributable to length. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]

The DvH models excluded 678 essential genes because no transposon mutants were recovered; those genes were 80.1% core, compared with 76.3% among the 2,725 non-essential genes analyzed with fitness and pangenome data. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Thus, the reported AUC values describe prediction among genes with usable non-essential-gene fitness measurements, not prediction across the complete pangenome. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] This connects to [[concepts/gene-essentiality]], where absence of perturbation data is itself informative rather than random.

The broader analysis **supports** the same concern: singleton and novel genes showed near-zero mean fitness under tested laboratory conditions, but apparent neutrality may reflect poor transposon coverage rather than true neutrality. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] Its essential genes were 82% core, directionally consistent with the 80.1% core fraction among DvH essential genes excluded from the models, although the populations and analyses differ. [src: fitness_effects_conservation; field_vs_lab_fitness] The conservation_vs_fitness report likewise cautions that its essential-gene definition is an upper bound because missing fitness data can reflect short genes, low-complexity regions, or scaffold edges rather than essentiality. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]

## Module and functional evidence

Across 52 ICA fitness modules, the mean core fraction was 0.886 and the median was 1.000. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Module conservation was not significantly correlated with field-condition activity (Spearman rho=0.071, p=0.62). [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Using 0.886 as the classification threshold, 21 ecological modules had a mean core fraction of 0.980, 17 conserved-quiet modules had 0.983, 5 field-variable modules had 0.829, and 9 lab modules had 0.516. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

These results did not support the hypothesis that field activity predicts module conservation. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] They did distinguish 21 field-active, conserved ecological modules from 9 lower-conservation lab modules, but this does not establish that the 52 unannotated genes among the 239 ecological-module members mediate environmental adaptation. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Those genes remain candidates for testing, not confirmed ecological determinants.

The core_gene_tradeoffs analysis **refines** the conservation interpretation by showing that the burden–conservation relationship is function-specific rather than uniform: core genes were more burdensome in Protein Metabolism, Motility, and RNA Metabolism, whereas non-core Cell Wall genes were more burdensome. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs] It identified 25,271 condition-dependent trade-off genes, or 17.8% of the genes examined, which were 1.29 times more likely to be core than non-core genes (odds ratio 1.29, p=1.2e-44). [src: core_gene_tradeoffs] This **supports** the view that biological trade-offs coexist with weak predictive power and measurement confounding, but that analysis did not test gene length as a covariate. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs]

The cross-organism results further **support** a broader core-gene trade-off architecture: core genes were more likely than auxiliary genes to show beneficial deletion effects (24.4% versus 19.9%) and were 1.78 times more likely to have strong condition-specific phenotypes. [src: fitness_effects_conservation; conservation_fitness_synthesis] This does not overturn the DvH context comparison; it **refines** it by showing that conservation tracks functional importance more consistently than a field-versus-lab distinction while the measured effect remains modest. [src: fitness_effects_conservation]

## Interpretation and boundaries

The analysis **supports** [[concepts/adaptive-versus-housekeeping-functional-differentiation]] insofar as broadly important genes were more conserved, but it does not establish that field-associated genes are generally more conserved than laboratory-associated genes. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] Field and lab fitness effects were correlated at approximately r ~ 0.7 from the scatter plot, so most genes that were sick in one context were also sick in the other. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The weak performance of field and lab fitness alone indicates that ecological condition class is insufficient to predict pangenome conservation in this dataset. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] The stronger model containing gene length indicates that measurement and genomic-architecture effects must be separated from biological fitness effects before claiming that fitness importance predicts conservation. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] This is a single-organism result from DvH, whose pangenome contains relatively few genomes and has a 76.3% baseline core fraction among analyzed non-essential genes; generalization to organisms with larger accessory genomes has not been established. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The broader analyses have related boundaries: measurements were biased toward rich media and standard stresses, covered 43 bacteria primarily from Proteobacteria, used single-gene knockouts that did not capture epistasis, and may have mistaken poor transposon coverage for neutrality in singleton genes. [src: fitness_effects_conservation] The cross-organism synthesis also showed that pangenome coverage varied among clades; clades with only 2 genomes could have trivially high core fractions, 10 organisms were excluded for <90% DIAMOND coverage, and Dyella79 was excluded because a locus-tag mismatch produced a 0% join rate. [src: conservation_vs_fitness] These limitations **refine** the inference by requiring control for both gene-level callability and uneven pangenome integration. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]

The low conservation of lab-antibiotic and heavy-metal-important genes is consistent with resistance functions being disproportionately accessory and potentially associated with recently acquired mobile genetic elements, but the analysis did not directly measure mobile-element association. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] It therefore suggests the hypothesis that specific metal-resistance mechanisms such as efflux pumps and metal-binding proteins are accessory, whereas uranium and mercury responses may involve more fundamental stress pathways, including DNA repair and sulfate reduction. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] This connects to [[concepts/environmental-resistome]] and [[concepts/metal-cross-resistance]] without establishing a causal mechanism. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

The core_gene_tradeoffs analysis classified 28,017 genes as Costly + Conserved and 5,526 as Costly + Dispensable, but treated these groups as signatures consistent with selection rather than direct measurements of selection in nature. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs] Its use of laboratory conditions as an impoverished proxy for nature, together with fitness-burden and condition biases, **reinforces** the need to distinguish ecological interpretation from length- and callability-adjusted prediction. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs] Likewise, the conservation_fitness_synthesis categories do not remove the need to control measurement and genomic-architecture confounding. [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis]

The field-specific and lab-specific gene sets were small, with n=50–52 per group, limiting statistical power for their comparison. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]

## Tensions

The DvH model shows that adding gene length raised CV-AUC to 0.645, whereas fitness alone had CV-AUC values of 0.517–0.548, indicating strong predictive importance for length in that dataset. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness] The broader analyses report a real but weak fitness–conservation gradient and do not test gene length as a covariate. [src: fitness_effects_conservation; conservation_fitness_synthesis] These are not contradictory estimates of the same model: the datasets, organisms, predictors, and outcomes differ. The unresolved question is how much of the broader gradient survives explicit adjustment for length, insertion callability, phylogeny, and pangenome coverage.

## Relation to existing concepts

- This **supports and qualifies** [[concepts/condition-specific-fitness]]: condition-specific importance is informative, but condition category adds little predictive value beyond fitness importance, and its interpretation requires structural and measurement controls. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- This **extends** [[concepts/pangenome-integration]]: fitness effects and core/auxiliary status can be integrated, while gene length, callability, and uneven pangenome coverage constrain inference. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness; conservation_vs_fitness]
- This **supports** [[concepts/gene-essentiality]]: essential genes were largely core but absent from the DvH transposon fitness comparison, separating constitutive essentiality from measured conditional importance. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- This **refines** [[concepts/environmental-resistome]]: resistance-associated fitness importance can occur in relatively unconserved, accessory portions of the DvH pangenome. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- The broader cross-bacterial analyses **support** these links by showing that fitness magnitude and breadth predict conservation across approximately 194,000 genes, while demonstrating that prediction is weak. [src: fitness_effects_conservation]

## Open Directions

- Use `gene_fitness_conservation.csv` for the 2,725 analyzed genes, together with insertion counts or other transposon-callability measures, in nested cross-validated models to test how much of gene length's CV-AUC 0.645 contribution remains after measurement quality is modeled explicitly. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Add the 678 essential genes through an essentiality-aware missing-fitness or two-stage model, and test whether gene length predicts core status similarly among essential genes and genes with recovered transposon mutants. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Replace binary core/auxiliary labels with quantitative gene-cluster prevalence and fit length-adjusted models to ask whether gene length primarily predicts the core boundary or also intermediate prevalence across the pangenome. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Reanalyze thresholds from -1 to -3 with gene length, insertion coverage, and fitness uncertainty as covariates to test whether length confounding changes condition-specific conservation patterns. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Extend length- and callability-adjusted analysis across the 194,216-gene, 43-bacterium comparison to test whether the weak fitness–conservation gradient persists after measurement quality and lineage are controlled. [src: fitness_effects_conservation]
- Reanalyze the 33-organism conservation_vs_fitness dataset with gene length, insertion coverage, pangenome clade size, and quantitative cluster prevalence to determine whether the median odds ratio of 1.56 persists after callability and pangenome-coverage confounding are controlled. [src: conservation_vs_fitness]
- Test whether the 28,017 costly-and-conserved and 5,526 costly-and-dispensable genes show different length, insertion-callability, mobile-element, or prevalence profiles before interpreting them as signatures of natural selection. [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis]
- Stratify the 25,271 trade-off genes and the Costly + Conserved and Costly + Dispensable groups by gene length, insertion callability, functional category, and pangenome prevalence to test whether core enrichment persists after structural and measurement adjustment. [src: core_gene_tradeoffs]
- Use Fitness Browser experiments and quantitative gene-cluster prevalence across additional environmentally relevant organisms to test whether the weak field-versus-lab effect generalizes beyond DvH. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Combine genomic-context analyses with resistance-gene calls to test whether the 73.4% core fraction for lab-antibiotic genes and 71.2% for heavy-metal genes is associated with mobile genetic elements. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Reanalyze the 52 ICA modules using functional annotation and environmental metadata to test whether the 52 unannotated genes in ecological modules mediate adaptation rather than merely co-varying with conserved genes. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
- Link the 4,346 ENIGMA CORAL field samples with geochemistry and 213,044 ASVs to test ecological associations for DvH or other organisms with both environmental data and gene-level fitness measurements. [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]
