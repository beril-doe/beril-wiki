# Community-level niche coverage and resource depletion for competitive exclusion

Whether no single commensal outcompetes a pathogen on its substrates and communities must jointly cover its niche, with growth-kinetics and lag evidence; stops at specific formulation rankings.

## Literature Context

The one candidate paper bears on the hub's central idea, which is competitive exclusion through resource limitation, but only indirectly. It studied nasal bacteria, not CF airway communities. It reports that coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) were sensitive to growth inhibition by nasal Actinobacteria, while Staphylococcus aureus isolates were resistant [PMID 30578265](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30578265/). Corynebacterium species varied in how strongly they inhibited CoNS. Three Corynebacterium propinquum isolates inhibited strongly, and seven other Corynebacterium isolates inhibited only weakly [PMID 30578265](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30578265/). Comparative genomics found that the C. propinquum genomes were enriched in iron-acquisition genes and carried a siderophore biosynthetic gene cluster that the weakly inhibitory genomes lacked. The siderophore was identified as dehydroxynocardamine. Iron supplementation rescued CoNS from inhibition, which suggests the inhibition came from iron restriction [PMID 30578265](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30578265/). The authors also report that the cluster is expressed in vivo, based on human nasal metatranscriptomes. They frame this as exploitation competition, in which bacteria deplete or block access to scarce nutrients, as opposed to interference competition through antimicrobials [PMID 30578265](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30578265/). This is an abstract-level summary, and the evidence is in vitro coculture plus genomics and metatranscriptomics.

The corpus's working theory in [[concepts/community-level-niche-coverage-and-resource-depletion-for-competitive-exclusion]] is also a resource-competition argument. It holds that commensals could limit P. aeruginosa by consuming its preferred amino acids in CF sputum. This is **consistent in principle** with the nasal study. Both treat exclusion as nutrient limitation and not as direct antagonism, and both start from the premise that nutrients are scarce in the host niche [PMID 30578265](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30578265/).

The two differ in mechanism and in what they test. In the nasal study the contested resource is iron. Three C. propinquum isolates restrict it through a specialized siderophore trait that most other Corynebacterium isolates lack. This is a species-associated trait of particular organisms, so it is not a property of the community. The corpus instead argues that no single commensal outgrows PA14 on any tested substrate. It reports that commensals beat PA14's maximum growth rate in only 13.8% of substrate comparisons. It also reports that a 3–5 organism consortium covers 100% of PA14's metabolic niche, which it calls an emergent community property. The nasal paper therefore does not support or contradict the community-coverage claim. It shows that single-organism exploitation competition can work when a specialized trait exists, whereas the corpus finds no such individual advantage for carbon substrates.

The corpus's claims about carbon-source depletion, the 1.5× collective depletion argument and the lag advantage (commensals starting before PA14 in 43.1% of comparisons) have no counterpart in the candidate paper. They remain untested against this literature. The nasal paper suggests the hypothesis that iron competition could add to carbon-niche coverage in a CF formulation. That is an extrapolation across body sites and has not been tested.

## The working theory

The report's working theory is that commensal communities can exclude P. aeruginosa by consuming its carbon sources. These are primarily the amino acids abundant in cystic fibrosis (CF) sputum: proline, histidine, ornithine, glutamate, aspartate and arginine. [src: cf_formulation_design] See [[entities/cystic-fibrosis-sputum]] and [[entities/pseudomonas-aeruginosa-pa14]]. The report hypothesizes that if commensals collectively deplete these substrates faster than the pathogen can use them, its growth becomes resource-limited. [src: cf_formulation_design]

## Why individual superiority fails

PA14 outgrows the average commensal on every tested substrate in the prebiotic analysis. [src: cf_formulation_design] A related digest says it does so on all 20 tested amino acids plus glucose and lactate, with a selectivity ratio <1.0 for all. [src: discoveries] Commensals beat PA14's maximum growth rate in only 13.8% of substrate comparisons, so PA14 is generally the fastest grower. [src: cf_formulation_design] The growth curves (NB02) show PA14 outgrowing most commensals on its preferred amino acids. [src: cf_formulation_design] The report reads this as showing that catabolic enzyme efficiency, not ribosomal translation rate, drives PA14's advantage. [src: cf_formulation_design] It attributes the advantage to specialized amino acid catabolic enzymes. [src: cf_formulation_design] That attribution is the report's interpretation. No individual commensal outgrows PA14 on any tested substrate. [src: cf_formulation_design]

## Community-level coverage

A 3–5 organism consortium collectively covers 100% of PA14's metabolic niche. The report calls this an emergent community property not predictable from individual organism profiles. [src: cf_formulation_design] The discoveries digest records the same conclusion: exclusion requires community-level coverage by 3+ organisms, not individual superiority. [src: discoveries] The report concludes that exclusion must work through community-level resource depletion rather than individual substrate advantage. [src: cf_formulation_design]

Micrococcus luteus is the keystone in the report's formulation design. Adding it at k=3 achieves 100% PA substrate coverage. [src: cf_formulation_design] It grows on 9 of PA14's 11 preferred substrates, including proline, histidine, glutamate, aspartate, arginine and glucose. This drives the jump from 18% to 100% coverage, because it reaches amino acid substrates that N. mucosa and S. salivarius do not cover individually. [src: cf_formulation_design] It has the widest carbon utilization profile among strict-safe candidates and closes every remaining PA substrate gap in one addition. [src: cf_formulation_design] The report argues that 100% coverage neutralizes PA14's fast growth on any single substrate, because all substrates are contested at once. [src: cf_formulation_design]

The report also argues that community biomass matters more than individual growth rate. A 3-organism consortium, each member growing at half PA14's rate, would still deplete resources 1.5× faster collectively. [src: cf_formulation_design] The report presents the niche coverage model as operationalizing ecological competition for therapeutic design against P. aeruginosa in CF, and as a demonstration of community-level exclusion where no individual dominates. [src: cf_formulation_design]

## Lag as a counterweight

Commensals start growing before PA14 in 43.1% of substrate comparisons, defined as lag advantage > 0. [src: cf_formulation_design] The report suggests that delivering commensals before pathogen exposure, or boosting them first with prebiotics, could exploit this window. [src: cf_formulation_design] It adds that pre-establishing commensals, for example by prebiotic biomass pre-loading, could matter more than raw growth rate. [src: cf_formulation_design] See [[concepts/selective-prebiotics-for-commensal-consortia]].

## Limits of the measurements

Endpoint optical density (OD) shows whether an organism can grow on a substrate, not how fast. [src: cf_formulation_design] Kinetic parameters correlate only moderately with endpoint OD (r ≈ 0.40) and are not redundant. [src: cf_formulation_design] For the 29 isolates with all three assay types, adding kinetic features to the metabolic overlap model improves prediction to R² = 0.311. [src: cf_formulation_design] Related limits are in [[concepts/generalizability-limits-of-planktonic-single-strain-inhibition-assays]] and [[concepts/pathogen-metabolic-adaptation-in-the-lung-and-stability-of-competition-targets]]. For formulation design see [[concepts/multi-criterion-design-of-protective-microbiome-formulations]].

## Open Directions

- Measure resource depletion directly in co-culture: grow PA14 with the k=3 consortium on single amino acids and test whether the pathogen becomes resource-limited as the depletion argument predicts.
- Test the lag window experimentally by adding commensals before PA14, with and without prebiotic pre-loading, and compare PA14 yield with simultaneous inoculation.
- Check the 1.5× collective depletion argument by measuring consumption rates of consortium members rather than inferring them from growth rates.
- Repeat the coverage and kinetics analysis in CF sputum-like medium and with more PA strains. This would show whether the 100% coverage depends on PA14 and on defined substrates.
