# Adam Deutschbauer

ORCID: [0000-0003-2728-7622](https://orcid.org/0000-0003-2728-7622)

## Contributions

The [[summaries/metal_fitness_atlas__REPORT]] project assembled a cross-species atlas of bacterial fitness under metal stress from 559 metal-related experiments across 31 organisms and 16 metals, comprising 383,349 gene × metal fitness records drawn from 24 organisms with fitness matrices [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. It found that the 559 metal experiments represented 8.2% of 6,804 total Fitness Browser experiments, that six metals had cross-species coverage in at least three organisms — cobalt (27 organisms), nickel (26), copper (23), aluminum (22), zinc (17), and iron (3) — and that DvH was the most metal-profiled organism with 149 experiments across 13 metals [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. Using a broad metal-important definition of fit < -1 or n_sick ≥ 1, the project counted 12,838 broad metal-important genes (3.3%), of which 5,667 were strict metal-important genes (1.5%), and reported that iron produced 12.3% important genes and molybdenum/tungsten 11.2%, against 2.7-4.4% for toxic metals [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. It further found that DvH had 1,366 metal-important genes, 49.8% of its genome, across 13 metals, while *Synechococcus elongatus* had 33.6% of genes important across two metals [src: metal_fitness_atlas].

Across 22 organisms and 14 metals, the project found that genes with significant metal-associated fitness defects were 87.4% core versus 76.9% core for baseline genes (OR=2.08, p=4.3e-162), reversing its own initial hypothesis that toxic-metal genes would be accessory-enriched and contrasting with a prior DvH condition-specific result of 71.2% core for heavy-metal genes, a discrepancy it attributed to the genome-wide definition used here capturing core cellular processes such as cell envelope function, DNA repair, central metabolism, protein quality control, and general stress response [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. It reported that 21 of 22 organisms had metal-important genes more core than baseline, 14 significant at p<0.05, with *P. fluorescens* FW300-N2E3 showing a negligible negative delta of -0.003 (p=0.70) [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. The project found essential-metal tolerance genes for Fe, Mo, W, Se, and Mn had a mean core-fraction delta of +0.148 versus +0.081 for toxic metals (one-sided Mann-Whitney U=39, p=0.015), with the strongest per-metal enrichments in manganese (30 important genes, core fraction 1.000, delta +0.198, OR=inf, p=2.0e-03), zinc (1,517, 0.890, +0.151, OR=2.94, p=2.0e-49), molybdenum (302, 0.950, +0.148, OR=5.32, p=1.3e-14), tungsten (303, 0.947, +0.145, OR=4.98, p=5.3e-14), and iron (651, 0.919, +0.116, OR=3.07, p=8.5e-18) [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. It recorded two null per-metal results — cadmium at delta=-0.010 (92 genes, core fraction 0.522, OR=0.96, p=9.2e-01) and uranium at delta=+0.035 (178 genes, 0.685, OR=1.18, p=3.4e-01) — with limited organism coverage given as a likely explanation, and found the overall enrichment robust to excluding four duplicate *P. fluorescens* FW300 strains (OR=2.065, p=5.9e-141 over 18 organisms, versus OR=2.083, p=4.3e-162 over 22), while stating that phylogenetic non-independence nonetheless remains a limitation because multiple *Pseudomonas fluorescens* strains can inflate apparent cross-species conservation [src: metal_fitness_atlas].

On conserved gene families and regulatory modules, the project found that of 2,891 ortholog groups with metal phenotypes, 1,182 were conserved across at least two organisms and 601 across at least three, with the most broadly conserved family, OG00128, spanning 17 organisms and nine metals [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. It identified 149 novel metal-biology candidates with conserved phenotypes across at least two organisms but without full functional annotation — 89 truly unknown, 43 carrying DUF/UPF domains, and 17 with partial hints such as transporter or hydrolase annotations lacking a characterized metal function — and stated these are function predictions resting solely on cross-species fitness data [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. Using z-scored module activity profiles standardized per organism across all experiments, the project found 600 metal-responsive module records at |z| > 2.0 among 19,453 module × metal-experiment records (3.1%), with 47 responsive modules across 12 metals in DvH and 11 across six metals in psRCH2, and the 183 responsive modules with conservation data had a mean core fraction of 0.826 and median 0.929 [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. It reported a null result from an earlier pass: raw activity scores from module_conditions.csv, whose maximum was 0.96, yielded zero responsive modules because they were on a different scale from z-scores, which per-experiment z-normalization resolved [src: metal_fitness_atlas].

The project derived a metal functional signature of 1,286 KEGG KO terms from conserved metal gene families and scored 27,702 pangenome species with it, finding after genome-size normalization (metal clusters divided by total KEGG-annotated clusters) that *Leptospirillum* ranked at the 91st percentile, *Acidithiobacillus* at the 77th, *Marinobacter* at the 75th, and *Sulfobacillus* at the 71st [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. It found bioleaching genera were not significantly enriched over background after normalization (Mann-Whitney p=0.17), that without normalization large open-pangenome species including *K. pneumoniae* and *P. aeruginosa* dominated on genome size alone, and that the simple gene-presence repertoire score failed to predict metal fitness, which it read as evidence that gene essentiality is context-dependent and that regulatory or expression-based models are needed [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. From these results the project proposed a two-tier model in which Tier 1 core general-stress functions make up the majority of metal fitness genes and stay conserved for roles beyond metal tolerance, while Tier 2 accessory specialized resistance functions — efflux, sequestration, enzymatic detoxification — become visible once general-stress genes are excluded, refining rather than rejecting the idea that metal resistance can be an accessory-genome trait, and feeding [[concepts/metal-cross-resistance]] and [[concepts/pangenome-integration]] [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. The report stated its own limits: metal coverage was uneven, with uranium, chromium, mercury, cadmium, selenium, and manganese tested in only one or two organisms so that cross-species patterns for rare metals largely reflect DvH and psRCH2 biology; concentrations were not dose- or tolerance-normalized, with nickel spanning 0.01-2.0 mM across organisms; conservation analysis covered 22 of 31 metal-tested organisms (71%), the nine excluded — Putida, Keio, SynE, Miya, Kang, BFirm, Cola, Ponti, and Dino — lacking Fitness Browser pangenome links; and putatively essential genes, approximately 14.3% of protein-coding genes and approximately 82% core, lack transposon insertions and are absent from the data, making the observed core enrichment a conservative estimate [src: metal_fitness_atlas].
