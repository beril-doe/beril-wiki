# Heather MacGregor

## Contributions

The [[summaries/metal_resistance_global_biogeography__REPORT]] project mapped environmental bacterial metal resistance by extracting 260,652 environmental metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from MGnify, of which 30,497 remained after filtering host-associated biomes, and found that 22,356 MAGs had usable geospatial coordinates (73.3% coordinate coverage) [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]. It found that among those 22,356 coordinate-bearing MAGs, 2.8% carried at least one metal-resistance type, making metal resistance rare in the global environmental MAG pool [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]. A 5° grid analysis over 289 cells with at least 5 MAGs, using Fisher's exact tests with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH FDR) correction, identified 11 significant hotspots with OR>2 and q<0.05 and 3 coldspots with OR<0.5 and q<0.05, the top hotspot being the Atacama/Andean region of Chile at lat=-25°, lon=-70° with 21.8% prevalence, OR=9.83, q=7.6e-12 and n=101 MAGs [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]. Biome-stratified Fisher's tests found soil enriched at 5.8% prevalence, OR=5.05, q=1.8e-82, rhizosphere at 1.9% prevalence, OR=0.66, q=0.30 (NS), and marine at 1.2% prevalence, OR=0.20, q=9.2e-80 [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]. The report is preliminary: ENA batch API retrieval returned 24,511 sample records of which 16,964 (69.2%) had valid latitude/longitude pairs, and it showed that this 30.8% gap is a per-sample gap rather than a geographic one, since 0 of 532 MAG grid cells lacked ENA coordinate coverage; it also cautioned that the Atacama/Andean and USA hotspots may reflect single-study or expedition-level artefacts and that sampling-effort correction by log(n_MAGs) remains necessary, while production figures remain pending and the NB01 map cell carries a known `NameError` from a missing matplotlib import [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography].

The [[summaries/t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt__REPORT]] project found that 6,652 of 30,497 high-quality environmental MAGs (21.8%) carry type IV secretion system (T4SS) or conjugative machinery under a multi-marker definition comprising VirB4/6/8/9/10/11, VirD4, TraI/D, TrwB and TraG, and that 92 carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZy) families show elevated co-occurrence with T4SS loci at ≤10 kb, with GT2 glycosyltransferases the top hit in 767 genomes at an average length of 5,041 bp [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]. It detected 77 horizontal gene transfer (HGT) events in the GT2 gene tree, including 32 normalized high-confidence cross-phylum events, with 65 events spanning 2 phyla and 12 spanning ≥3 phyla (15.6%); the strongest event, Node_4915, contains 35 genes, is 82.9% syntenic and spans 8 phyla at Max_Divergence = 4.843, and divergence and synteny were negatively correlated (Spearman ρ = −0.615, p<0.001) [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]. The project reported that CAZy genes were not detected on plasmids by ICEfinder, that 12 integrative mobilizable elements occurred among the top 100 accumulators, and that T4SS-positive genomes have 10× higher mobile genetic element density than other genomes (p<0.001), consistent with chromosomal or integrative transfer rather than plasmid mobilization, while stating that all associations are observational and require experimental validation [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]. It also found that GT2-neighborhood MAGs (n=376) had a mean of 0.045 metal-resistance types versus 0.004 for non-GT2 MAGs (n=260,276; Mann–Whitney p=8.6e-27), an 11× difference, and that a 70/30 discovery/validation split reproduced the enrichment patterns across both sets, with NB05 threshold validation and NB06 figures pending [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]. These results feed [[concepts/pangenome-integration]] and [[concepts/environmental-resistome]] [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt].

The [[summaries/soil_metal_functional_genomics__REPORT]] project analyzed 51,748 soil samples and nine metals — copper, cobalt, chromium, nickel, zinc, lead, arsenic, cadmium and mercury — and identified 2,355 significant associations between metals and Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) functional categories at false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, with chromium and lead producing the strongest signals and transporters (including ABC and RND systems) and biosynthesis genes dominating the top hits [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]. Its distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA) model yielded R² = 0.799 and p = 0.005 over 999 permutations, with metal concentrations explaining 80% of variance in community COG profiles after conditioning on batch and project effects; the report cautioned that this R² is conditional because project accession was removed before fitting, that the unconditional R² was not reported and may be substantially lower, and that the 2,355 discoveries among 3,915 implied tests (nine metals × 435 COGs) represent a 60% discovery rate under which Benjamini–Hochberg correction may be anti-conservative given non-independent, co-contaminated metals [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]. A copper-specific analysis of 116 COGs at FDR < 0.05, using 7,566 samples with KBase genomes within 10 km, found the strongest positive associations in cell division and nucleotide transport categories (BQ, FQ, FK) and the strongest negative associations in energy-production categories (DI, CE), which the report reads as suggesting energetic trade-offs while stressing the associations are observational and require proximity sensitivity analyses at 5 km and 20 km [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]. Its biome-stratified phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS, a comparative regression accounting for phylogenetic relationships) found distinct metal–COG relationships in soil, marine and wastewater environments, supporting environment-specific responses rather than a universal resistance programme, and the report noted that NB05 items 1–4 cannot be completed without Spark access to the `kescience_mgnify` and `kbase_ke_pangenome` tables [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics].

The [[summaries/soil_frontier_genomics__REPORT]] project tested the global-scale clay shield hypothesis on 5,441 soil samples and reported a null result: all predictive model families had negative out-of-sample R² (Soil & Climate, R² = −0.205 ± 0.197; Geochemical, R² = −0.331 ± 0.071; Industrial, R² = −0.221 ± 0.042), and the shield-efficiency test gave low-clay cross-validation R² = −0.268 versus high-clay R² = −0.292, a difference of 0.024 with 95% CI −0.423 to 0.161 that includes zero [src: soil_frontier_genomics]. The report noted clay was a consistent feature with importance approximately 0.14 but did not improve predictive accuracy in high-clay soils, and stated that the negative out-of-sample R² diagnosis is incomplete because the analyses do not distinguish distributional shift from spatial autocorrelation, high-leverage outliers, and genuine unpredictability [src: soil_frontier_genomics]. The same project introduced a Genomic Discovery Index (GDI), defined as OTU (operational taxonomic unit, a sequence-similarity cluster standing in for a taxon) Richness / (Mean Genome Completeness + 1) at 1° bins, and found forest GDI = 902.36 and cropland GDI = 890.82 against grassland 503.42 and wetland 525.13, describing forest and cropland as jointly highest because the 1.3% difference is not distinguishable without bootstrap confidence intervals; frontier areas with GDI > 1000 had mean pH = 6.74 versus 5.94 in mapped areas, a +0.8 pH unit gap the report flags as requiring a reverse-causality check [src: soil_frontier_genomics]. These results feed [[concepts/genomic-under-representation]] and [[concepts/environment-embedding-geography]] [src: soil_frontier_genomics].

The [[summaries/microbeatlas_metal_ecology__REPORT]] project linked genus-level metal-resistance type diversity from AMRFinderPlus antimicrobial resistance (AMR) pangenome annotations across 6,789 GTDB species to niche breadth derived from a 464,000-sample 16S amplicon atlas, and found across 1,264 bacterial genera with ≥3 OTUs that Levins' B_std niche breadth was phylogenetically conserved (Pagel's λ = 0.787, p = 7.9×10⁻¹⁰²), with habitat range more conserved at λ = 0.909, p = 1.4×10⁻¹⁵⁷ [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]. In its primary PGLS analysis of 606 genera, metal type diversity was the only metal AMR predictor surviving Bonferroni correction across 6 simple models (β = +0.021, SE = 0.0056, p = 1.5×10⁻⁴, ΔAIC = −12.4; threshold p < 0.0083), while the corresponding simple models for total AMR cluster count (β = +0.010, SE = 0.0056, p = 0.090, ΔAIC = −0.9) and core AMR fraction (β = +0.003, SE = 0.0054, p = 0.523, ΔAIC = +1.6) were not significant; the multi-predictor model retained a positive metal type effect (β = +0.023, p = 5.5×10⁻⁴), with total AMR cluster count (β = −0.002, SE = 0.0065, p = 0.789) and core AMR fraction (β = +0.006, SE = 0.0054, p = 0.288) not significant [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]. The effect persisted controlling for genome size (β = +0.0218, SE = 0.0061, p = 3.6×10⁻⁴; ΔAIC = 10.8 in favor of including metal type diversity) and in a three-covariate model of 527 genera (β = +0.0218, t = 3.60, p = 3.5×10⁻⁴, BH-FDR q = 0.003) [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]. The project recorded several negative and weakening results: a strict 5% within-environment prevalence analysis cut the PGLS sample from 606 to 379 genera and left the coefficient positive but non-significant (β = +0.0166, SE = 0.0099, p = 0.092); all 7 leave-one-metal-out exclusions retained a positive coefficient but none remained significant (weakest, excluding As, β = +0.0039, p = 0.518); and the archaeal analysis of 48 genera was underpowered at 11% formal power (β = +0.0145, p = 0.467, q = 0.55), which the report declines to read as evidence against the association [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]. Independent validation on 1,624 groundwater samples and 767 genera found metal type diversity correlated with groundwater prevalence (Spearman ρ = +0.112, p = 0.0019; top-Q4 median 0.81% versus bottom-Q1 0.62%, ratio 1.31×) but produced a null groundwater-specific fold-enrichment result (ρ = +0.042, p = 0.242), and a 133-sample × 24,295-OTU table showed community-weighted mean metal-type diversity differing across 8 wells (Kruskal–Wallis H = 29.10, p = 0.0001) while Sulfurimonas relative abundance decreased with time (ρ = −0.323, p = 0.0002), which the report reads as a counterexample in which dominance tracks electron-acceptor availability rather than metal resistance [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]. The report states the evidence is correlational and concludes that genera with diverse metal-resistance repertoires are on average broader ecological generalists, not that metal-resistance diversity causes ecological generalism [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology].
