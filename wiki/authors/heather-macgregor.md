# Heather MacGregor

## Contributions

[[summaries/metal_resistance_global_biogeography__REPORT]] extracted 260,652 environmental metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs — genomes reconstructed from metagenome assemblies) from MGnify and retained 30,497 after filtering host-associated biomes, of which 22,356 (73.3%) carried usable geospatial coordinates [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]. The same project retrieved 24,511 sample records through the ENA batch API and found 16,964 (69.2%) with valid latitude/longitude pairs, a 30.8% gap that it showed was per-sample rather than geographic because 0 of 532 MAG grid cells lacked ENA coordinate coverage [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]. Among the 22,356 MAGs with coordinates, 2.8% carried at least one metal-resistance type, and a 5° grid analysis of 289 cells with at least 5 MAGs used Fisher's exact tests with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction to identify 11 hotspots (OR>2, q<0.05) and 3 coldspots (OR<0.5, q<0.05), the top hotspot being the Atacama/Andean region of Chile at lat=-25°, lon=-70° with 21.8% prevalence, OR=9.83, q=7.6e-12 and n=101 MAGs [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]. Biome-stratified tests in that project found soil enriched at 5.8% prevalence (OR=5.05, q=1.8e-82), marine depleted at 1.2% (OR=0.20, q=9.2e-80), and rhizosphere non-significant at 1.9% (OR=0.66, q=0.30), evidence it slots into [[concepts/environmental-resistome]] [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]. The report is preliminary and states that the Atacama and USA hotspots may be single-study or expedition-level artefacts pending a check of distinct `sample_accession` prefixes, that sampling-effort correction by log(n_MAGs) has not been done, and that the drafted global map cell raises a `NameError` because `import matplotlib.pyplot` is missing [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography].

[[summaries/t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt__REPORT]] found that 6,652 of 30,497 high-quality environmental MAGs (21.8%) carry type IV secretion system (T4SS) or conjugative machinery under a multi-marker definition comprising VirB4/6/8/9/10/11, VirD4, TraI/D, TrwB and TraG [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]; separately, the biogeography project contrasted its own 2.8% metal-resistance prevalence with this 21.8% T4SS prevalence and read the two as distinct, not uniformly co-distributed, features [src: metal_resistance_global_biogeography]. The t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt project reported 92 carbohydrate-active enzyme (CAZy) families with elevated co-occurrence with T4SS loci at ≤10 kb — with threshold validation still pending — led by GT2 glycosyltransferases in 767 genomes at an average length of 5,041 bp, and found T4SS–CAZy associations enriched in marine sediment (OR=5.5, q<10⁻⁹⁸), barley rhizosphere (OR=10.4) and maize rhizosphere (OR=4.1) [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]. It detected 77 horizontal gene transfer (HGT) events in the GT2 gene tree, including 32 normalized high-confidence cross-phylum events, with 65 events spanning 2 phyla and 12 spanning ≥3 phyla (15.6%), and a strongest event, Node_4915, containing 35 genes at 82.9% synteny across 8 phyla at Max_Divergence = 4.843; divergence and synteny were negatively correlated (Spearman ρ = −0.615, p<0.001) [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]. ICEfinder detected no CAZy genes on plasmids, 12 integrative mobilizable elements occurred among the top 100 accumulators, and T4SS-positive genomes had 10× higher mobile genetic element density (p<0.001), which the report reads as consistent with chromosomal or integrative transfer rather than plasmid mobilization [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt]. The project also found that GT2-neighborhood MAGs (n=376) averaged 0.045 metal-resistance types versus 0.004 for non-GT2 MAGs (n=260,276; Mann–Whitney p=8.6e-27), an 11× difference feeding [[concepts/metal-cross-resistance]], while stating that all associations are observational and that a synteny-threshold permutation test, BLAST validation of Node_4915 against NCBI nr, a housekeeping-gene null baseline and biome-enrichment factorization remain pending [src: t4ss_cazy_environmental_hgt].

[[summaries/soil_metal_functional_genomics__REPORT]] tested associations between soil metal concentrations and microbial functional gene content across 51,748 soil samples and nine metals (Cu, Co, Cr, Ni, Zn, Pb, As, Cd, Hg), identifying 2,355 significant COG–metal associations at FDR < 0.05, where COG denotes a Cluster of Orthologous Groups functional category; chromium and lead produced the strongest signals, and transporters including ABC and RND systems plus biosynthesis genes dominated the top hits [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]. A copper-specific analysis of 116 COGs at FDR < 0.05, using 7,566 samples with KBase genomes within 10 km, found the strongest positive associations in cell division and nucleotide transport categories (BQ, FQ, FK) and the strongest negative associations in energy-production categories (DI, CE), which the report reads as suggesting energetic trade-offs while noting the associations are observational [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]. Distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA, an ordination that partitions community variation among predictors) yielded R² = 0.799 and p = 0.005 over 999 permutations after conditioning on batch and project effects, and the report cautions that this value is conditional because project accession was removed before fitting, so the unconditional R² for metals alone — not reported — may be substantially lower [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]. Biome-stratified phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS, regression that accounts for shared evolutionary history) found distinct metal–COG relationships in soil, marine and wastewater environments [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics]. The report flags that 2,355 discoveries among 3,915 implied tests is a 60% discovery rate under non-independent tests, so Benjamini–Hochberg correction may be anti-conservative and the true FDR higher; that co-contamination of chromium, copper, lead and zinc leaves it unresolved whether associations are metal-specific; that effect sizes were not systematically reported and associations with rho < 0.05 remain to be flagged; and that NB05 validations cannot be completed without Spark access to the `kescience_mgnify` and `kbase_ke_pangenome` tables [src: soil_metal_functional_genomics].

[[summaries/soil_frontier_genomics__REPORT]] evaluated the global-scale clay shield hypothesis on 5,441 soil samples and returned a null result: all predictive model families had negative out-of-sample R² (Soil & Climate −0.205 ± 0.197; Geochemical −0.331 ± 0.071; Industrial −0.221 ± 0.042), and the shield-efficiency test gave low-clay cross-validation R² = −0.268 versus high-clay −0.292, a difference of 0.024 with 95% CI −0.423 to 0.161 that includes zero, with clay a consistent feature at importance approximately 0.14 but no gain in predictability for high-clay soils [src: soil_frontier_genomics]. The report states that this negative-R² diagnosis is incomplete because the analyses do not distinguish among train/test distributional shift caused by spatial autocorrelation within GroupKFold folds, high-leverage outlier samples dominating test-fold mean squared error, and genuine unpredictability of functional gene counts from the measured predictors at global scale, of which only the third would support a strong biological null interpretation while the first two would indicate modelling failure [src: soil_frontier_genomics]. The same project introduced a Genomic Discovery Index (GDI = OTU Richness / (Mean Genome Completeness + 1), where an OTU is an operational taxonomic unit, a sequence-similarity cluster used as a taxon proxy) at 1° bins and reported forest GDI = 902.36 and cropland GDI = 890.82 against grassland 503.42 and wetland 525.13, plus a mean pH of 6.74 in frontier areas with GDI > 1000 versus 5.94 in mapped areas, a +0.8 pH unit gap it interprets as systematic under-sampling of alkaline soil microbiomes and slots into [[concepts/genomic-under-representation]] [src: soil_frontier_genomics]. It notes the GDI is a novel index without published precedent that can reach 902 with zero genomes because completeness = 0 leaves a denominator of 1, that the 1.3% forest–cropland difference is not meaningfully distinguishable without bootstrap confidence intervals so the two should be described as jointly highest, that the pH gap needs a reverse-causality check controlling for the number of 16S samples per pH bin, and that these NB05 validations require re-running from the BERIL Observatory 16S tables and `kbase_ke_pangenome` completeness data [src: soil_frontier_genomics].

[[summaries/microbeatlas_metal_ecology__REPORT]] linked genus-level metal-resistance type diversity, inferred from AMRFinderPlus pangenome annotations across 6,789 GTDB species, to ecological niche breadth from a 464,000-sample 16S amplicon atlas, finding strong phylogenetic conservation of Levins' B_std across 1,264 genera (Pagel's λ = 0.787, p = 7.9×10⁻¹⁰²) and of habitat range (λ = 0.909, p = 1.4×10⁻¹⁵⁷) [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]. In its primary PGLS analysis of 606 genera, metal type diversity was the only metal AMR predictor surviving Bonferroni correction across 6 simple models (β = +0.021, SE = 0.0056, p = 1.5×10⁻⁴, threshold p < 0.0083), while total AMR cluster count (β = −0.002, SE = 0.0065, p = 0.789) and core AMR fraction (β = +0.006, SE = 0.0054, p = 0.288) were not significant [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]. The project reported intermediate phylogenetic signal for metal AMR traits (cluster count λ = 0.260, core fraction λ = 0.441, metal type diversity λ = 0.335) against a nitrification positive control at λ = 0.939 in bacteria and λ = 1.000 in archaea [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]. Sensitivity analyses in that project kept the effect after adding pangenome coverage (β = +0.0204, p = 3.4×10⁻⁴) and genome size (β = +0.0218, p = 3.6×10⁻⁴, ΔAIC = 10.8 in favour of including metal types), but the strict 5% within-environment prevalence analysis cut the PGLS sample from 606 to 379 genera and left the coefficient positive yet non-significant (β = +0.0166, SE = 0.0099, p = 0.092), all 7 leave-one-metal-out exclusions retained a positive but non-significant coefficient, and the 48-genus archaeal analysis was underpowered at 11% power (β = +0.0145, p = 0.467), which the report declines to treat as evidence against the association [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]. Independent validation found metal type diversity correlated with groundwater prevalence across 1,624 samples and 767 genera (Spearman ρ = +0.112, p = 0.0019; top-Q4 median 0.81% versus bottom-Q1 0.62%, ratio 1.31×) while the groundwater-specific fold-enrichment test was null (ρ = +0.042, p = 0.242), and a 133-sample well series showed community-weighted mean metal-type diversity differing across 8 wells (Kruskal–Wallis H = 29.10, p = 0.0001) and rising with time point (ρ = +0.383, p < 0.0001), alongside a counterexample in which Sulfurimonas relative abundance decreased with time (ρ = −0.323, p = 0.0002) [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology]. The report states its primary evidence is correlational and cannot distinguish whether metal tolerance enables habitat expansion, broad ecological range increases acquisition opportunities, or a shared factor such as genome size drives both [src: microbeatlas_metal_ecology].
