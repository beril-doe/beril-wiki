# Tungsten

## What it is

**Canonical name:** Tungsten. [^metal_fitness_atlas]

**Known alias:** W. [^metal_fitness_atlas]

**Stable external identifier:** No stable external identifier was specified in the source report. [^metal_fitness_atlas]

## Key facts from the metal fitness atlas

Tungsten was analyzed as an essential metal in the cross-species [Pan-Bacterial Metal Fitness Atlas](../summaries/metal_fitness_atlas__REPORT.md), which integrated 559 experiments across 31 organisms and 16 metals. [^metal_fitness_atlas]

Tungsten-associated fitness defects showed strong core-genome enrichment: 303 important genes had a core fraction of 0.947, a core-fraction delta of +0.145, an odds ratio of 4.98, and p=5.3e-14. [^metal_fitness_atlas]

The tungsten core-fraction delta was among the strongest observed in the atlas and was exceeded by manganese (+0.198), zinc (+0.151), and molybdenum (+0.148). [^metal_fitness_atlas]

Across essential metals—iron, molybdenum, tungsten, selenium, and manganese—the mean core-fraction delta was +0.148, nearly double the toxic-metal delta of +0.081; this difference was significant by one-sided Mann-Whitney U testing (U=39, p=0.015). [^metal_fitness_atlas]

The report found that metal-important genes overall were 87.4% core, compared with 76.9% core for baseline genes (OR=2.08, p=4.3e-162), supporting a model in which tungsten-associated fitness effects can reflect conserved cellular functions rather than only accessory resistance mechanisms. [^metal_fitness_atlas]

A later specificity analysis **refines** this atlas-level view: 173 of 306 tungsten-important gene records (56.5%) were classified as metal-specific, meaning they had significant fitness defects under metal stress but a sick rate below 5% across 5,945 non-metal experiments. [^metal_specificity]

The specificity analysis also **supports** the broader distinction between conserved general stress functions and specialized metal determinants: tungsten ranked among the metals with a relatively high metal-specific fraction, while metal-specific genes were still strongly core-associated overall. [^metal_specificity]

These findings support the [metal cross-resistance](../concepts/metal-cross-resistance.md) concept by placing tungsten-associated fitness effects within a broader pattern of conserved, core-genome metal-stress functions alongside specialized accessory resistance functions. [^metal_fitness_atlas]

The atlas cautions that metal concentrations were not normalized by dose or tolerance threshold, so tungsten fitness effects should not be compared directly across organisms without accounting for relative exposure. [^metal_fitness_atlas]
