# Is the negative pairing of nitrogen fixation with cyanide synthesis a lineage effect or a within-genus conflict?

Across genomes, the nitrogen-fixation gene is less often found with the cyanide-synthesis gene and the pyrroloquinoline quinone gene, and nitrogen fixers form ecologically distinct guilds. Does the pairing remain within single genera, and if so, does cyanide production or its regulation conflict with nitrogenase activity?

## Hypothesis

The negative pairing is explained mostly by guild and lineage differences and weakens or disappears within genera. If it persists within genera, nitrogenase is inhibited by cyanide in non-producers, and dual carriers either resist cyanide or repress its synthesis under nitrogen-fixing conditions.

## Why it matters

It would separate a trade-off imposed by biochemistry from a by-product of which lineages occupy which niches. That determines which plant-growth trait pairs can be combined in one inoculant strain.

## How to test it

In the Pangenome Collection, test co-carriage of the nitrogen-fixation and cyanide-synthesis genes within genera that contain carriers of both, against expectation from their separate frequencies, and separately by ecological guild. If the pairing persists, measure acetylene reduction (a nitrogenase activity assay) with and without cyanide in nitrogen-fixing strains, and measure cyanide-synthesis gene expression under nitrogen-fixing versus nitrogen-replete conditions in dual carriers where they exist.

## What would support or refute it

Support for a conflict: the pairing persists within genera, nitrogenase activity falls with cyanide in non-producers but not in dual carriers, or cyanide synthesis is repressed during nitrogen fixation. Refute: co-carriage matches expectation within genera and guilds, and cyanide does not affect nitrogenase, so the pattern is a lineage and niche effect.

## Data

Approach: analysis then experiment. Readiness: needs new measurements. The genomic co-carriage test can run on existing data, but the nitrogenase and expression tests need strain assays; dual carriers may be too few to study.
