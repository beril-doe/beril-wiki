# Laboratory Fitness Versus Natural Selection and Gene Conservation

This topic asks whether a gene's measured effect on growth in the laboratory explains why the gene is kept or lost across a species' genomes in nature. The corpus speaks to it because many projects joined transposon-mutant fitness data (RB-TnSeq, random barcode transposon sequencing) from the Fitness Browser to pangenome classifications of genes as core (in nearly all genomes of a species) or accessory. Across these projects, laboratory fitness predicts conservation only modestly, and the step from lab result to natural selection is nearly always an interpretation.

## Literature Context

The two candidate papers are eukaryotic and do not examine bacterial pangenomes or transposon-mutant fitness. They bear on this topic only as examples of how a gene's mutant phenotype gets linked to evidence of selection in natural populations.

In Neurospora crassa, a predicted mannosyltransferase, GT69-2, was required for somatic cell fusion. Cells lacking it stopped chemotropic signaling early and failed to complete cell wall dissolution and membrane merger [PMID 33727349](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33727349/). Loss-of-function mutations in the linked locus rfw-1 suppressed the self-fusion defect. The double mutants still failed to fuse with wild-type cells [PMID 33727349](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33727349/). Population data added a second line of evidence. Alleles of gt69-2 were highly polymorphic and fell into two haplogroups. rfw-1 was conserved and linked to gt69-2 in haplogroup I, and every haplogroup II isolate lacked rfw-1. The authors interpret this as balancing selection [PMID 33727349](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33727349/). In this case a gene's presence or absence across isolates is tied to a defined mutant phenotype, and the reading is selection that maintains variation. It is not a simple core-versus-accessory pattern.

In Tibetan sheep, an integrated transcriptomic and genomic analysis identified 165 reproduction-associated genes. Three top-ranked selection signals (VEPH1, HBB and MEIKIN) were differentially expressed [PMID 40869957](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40869957/). Knockout orthologs in mice were reported to give phenotypes such as male infertility and abnormal meiosis, and the authors conclude that these genes support reproductive fitness under chronic hypoxia [PMID 40869957](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40869957/). The evidence is an abstract-level pairing of selection signals with mutant phenotypes from a different species. It does not show that those phenotypes drive the selection.

The corpus findings are consistent with these papers at the level of logic and do not test the same claims. The corpus finds that laboratory fitness predicts pangenome conservation only modestly. Core fraction fell by 16 percentage points from essential genes (82% core) to always-neutral genes (66% core), a gradient the report authors call weakly predictive. The corpus also reads costly-and-conserved genes as purifying selection on functions the lab does not test, and it flags that as an inference from laboratory data. Both candidate papers make the same kind of move, from a knockout or mutant phenotype to a selective interpretation, and neither tests it directly. The Neurospora case also suggests that selection can maintain a gene's presence in only part of a population. A bare core-versus-accessory classification could not distinguish that from neutral loss. This is a hypothesis and is untested in the corpus. The corpus's bacterial results (for example, the single-organism Desulfovibrio vulgaris Hildenborough field-versus-lab comparison, p=0.27) extend the literature in a different direction. They use large-scale mutant fitness data across 43 bacteria, which neither candidate paper provides. Direct comparison between the corpus and these papers is therefore not possible. The papers neither support nor contradict the corpus's quantitative results.

## What the Corpus Shows

**Fitness importance predicts conservation, weakly.** Across 194,216 protein-coding genes in 43 bacteria, core fraction fell by 16 percentage points from essential genes (82% core) to always-neutral genes (66% core) [src: discoveries, conservation_fitness_synthesis]. Genes important in 20+ conditions were 79% core, against 66% for genes important in none [src: discoveries]. The report authors call this gradient statistically robust yet only weakly predictive [src: fitness_effects_conservation]. See [[concepts/gene-fitness-importance-as-a-predictor-of-pangenome-conservation]]. Essentiality shows the same modest pattern: across 33 bacteria, putative essential genes (no transposon insertions) were 86.1% core versus 81.2% for non-essential genes, with 18 of 33 organisms significant [src: conservation_vs_fitness, discoveries]. The essential-gene categories differ in annotation: essential-core genes are 41.9% enzymes and 13.0% hypothetical, while essential-auxiliary genes are 13.4% enzymes and 38.2% hypothetical [src: conservation_vs_fitness]. The core percentage also depends on the denominator: 82% using all genes including unmapped ones versus 86% using mapped genes only [src: pitfalls]. See [[concepts/essential-genes-and-core-genome-conservation]].

**Burdensome genes are often conserved.** Core genes are more likely than accessory genes to show positive fitness when deleted (24.4% versus 19.9%), which contradicts the streamlining hypothesis that accessory genes are the costly ones [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis]. The reports count 28,017 costly-and-conserved genes and read them as purifying selection for functions the lab does not test [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis, core_gene_tradeoffs, costly_dispensable_genes]. This is an inference from laboratory data [src: core_gene_tradeoffs]. See [[concepts/costly-but-conserved-genes-laboratory-burden-versus-conservation]]. Core genes are also 1.29x more likely to be trade-off genes (important in some conditions, burdensome in others) and genes with strong condition-specific effects are 1.78x more likely to be core [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis, core_gene_tradeoffs, fitness_effects_conservation]; the proposed explanations (more pathways, better detectability) were not directly tested [src: core_gene_tradeoffs, fitness_effects_conservation]. See [[concepts/condition-dependent-fitness-trade-offs-of-core-versus-accessory-genes]].

**Costly and non-conserved genes look like mobile-element debris.** The 5,526 costly-and-dispensable genes [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis] are 7.45x more likely than costly-and-conserved genes to carry mobile-element keywords [src: costly_dispensable_genes]. The evidence is annotation-based and correlational; genomic-context tests are only proposed [src: costly_dispensable_genes]. See [[concepts/mobile-element-origin-of-costly-non-conserved-genes]].

**Co-regulated units and metal genes sit in the core.** Genes in ICA (independent component analysis) fitness modules are 86% core versus an 81.5% baseline (OR 1.46) [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis, module_conservation], and 577 of 974 modules are more than 90% core [src: module_conservation]. See [[concepts/conservation-of-cofitness-modules-in-the-core-genome]]. Metal-important genes are 87.4% core versus a 76.9% baseline across 22 organisms [src: metal_fitness_atlas], reversing the expectation of accessory enrichment; metal-specific genes remain 88.0% core [src: metal_specificity]. See [[concepts/core-genome-enrichment-of-metal-stress-fitness-genes]]. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) knockouts show a small pooled shift of +0.086 across 25 organisms, with no difference between core and accessory AMR genes [src: amr_fitness_cost]. See [[concepts/fitness-cost-of-antimicrobial-resistance-genes]].

**Field and ecological tests are thin.** In one organism, Desulfovibrio vulgaris Hildenborough, field-stress genes were 83.6% core versus a 76.3% baseline, but lab-nutrient genes were also enriched and field-specific versus lab-specific was not significant (p=0.27) [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]. The report concludes that fitness magnitude predicts conservation better than the condition producing it [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]. See [[concepts/field-relevant-versus-laboratory-fitness-as-a-predictor-of-gene-conservation]]. For dark genes, 29 of 47 testable clusters (61.7%) were concordant with pre-registered lab-environment predictions [src: functional_dark_matter, discoveries]; see [[concepts/concordance-between-laboratory-fitness-phenotypes-and-field-environments-of-gene-carriers]]. A lab metal-tolerance score correlated with field abundance at Oak Ridge only suggestively (rho=0.503, p=0.095, n=12 genera) [src: lab_field_ecology]; see [[concepts/laboratory-fitness-as-a-predictor-of-field-abundance]].

**Co-inheritance and cross-genus signals are weak.** Co-fitness pairs co-occur across genomes only slightly more than prevalence-matched random pairs (aggregate delta +0.003) [src: cofitness_coinheritance]; see [[concepts/co-fitness-as-a-predictor-of-gene-co-inheritance-in-pangenomes]]. Genes predicting growth across genera show only 1.19x enrichment for fitness significance in matched experiments [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]; see [[concepts/concordance-between-cross-genus-predictive-genes-and-within-strain-gene-fitness]].

**Context-specific cases.** In Acinetobacter baylyi ADP1, pangenome status was not enriched in genes where metabolic-model and experiment disagree (OR = 0.89, p = 0.80), a single-strain null [src: adp1_triple_essentiality]; see [[concepts/pangenome-conservation-of-metabolic-model-discordant-genes]]. In lung P. aeruginosa genomes, amino acid catabolism is 97.4% conserved while sugar pathways are lost, read as adaptation but not a direct test of selection [src: cf_formulation_design]; see [[concepts/pathogen-metabolic-adaptation-in-the-lung-and-stability-of-competition-targets]]. Type II NADH dehydrogenase (NDH-2) presence did not predict Complex I dispensability on aromatics after correction (p = 0.52) [src: discoveries, respiratory_chain_wiring]; see [[concepts/ndh-2-compensation-for-complex-i-loss-across-species]].

## Tensions and Caveats

**Streamlining versus core burden.** The expectation that accessory genes are burdens is not supported: core genes are more often beneficial to delete [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis]. Yet costly non-conserved genes do look like mobile debris [src: costly_dispensable_genes]. Both can hold, but the mechanism linking cost to loss is untested.

**Metal genes: DvH versus atlas.** The DvH analysis found 71.2% core for heavy-metal genes. The atlas reports that this reflects the DvH analysis isolating genes important only for metals, whereas the atlas captures all genes with any metal fitness defect [src: metal_fitness_atlas]. A separate report finds metal-specific genes still 88% core [src: metal_specificity].

**Statistical versus biological significance.** The co-fitness aggregate p-value is tiny while the across-organism Wilcoxon test is not significant (p=0.13) [src: cofitness_coinheritance]. Field-versus-lab results rest on 50-52 genes per group and an r of about 0.7 between field and lab effects [src: field_vs_lab_fitness]. The lab-field concordance binomial test is marginal (p = 0.072) and null controls were not run [src: functional_dark_matter].

**Lab as proxy.** Laboratory conditions capture only a fraction of natural conditions, and condition types are biased toward experimental convenience [src: core_gene_tradeoffs]. Condition-specific does not mean niche-specific [src: conservation_fitness_synthesis]. Essentiality calls rest on missing insertions and are putative [src: pitfalls]. The 1.19x concordance report is internally inconsistent about whether its validation is complete [src: genotype_to_phenotype_enigma]. Reports giving n=12 and n=11 genera for the Oak Ridge result do not explain the difference [src: lab_field_ecology, bacdive_metal_validation].
