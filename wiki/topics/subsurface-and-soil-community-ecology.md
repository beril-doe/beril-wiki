# Subsurface and Soil Community Ecology

This topic asks what structures microbial communities in soils and the subsurface: depth and flow, contamination, treatment history, warming, and lineage-level genome content. The corpus speaks to it through 16S amplicon surveys (sequencing of the 16S rRNA marker gene to profile taxa) of Oak Ridge groundwater and sediment [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology, lab_field_ecology], a soil-warming experiment at Harvard Forest [src: harvard_forest_warming], a lignin-enrichment experiment [src: lignin_community_enrichment], and comparative genomics of clay-associated Bacillota_B bacteria [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory, clay_confined_subsurface]. This page separates measured results from hypotheses. It has three sections after this lead, and six member concepts are linked below.

## Literature Context

Published work on Oak Ridge groundwater and other subsurface systems frames community structure as a balance of stochastic assembly (chance events such as drift and dispersal limitation) and deterministic assembly (selection by the environment). At the Oak Ridge Field Research Center, stochastic processes shaped more than 60% of community structure on average, but their importance fell as stress rose, while selection increased with stress, mainly tied to pH, cobalt and molybdenum; assembly also varied strongly among phylogenetic groups [PMID 38212658](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38212658/). An earlier subsurface study found that environmental filtering governs composition, and that its influence relative to stochastic factors was greatest at both ends of an environmental gradient [PMID 22456445](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22456445/). Other settings show depth stratification: in 130-year-old mine tailings, bacterial diversity fell with depth and pH was the primary factor for bacterial and fungal assembly [PMID 41124740](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41124740/). By contrast, Oregon coastal subsurface sediments showed stable functional structure but highly variable taxonomy that local physicochemistry largely failed to explain, which the authors attributed to biological interactions [PMID 39702405](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39702405/). In soil, dispersal and stochasticity also dominated along a Tibetan Plateau elevation gradient, where soil temperature was the key environmental factor [PMID 38583623](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38583623/), and perturbations such as biochar (bacteria shifting from deterministic to stochastic assembly at the highest dose) change assembly processes [PMID 37083970](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37083970/).

The corpus's depth and zone results are consistent with this picture of strong environmental structuring. Hydrogeological zone explained 27.5% of variance in the SSO sediment cores while well identity did not, and this resembles the depth stratification seen in tailings. The uranium split in Oak Ridge groundwater, with distinct communities at contaminated sites, is directionally consistent with stress-driven selection, but it extends the literature only weakly: the corpus reports no test statistic and no control for pH, metals or zone, whereas the published study quantified stress against assembly processes using more than 200 biogeochemical variables. The corpus did not run assembly null models (randomized-community comparisons that estimate how much structure exceeds chance), so it cannot say whether stochastic processes dominate at its sites.

The lignin experiment's finding that Round-1 carbon history outweighed current carbon source (an ecological memory effect) is not addressed by these candidate papers; as a bacterial-only result with n=3 per group, it is best treated as novel and tentative here. Likewise, the Harvard Forest warming results and the clay Bacillota_B genome comparisons have no direct counterpart among the candidates. The Oregon sediment report that taxonomy and function can decouple is a useful frame for reading the corpus's lineage-level genome enrichment alongside the failure of stressors to predict functional gene counts across 5,441 soil samples, though the two analyses ask different questions and this is an analogy, not a test.

Taken together, the literature supports treating physical position and stress as plausible structuring axes, and the corpus adds site-specific quantification for depth, while leaving assembly-process partitioning for the corpus's soil and subsurface datasets as an open analysis.

## What the Corpus Shows

**Physical position is the strongest measured structuring axis.** In 37 sediment core segments from the SSO subsurface site, hydrogeological zone explained 27.5% of community variance (F = 4.05, p = 0.0001), while well identity explained 19.2% and was not significant (F = 0.80, p = 0.979) [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology]. PERMANOVA (permutational multivariate analysis of variance, a test of whether groups differ in community composition) was the method. Ten of 12 dominant phyla had significant depth associations; Firmicutes rose with depth (Spearman ρ = +0.76) and Chloroflexi fell (ρ = −0.73) [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology]. Groundwater and sediment from the same well also differed (median Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, a 0-to-1 measure of compositional difference, 0.424), so habitat matters as well as location [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology]. See [[concepts/subsurface-hydrogeological-zonation]]. Soil shows the same pattern at a different scale: at Harvard Forest, horizon (organic versus mineral soil layer) explained R²=30.6% of genus-level composition (p=0.0002), while warming explained R²=7.6% (p=0.069) [src: harvard_forest_warming]. See [[concepts/soil-horizon-response-heterogeneity]].

**Contamination is a plausible second axis, but the evidence is thin.** Splitting Oak Ridge groundwater sites at the median uranium concentration gave distinct community compositions, with rare-biosphere taxa and subsurface specialists more prominent at contaminated sites [src: lab_field_ecology]. The report explains this with published work (Carlson et al. 2019) on acid-plus-metal inhibition of non-*Rhodanobacter* taxa. That mechanism is cited from the literature, not tested here, so applying it is a hypothesis [src: lab_field_ecology]. The SSO report likewise attributes depth structure and a northeast-to-southwest corridor of similar communities (U3–M6–L7) to a contamination plume. It concedes that all inferences come from community composition alone and await geochemical confirmation [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology]. See [[concepts/contaminant-selection-of-subsurface-communities]].

**Treatment history can outweigh current conditions.** In the lignin-enrichment experiment, Round-1 carbon history explained 58.9% of bacterial community variance in Round 2 (R²=0.589, p=0.002), versus 32.7% for the current carbon source (R²=0.327, p=0.018). Communities with different histories did not converge under identical conditions [src: lignin_community_enrichment]. This is quantitative evidence for ecological memory, the persistence of composition differences after the same current conditions are imposed. It is bacterial only: the fungal ITS (internal transcribed spacer marker) history signal was not detectable (p=0.090) [src: lignin_community_enrichment]. See [[concepts/ecological-memory]].

**Warming is better supported in composition than in function.** In the Harvard Forest organic horizon, Actinobacteria rose (control mean 0.249 to heated 0.315, q=0.049) and Acidobacteria fell (0.035 to 0.024, q=0.049). These were the only phylum shifts below q<0.05, and they passed narrowly [src: harvard_forest_warming]. Gene-content evidence is weaker: carbon-cycling KOs (KEGG Orthology gene groups) were enriched at OR=2.78, p=0.042, confounded with lab incubation. Methanotrophy (pmoA/pmoB, subunits of particulate methane monooxygenase) and glyoxylate-shunt transcript signals did not survive false discovery rate (FDR) correction [src: harvard_forest_warming]. Warming responses of individual KOs correlated only weakly between horizons (DNA Pearson r=0.075) [src: harvard_forest_warming]. See [[concepts/long-term-soil-warming-microbial-response]].

**Deep lineages show genome expansion, not streamlining.** Comparing 10 deep-clay with 62 soil-baseline Bacillota_B genomes, 547 of 14,109 tested orthologous groups were significantly enriched at q<0.05. Keyword classification put 42 in anaerobic respiration; the report estimates manual reclassification would raise that to closer to 80–100 [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory]. In a separate cohort comparison, mean anaerobic-toolkit scores were 1.889 for deep clay (n = 9), 0.033 for shallow clay (n = 30) and 0.393 for soil baseline (n = 140) [src: clay_confined_subsurface]. Sulfate-reduction markers were the signal that held within Bacillota_B (5/5 deep versus 4/19 baseline, Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted p = 0.044), whereas the Wood–Ljungdahl and hydrogenase enrichments did not survive within-phylum control [src: clay_confined_subsurface]. See [[concepts/subsurface-bacillota-specialization]].

## Tensions and Caveats

**Cohort and compartment bias limit the genomic story.** The cultured deep-clay genomes match a porewater paradigm and cannot show whether rock-attached communities differ [src: clay_confined_subsurface, bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory]. At Mont Terri, cited studies describe sulfate reducers dominating porewater and Geobacter/Geothrix iron reducers dominating rock surfaces [src: discoveries]. The anchor genomes were clumped taxonomically, so some enriched groups may be lineage markers rather than subsurface signatures [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory]. The report also gives 3 *Desulfosporosinus* genomes in one place but lists 2 in its anchor composition, and this is unresolved [src: bacillota_b_subsurface_accessory].

**Local specialization does not scale to a global prediction.** Across 5,441 soil samples, measured stressors did not predict functional gene counts out of sample. The low-clay versus high-clay cross-validation difference was 0.024 (95% CI: −0.423, 0.161), which includes zero [src: soil_frontier_genomics]. This does not contradict the deep-clay genome result, since the two analyses ask different questions, but it argues against generalizing a clay effect [src: soil_frontier_genomics].

**Interpretation outruns statistics in several reports.** The SSO discussion says all findings converge on a single plume model and that community similarity "demonstrates" hydrology mapping at meter scale. The same report grants that no direct geochemical data back it, and Procrustes correspondence with the grid was marginal (m² = 0.379, p = 0.080) [src: enigma_sso_asv_ecology]. Harvard Forest language such as "direct molecular signature" of substrate depletion rests on one richness comparison (155 vs 167 detectable ChEBI, Chemical Entities of Biological Interest, compounds, p=0.012), and the transcript signals do not survive FDR [src: harvard_forest_warming].

**Confounds are built into designs.** In the Harvard Forest DNA cohort every organic sample was lab-incubated and every mineral sample was direct, so horizon cannot be separated from incubation [src: harvard_forest_warming]. The significant mineral ChEBI drop and non-significant organic drop (p=0.209) went in the same direction, and no interaction was tested, so this is weak evidence for horizon heterogeneity [src: harvard_forest_warming]. The uranium split is reported without a test statistic or control for pH, metals or hydrogeological zone [src: lab_field_ecology].

**Small samples bound the lignin and fungal results.** With n=3 per group, no individual OTU reached FDR significance [src: lignin_community_enrichment]. Fungal replicates were highly variable (within-group Bray–Curtis 0.65, versus 0.09 for 16S), so a bacteria-versus-fungi assembly difference is interpretation, not demonstration [src: lignin_community_enrichment].
